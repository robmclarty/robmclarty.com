The Leader

The engineering constraint for long-horizon agentic systems is structural efficiency rather than raw model capability. Delegent demonstrates that maintaining a frontier model in leadership while offloading focused investigation to local small language models reduces coding costs by 67 percent without performance loss. This architecture avoids the fragility of full-task switching, enabling durable state management at scale.

Durable state integrity requires rigorous recovery mechanisms and accurate retrieval. AppWorld analysis shows that 24.6 percent of agent restarts cause harm through partial executions, exposing the gap between theoretical idempotency and practical data deletion. BRAG addresses this by formalizing time-decay via Bayesian filtering, raising recall on long-term memory to 0.98.

Multimodal training must correct for structural shortcuts that reinforce incorrect reasoning. A large-scale audit of 33,000 benchmark items reveals that 24.2 percent rely on single-image shortcuts. The proposed CICA objective directs credit assignment toward specific token contributions, ensuring models learn cross-image reasoning rather than exploiting static artifacts.

Delegent Reduces Long-Horizon Coding Cost By 67 Percent

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Agents built on frontier language models are expensive. At every step, the frontier model re-reads its entire context and executes every subtask regardless of its difficulty. This paper explores the simple thesis that much of this cost can be removed by delegating easy subtasks to a small language model, or SLM, that runs on hardware one already owns. To test it, we build Delegent, a simple and effective agent harness built on Claude Code, in which the frontier model stays in charge of every task but hands focused subtasks, such as investigating code, running tests, and searching a corpus, to SLM subagents. Existing approaches such as cascades and model routers usually choose which model executes the entire task, whereas in Delegent, the frontier model always leads the execution and delegates easy subtasks to the SLM. No router or model is trained, so either model can be swapped freely. With the help of a 27B FP8 SLM, Delegent reduces the frontier cost of long-horizon coding by 67% and of agentic search by 59%, with no loss in task performance, and even a 2B SLM that fits on a laptop reduces the coding cost by 66%. The savings grow with the legwork that the frontier model would otherwise do itself, and a short instruction, which leaves the main coding tasks to the frontier model, accounts for most of the saving and keeps the 2B SLM from losing tasks. AppWorld Study Shows 24.6 Percent of Agent Restarts Cause Harm
Why: Ensure your agentic systems survive failures without corrupting state.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 299 words

Analysis of 200 trajectories reveals that partial executions frequently produce duplicate effects or data deletion. Standard duplication detection addresses only a fraction of total harm.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Code-executing LLM agents submit blocks of code that issue several API writes. If a block fails after some of its writes have taken effect, the agent rewrites the block and continues. It is unknown how often such a recovery harms the external state when the failure occurs naturally in the code written by the agent. In this paper, we measure it on recorded trajectories of AppWorld agents. In a stratified random sample of 200 candidate episodes across ten models, 145 are partial executions confirmed by the database state. In the sample, 30 of 143 confirmed partial executions with a determinable outcome are followed by a harmful recovery, and its violation remains at the end of the run, which gives a stratification-weighted estimate of 24.6%. Two thirds of the harmful recoveries duplicate an effect, and the rest delete user data or make a wrong first-time write, e.g., an order of a wrong product. A rule that searches for repeated calls finds 75% of duplications but only 41% of all harm. We also examine two sources of operation identity for an idempotency contract. In a small pilot (17 episodes, one local model), identifiers supplied by the model do not detectably reduce harm, because the model issues a new identifier for the same operation. Identity derived from the call content, evaluated counterfactually on the sample, would have matched every harmful write in 14 to 18 of the 30 harmful recoveries, but in none of those with a wrong first-time write, and it would also have matched writes required by the task in 30 episodes. Based on these results, we outline design requirements for safer agent recovery.

Cross-image credit assignment corrects single-image shortcuts in multimodal training
Why: You need reliable long-horizon agent vision, not models that cheat by ignoring most inputs.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 336 words

ASTERISM audits 33,000 benchmark items and finds that 24.2% rely on single-image shortcuts rather than cross-image reasoning. Standard RLVR training reinforces these shortcuts; the authors introduce CICA to direct signals toward specific token contributions.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Multi-image benchmarks aim to test whether a multimodal large language model (MLLM) combines evidence across images, but many of their items can be answered from a single image. Across about 33k items from 19 public benchmarks, our audit and a two-stage single-image filter find that one image or the question alone settles 24.2% of them. On these items, MLLMs recover from one image a median 78% of the accuracy that all images add over the text alone, so these items reward answering from one image, which we call the single-image shortcut. MLLMs take this shortcut even on items where one image is not enough. On such items, most of their wrong answers repeat an answer they give from one image, at a rate well above chance. We build ASTERISM, to our knowledge the largest audited multi-image benchmark, from these 19 benchmarks by keeping their items that need more than one image. Its 18.7k items, 2.0k of them corrected or rewritten, share one format, one scorer and seven task categories. We also release ASTERISM-Train with 49k training items and ASTERISM-Lite, a hard subset whose items we manually verify one by one. On ASTERISM-Lite, the strongest proprietary MLLM scores 68%. Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), now common in MLLM post-training, rewards the shortcut as well, because a correct answer read off one image earns as much reward as one that combines images. In line with this, existing RLVR objectives add little to the ASTERISM score. To stop rewarding the shortcut in training as ASTERISM does in evaluation, we propose cross-image credit assignment (CICA), an extension of RLVR that moves the credit toward the tokens that need more than one image. On three open-source MLLMs, CICA raises the ASTERISM score by 2.6 to 6.0 points, more than twice the gain of each general and vision-specific RL objective trained on the same data.

BRAG formalizes retrieval time-decay via Bayesian filtering
Why: Provides a practical closed-form filter for your agent state's memory decay.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 302 words

BRAG treats chunk utility as a drifting latent variable to enable dynamic reranking of long-term memory. On nine months of agent memory, it raised Recall@5 from 0.75 to 0.98.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) ranks memory chunks by semantic similarity to the query. Many methods improve on this by learning from feedback on what was retrieved, but each is a separate heuristic, with no account of when it is the right choice. We introduce BRAG, a generative model of retrieval with use. Each chunk generates the queries it answers from a mixture of von Mises-Fisher components anchored at its original embedding, and each chunk has a latent utility that drifts over time. The model gives the optimal inference, a closed-form filter that stores a few keys and two scalars per chunk and updates them online from the labels of a curator, and it frames retrieval as a control problem. It also shows when existing methods are optimal. Query averaging, Rocchio-style feedback and frequency reranking are special cases with fixed parameters, NUDGE is the case of one key per chunk with no dynamics, and LADER the limit of one key per past query. On two public long-term memory benchmarks, BRAG matches these methods on queries asked before, as expected, because when nothing changes over time the assumptions behind these special cases hold. On harder problems, where a new chunk makes an old one obsolete, BRAG outscores them. They all stay at plain-search level (Recall@5 0.67-0.71), while BRAG retrieves the new chunk 0.80-0.86 of the time. We also ran BRAG on three of NUDGE's seven text benchmarks, and it matches or beats NUDGE on all three. On nine months of a personal agent's memory (20,640 chunks), BRAG raises Recall@5 on held-out queries from 0.75 to 0.98 and Recall@1 from 0.44 to 0.97, with little change on chunks never queried.

PICK uses human redundancy checks to resolve ambiguity in GenAI specifications
Why: You need intent validation for spec-to-code pipelines with regex, ABAC, or LTL.
Murat Demirbas ↗HN 4↑extracted 04:00 · 1,205 words

When the only comment on a PR is "LGTM", due diligence has not been done. A glance is not understanding. Understanding has levels, and we almost always overestimate ours. But the LGTM stamps the code as correct anyway. Make no mistake (pun intended), this is also true for LLM-generated code, even with so-called "correct-by-construction" approaches.

Why does verification catch anything at all? Because of redundancy. You write the specification carefully and then write the implementation separately, and the verification check finds the places where the two disagree.

LLM code synthesis throws that away. It derives the implementation from the specification, so the two can almost never disagree. Correct-by-construction becomes a dangerous misnomer. If the specification is wrong, the implementation is wrong in exactly the same way. Better models will not fix this. Any move from natural language to a specification leaves an intent gap between what the user meant and what the specification captures. The devil is in the cornercases.

We need to do better than LGTM.

This ECOOP 2026 paper is brought to you by Brown. To resolve ambiguity in LLM-generated specification snippets, the paper introduces PICK, a human-in-the-loop tool that eliminates mismatched candidate formulas by asking users to classify concrete distinguishing scenarios sampled from the candidates' semantic differences. PICK brings the redundancy back in verification checks. Your answers about concrete cases are a second statement of intent, made in a different form than the prose prompt.

The idea

PICK derives not one but four candidate expressions from the user's prose description. It computes where the candidates disagree, and samples concrete scenarios from those regions to show them to the user two at a time, in a this-or-that format. The scenarios they study in the paper are strings for regexes, traces for LTL, and requests for access-control policies. The target is restricted languages for now as the title says. The user accepts or rejects each scenario, and each vote is checked against every candidate. A candidate is eliminated after two downvotes and needs at least one upvote to be selected. This repeats until one or zero candidates remain.

Each classification is checked against every candidate, not just against the one the scenario came from. Accept a scenario and every candidate whose language contains it gets an upvote, while the ones that exclude it get a downvote. One answer updates several candidates at once, and this keeps the number of questions small.

Rejecting is deliberately asymmetric. If you reject a scenario, the candidates that accept it get downvoted, but the candidates that also reject it get nothing. Otherwise a candidate could win by outlasting the others rather than by being chosen. A candidate has to earn at least one upvote to get selected. Ending with zero candidates is a legitimate outcome. It means either the LLM produced nothing correct or the user's intent is inconsistent.

How restricted do these languages need to be? Not very. You need to compute the set difference between two candidates, which means closure under negation and intersection. You need to decide equality, so that candidates with identical meaning can be collapsed before you waste questions on them. And you need to sample concrete instances out of a difference set. Regexes qualify. LTL qualifies, and they use Spot to generate the traces. ABAC (attribute-based access control) qualifies once you bound the entities.

Notably SQL does not as query containment is undecidable. The paper also gives a second reason: A query means nothing without a database, so the object the user has to judge is a query-and-database pair.

The experiments

The regex and ABAC studies had three groups of 50 participants each, recruited through the Prolific platform. Everyone got the same task: here are four candidate expressions, pick the right one.

The groups differ only in what help they get. The control group saw the four candidates and picked one, with no examples. They could also say none was correct, or that they were unsure. The other two groups used PICK and classified scenarios instead of reading formulas. One of those two could see the candidate list while classifying and the other could not. This is how they test whether seeing the formulas helps at all, but this turned out to make no measurable difference.

Regex experiment: On abWords (a/b strings with b in every even position), control got 22% right, versus 60-72% with PICK. The most common wrong answer, chosen by 19 of 50, was ~((a|b)b)+~, which requires even length. On Dates, where no candidate was correct, control got 26% and PICK 66-70%. Of the 13 control participants who said they regularly write regexes, none got both abWords and Dates right.

ABAC experiment: Significant improvement on two of the three policies. PICK users were also 2-4 times more likely to eliminate every candidate, while control users tended to pick a wrong policy that was close to the correct one. I think this is the right behavior. A subtly wrong policy may go unnoticed for a long time, whereas an empty result tells you immediately that something needs fixing.

LTL: There was no new control group. The candidates were formulas that LTL-trained participants wrote in two earlier studies by the same group, and those participants' accuracy served as the baseline. PICK users were crowdworkers with no LTL background who never saw a formula. On RedOnce ("red is on in exactly one state"), they got 68% versus about 25%. Pooled across three problems, 56% versus about 44%.

Inverting PICK: a Paxos tutor

The PICK idea should also work for TLA+. You write a prose description, an LLM gives you several candidate specs, and PICK asks you about concrete behaviors until one survives. That would close the intent gap, but the hard part is the design, not the theory. The regex, LTL, and access-control scenarios are considered toy compared with a full TLA+ spec. How to apply PICK in a scalable and manageable wayto a TLA+ spec is an open question. What do we show, and at what level of abstraction? I have not attempted that, but instead I tried something simpler: a Paxos tutor built on the PICK idea.

PICK assumes the specification is unknown and the user is the oracle. We flip that around here. The Paxos specification is known, but in this case the user would need to be brought up to speed on it. Following this approach, I built an interactive Paxos tutor. Ok, Opus 5.5 built it, but I had to babysit it over at least ten rounds. It is hard to teach a machine empathy for the user. And it turns out, as I suspected, distributed algorithms are still very hard for an LLM to manage, even with TLC as a backend.

The app is called "Do you Paxos?" and it is available here. The candidates are correct Paxos plus eleven mutants, each one a misconception people hold. Questions come from the steps where correct Paxos and a mutant disagree. None of this is hand-written. The app runs its own Paxos state machine, and every question is generated from one of its reachable states, with the answer attached. A TLA+ spec of Paxos is checked against that state machine on every build, so the two cannot drift apart.
