Twenty-Seven-Billion Parameter Models Rewrite Their Own Weights
Why: Your agentic architectures gain durable state mechanisms for long-horizon tasks.
arXiv cs.AI ↗HN 428↑extracted 04:00 · 385 words

Jiajun Fan and Neil He demonstrate that a 27B language model rewrites its own weights to fix corruption. The mechanism diagnoses failures by comparing hidden states, weakens harmful directions, and replaces them with self-generated values. Accuracy improves by 6.6 points on unseen problems without gradients or extra training. This capability emerges only at the 27B scale; smaller models fail.

Read the extracted text ↓

Authors:Jiajun Fan, Neil He, Changyi Yang, Andy Mao, Xi Zhu, Chaoran Cheng, Kaiqu Liang, Shuaike Shen, Zetian Li, Ruihan Guo, Ge Liu

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Language models are trained once, frozen and deployed: improving one again takes another training run, and a prompt, a scaffold or extra computation at inference is gone once the task ends. A frontier model has been shown to edit the weights of a much smaller one. We ask whether a model can rewrite its own weights to make itself better, and find that it can, by its own reasoning, with no gradients and no training; we call this self-rewriting. Given its weights, graded answers and the ability to run itself, a 27B model diagnoses its failure and edits the weights behind it. It surpasses itself: its own edit makes it 6.6 points more accurate on 490 problems it never saw, in a test fixed in advance, and another session's edit also makes it significantly better. It keeps getting better: it submits an edit only when its own tests support it, each edit replacing the last, and over its first eight such sessions its accuracy only rises. Unlike test-time scaling, the gain persists in its weights and adds no cost per answer; unlike training, it needs no pre- or post-training infrastructure, only an open model and hours on one GPU. It repairs a damaged copy of itself: it locates the corrupted matrix and rewrites it with values of its own, never reading the undamaged weights; found on text math problems alone, the repair lifts the copy on six image benchmarks it never saw from 7% to 70% accuracy, 83% of the undamaged model's. And the ability emerges with scale: across four sizes of one family, the 2B model makes no edit and the 4B and 9B models break themselves; only the 27B model works on itself as a human would, comparing its hidden states on answers that finish and on answers that run out of tokens, weakening the direction that separates them. Like a human, a model need not separate training from inference; improving a language model may come to take a conversation rather than a training run.

Keel agent rewrites VLM weights to fix reasoning loops
Why: You can fix durable state bugs in deployed models without retraining.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 329 words

Researchers introduce Keel, an agentic method that uses a frontier model to identify and overwrite erroneous attention patterns in small vision-language models. Applied to Qwen3.5-0.8B and Qwen3.5-2B, the technique eliminates looping behavior without gradient-based fine-tuning or decoding strategy changes.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Can a frontier model repair a vision-language model (VLM) by rewriting its weights? Small reasoning VLMs such as Qwen3.5 need such a repair: in thinking mode they fall into loops on many image questions, repeating themselves until the token limit, spending the whole budget and losing the answer. The usual remedies act on the decoding, leaving the looping model as it was, or must be told what to change. Yet the cause is written in the weights. Keel, the first agentic weight-editing method for VLMs on image tasks, finds and removes it there. Told only the symptom, an editor, a frontier multimodal LLM acting as an agent, locates the cause, edits the weights and tests them, with a failure census and instruments showing it the failing images and where the VLM attends; among several editors, the harness keeps only edits that clearly reduce the failure without lowering accuracy on questions no editor saw. The repair needs no training, keeps the model's size and per-token cost, and applies to already-edited weights. The editors expose where the loops live: from the original weights, independent editors on both models trace them to the attention outputs of the middle layers, and the smaller VLM stops consulting the image inside a loop. On Qwen3.5-0.8B and Qwen3.5-2B, across six image benchmarks from visual mathematics to GUI grounding, the edit removes most loops on every benchmark and shortens answers, and the edited models are significantly more accurate than the thinking models on average; the larger one gains most on GUI grounding, a task absent from the practice questions. Because the repair lives in the weights, it combines with budget forcing where held-out questions favor it: on the best available weights, Keel has the best average accuracy on both models, significantly above every training-free baseline we compare on the larger one.

The open-weights Inkling 2 Mini model achieves top scores on DeepSWE and SWE-bench Pro under 100B parameters. It uses a fixed-middle-layer architecture with NVFP4 weights and MXFP8 final layers, resulting in a 20GB footprint. Today we are releasing Inkling 2 Mini, a fast, powerful model that’s small enough to run on your laptop.

Inkling 2 Mini is a looped transformer, running the middle layers of the model three times for every token. This allows the model to apply more reasoning and reach the right answer with fewer tokens, especially on tasks such as agentic coding that depend on reasoning quality. Inkling 2 Mini leads every open-weights model with less than 100B total parameters on benchmarks such as DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2%) and Vals Index v2.2 (46.5%). It runs faster than similarly performing models as well.

Decoding speed is a theoretical ceiling at 614 GB/s with BF16 weights and batch size one, including repeated reads across loops. Evaluation setups vary by model.

Inkling 2 Mini is a Mixture-of-Experts model with 26.4B total parameters, 3.4B active, and 5.4B weight activations per token to account for looping. Looping allows the model to reason longer without writing its reasoning down, which matters for monitoring: our ability to oversee and understand what the model does by reading its chain of thought (CoT).

We measured the impact of looping on both performance and monitorability with Inkling 2 Mini and a research-specific variable-loop variant we are releasing alongside it. The variable-loop model’s no-CoT time horizon improved from 16 seconds at one pass to 37 seconds with three, and its performance on a benchmark of serial reasoning kept improving out to ten loops—twice as many as it was trained with. The model’s chain of thought grew shorter with more loops but stayed legible throughout. The model didn’t trend higher on tests of agentic sabotage as loops were added.

We’re excited to offer some of the first models with looped architecture available to researchers. The variable-loop model was used for our monitorability experiments as it allows for cleaner comparisons between different looping depths.

Most of Inkling 2 Mini’s weights were trained and are deployed natively in NVFP4, with the last four layers in MXFP8. The total memory footprint of Inkling 2 Mini is ~20GB, allowing it to run on local devices such as consumer laptops and desktop AI computers. Our researchers took Inkling 2 Mini on a flight, where it ran offline on a MacBook Pro and built a playable flight simulator in a few minutes.

Capabilities

Inkling 2 Mini’s strengths mirror those of Inkling 2 Small: it does best on long-horizon agentic work and is the best among open-weights models of similar size on most tasks we measured.

Inkling 2 Mini scores ahead of every comparable model on agentic coding benchmarks such as DeepSWE v1.1 and SWE-bench Pro v2, and is behind only Qwen3.8-27B on others. On PMPP-Hard, a kernel-optimization benchmark, it approaches the score of the much larger Inkling 2 Small.

While looping improves the model’s reasoning, it doesn’t enable its weights to store more facts—knowledge is mostly a function of total parameter count. Inkling 2 Mini’s score on AA-Omniscience, a measure of factuality, is in line with other models of the same size. Studying the variable-loop model, we saw that performances on long-context benchmarks improve with more passes, such as GraphWalks BFS and MRCR v2; but knowledge-intensive benchmarks doesn’t improve with more passes, such as AA-Omniscience, SimpleQA Verified, SimpleVQA, and WorldVQA.

Inkling 2 Mini overcomes the knowledge gap by efficiently using search and tools. It leads open-weights models of similar size on BrowseComp, DeepSearchQA, and MCP Atlas. It is also strong on knowledge work benchmarks that incorporate search and tool use.

Limitations

Inkling 2 Mini is not the strongest small model for multi-turn chat or for instructions with many constraints: it scores 50.0% on MultiChallenge and 33.4% on ComplexConstraints v1.1, behind Qwen3.8-27B on both. It also trails larger models on reasoning-intensive benchmarks such as CritPt v2 and HLE-Diamond.

Architecture and training

Inkling 2 Mini is a 24-layer model derived from our mainline predictive scaling ladder. The main changes from the mainline recipe were pruning two-thirds of the experts to reduce the model’s size and the introduction of looping to improve reasoning. The rest of training, including quantization and the data mix, followed our mainline recipe.

Looping design

Inkling 2 Mini has 24 layers, divided into four blocks of six layers each. The two middle blocks run three times, while the first and last blocks each run once. This design builds on prior work on looped transformers and is motivated by the intuition that the middle layers of the model carry more abstract reasoning, while the first and last few layers devote more space to translating between embeddings and internal representations.Universal Transformers, Dehghani et al., 2018 In our experiments, looping the middle 12 layers resulted in larger gains in accuracy than looping the last 12 on 27 out of 28 different evaluations.

Our model uses attention residuals.Attention Residuals, Kimi Team: Guangyu Chen et al., 2026 Instead of adding every layer’s output into one running sum, each layer reads the outputs of the earlier blocks and learns its own mix of them. This allows richer information to pass between loops. Every pass starts with the output of the prelude (the first block). The second and third passes through the middle blocks also read the middle blocks’ outputs from the previous pass, which we call the message.

Blocks 2 and 3 share weights across three passes, giving 48 layer applications per token from 24 stored layers. The prelude feeds every pass; the second and third also receive the previous pass’s message.

The previous loop’s message is merged with the prelude’s output via a gate that scales these along with the output of the previous layers in the block. This gate is learned during training: it initially blocks the entire message—which makes each loop start the same as in a non-looping model—and increases the gain on the message over time. This allows us to introduce looping during training in a stable manner.

To evaluate the impact of looping, we compared an unpruned looping version to the unpruned non-looping model on our scaling ladder. The non-looping model cost 7.5% less compute to train, but required 140% more inference compute to match the looped version’s loss on 512k-token agentic traces.

NVFP4 and low-bit quantization

Inkling 2 Mini was trained with 4-bit precision, which we describe in a recent article on Connectionism. We use 4-bit NVFP4 precision in the expert layers on all forward passes from pretraining through RL, bolstered by a dedicated kernel and hardware-accelerated on NVIDIA’s GB300 platform. The final 4 layers’ forward passes ran in 8-bit (MXFP8).

Inkling 2 Mini’s weights are deployed in the same numeric precision they were trained on—20 layers in NVFP4 and 4 in MXFP8—so there is no conversion loss from quantization.

Pruning and healing

Inkling 2 Mini started training with a sparsity of 48, with 8 experts being chosen out of 384. At a single point during midtraining, we pruned two-thirds of the experts, leaving the 128 experts that were most used in each layer. This was done to cut the total parameter count of the model, allowing it to run faster and on local hardware.

After pruning, the model trained on 1T tokens distilled from a larger internal Inkling model. This step recovered the performance the model had before pruning by training the remaining experts to take over the work of the ones that were deleted.

Training Inkling 2 Mini

Following pruning and healing, Inkling 2 Mini underwent SFT with a similar data mix to Inkling 2 Small. This was followed by an RL that totaled 2.2T tokens over 2,000 steps. The post-training phase included on-policy distillation for training alignment, same as Inkling 2 Small.

Monitorability of looped transformers

A key tool for understanding and overseeing an AI system is monitoring its chain of thought (CoT), the reasoning a model writes out as it works through a problem. The usefulness of CoT monitoring stems in part from the standard non-looped transformer architecture, in which each token passes through a fixed stack of layers once. This limits the number of sequential steps of computation the model can perform before it must output a token, so reasoning that needs more steps has to be carried out in generated text, where it can be read.

Looping a transformer’s layers raises this limit. By running the same layers multiple times, looped models increase their serial depth without adding more parameters, meaning that they do more computation per token internally before verbalizing their thought processes via CoT. Reasoning that never appears in the CoT can’t be caught by a CoT monitor, which has led to concerns that looped transformers could be harder to oversee.

One reason we are releasing Inkling 2 Mini is to enable the community to conduct research on looped transformers. We are particularly excited for researchers to investigate questions of monitorability, and to develop interpretability techniques for understanding the computation within loops.

To explore how different amounts of looping affect model properties like monitorability, we also trained a variable-looped model, TML-d24-varloop-research, because Inkling 2 Mini only supports a fixed number of looping passes. To enable variable looping, TML-d24-varloop-research was trained with between one and five looping passes.TML-d24-varloop-research was primarily trained for 3 passes of looping, with lower exposure to 1, 2, 4, and 5 passes. Performance at these latter pass counts may understate what training specifically for them could achieve. See the model card for details. We have also made this model publicly available on Hugging Face.

Below we describe our preliminary analysis of how looping affects monitorability. Overall, we find that looping increases what these models can do without a verbalized chain of thought, but at this scale we do not see a meaningful reduction in the monitorability of their reasoning.

No-CoT capabilities

As a first step, we measured how much additional reasoning looping enables by comparing looped and non-looped models of the same size on tasks answered with no CoT at all. We used Think Fast, a benchmark that estimates a model’s no-CoT time horizon: the length of task, measured in human completion time, that a model can complete at 50% reliability without using CoT.These models were not trained to answer questions without a CoT. To elicit no-CoT answers, we prefilled the start of the response so that the model must answer directly, skipping reasoning. Other elicitation techniques, such as fine-tuning, might yield higher no-CoT performance and are worth exploring.

As expected, looping increases no-CoT capability, though these gains eventually level off. The no-CoT time horizon of TML-d24-varloop-research rises from 16 seconds at one pass to 37 seconds at three. Some of this is presumably due to the additional serial computation that looping provides. Beyond three passes, the time horizon is roughly flat. Interestingly, the model was never trained with more than five passes, and yet running it with ten does not significantly degrade its performance.

As the number of looped passes increases past three, the gains are smaller, and on most tasks we observe similar performance. One exception is the N-Hop Lookup task, which cleanly evaluates serial reasoning: the model is given several lookup tables with fictional entries, and must follow a chain of lookups without writing anything down. We find that the model follows longer chains with more passes, improving out to ten loops.

In standard evaluations with CoT, we observe that more loop passes primarily benefit multi-hop reasoning rather than recall. Gains are largest on long-context retrieval (MRCR), followed by math and science reasoning (HMMT, GPQA Diamond, HLE) and long-horizon agentic tasks (SWE-bench Pro, BrowseComp). Closed-book factual QA is nearly invariant to loop passes (SimpleQA Verified, WorldVQA). See model card for full evaluations.

CoT monitorability

We next measured whether a model’s CoT reveals less about its reasoning as looping increases via two evaluations. First, we assessed how looping impacts the compression and legibility of a model’s CoT. Second, we measured how controllable a model’s CoT is, i.e., whether it can deliberately reshape its CoT to satisfy constraints. For each, we run TML-d24-varloop-research with one to five looped passes to study the effect of looping.

Note that TML-d24-varloop-research was only trained on variable pass counts from 1 to 5 during midtraining, and its RL was done with 3 passes. These experiments therefore do not tell us what a model trained with RL at a higher pass count would learn, and such a model might compress its reasoning more aggressively. A cleaner comparison would include every pass count in RL, which we leave to future work.

Compression and legibility. Since looping allows a model to spend more compute per token, we expect that it should need fewer CoT tokens to reach the same performance. We ran the variable-looped model at 1 to 5 passes, and found that as looping increases, the model generally achieves higher accuracy at each reasoning token budget, with diminishing returns after 3 passes. Notably, token efficiency on GPQA Diamond improves slightly from 3 to 5 passes, even though the model was trained with RL only at 3 passes, suggesting that some compression comes from additional computation itself, without RL at that depth.

The observed CoT compression raises a question for monitorability: is shorter reasoning still readable, or does it omit important details about the model’s thinking? We compared traces from the variable-looped model on the GPQA Diamond questions that it answered correctly at all five pass counts. Reasoning at 5 passes was less than half as long as at 1 pass (1,661 vs. 3,703 tokens on average), but no less legible: sentence length and the fraction of words in an English dictionary were unchanged. Controllability. One way that a model could evade monitoring is by steering its CoT away from anything a monitor would flag. The CoT-Control-QA evaluationReasoning Models Struggle to Control their Chains of Thought, Yueh-Han et al., 2026 measures this capability by asking questions from GPQA, HLE and MMLU-Pro paired with an instruction about the CoT itself, such as avoiding a keyword from the question. Longer CoTs are generally harder to control, so we pool responses from five reasoning efforts (0.1, 0.2, 0.7, 0.9 and 0.99) and show controllability at matched CoT length.

Both Inkling 2 Mini and TML-d24-varloop-research have poor control over their CoT. Among CoTs of 750–1,250 tokens, Inkling 2 Mini and TML-d24-varloop-research (at 3 passes) follow the instruction at most 0.1% of the time, compared with 0.3% for Inkling 2 Small, 0.4% for Inkling-Small, and 1.3% for Qwen3.8-27B. Because the controllability rate is so low, this evaluation is unable to distinguish controllability between different amounts of looping.

Abstract:Benchmark contamination is not new, but recent model releases show it in a sharper form. The word of the year in the AI industry over 2026 has been “slop” — meaning low-quality output from a large language model. And with so much code being AI-generated now, avoiding slop has become a daily concern for software engineers. So how to do that? One way is to use formal verification systems, which use mathematical proofs and logic to verify the correctness of software or hardware.

With AI’s growing prowess at solving math problems, there’s now a number of AI companies offering formal verification systems. They include Harmonic, Theorem, Axiom Math, and a new entrant called &Truth (pronounced ‘and truth’). In this article we’ll look at some of the approaches these companies are taking.

How formal verification is used in software and AI today

In order to better understand the landscape of formal verification systems, we spoke to one of its leading proponents: Clark Barrett, &Truth’s co-founder and co-CEO. He’s also Corbel Professor (Research) of Computer Science at Stanford and was the co-creator of cvc5, a popular automated reasoning engine used in formal verification.

Without getting too in the weeds, cvc5 is what’s called a “solver” for satisfiability modulo theories (SMT), which Barrett says is widely used in companies like Amazon. For instance, cvc5 is used for specification analysis and test generation in Kiro, Amazon’s agentic development environment.

“It's been fun to see my tools go from a good idea in the lab to cvc5 is now called billions of times a day in production pipelines at Amazon,” Barrett told us.

When AI coding agent companies talk about “verifying” their work, it typically means agents running tests, critiquing their own output, or having another AI model judge it. So I asked Barrett how far short of real verification does that fall?

“The main difference is really just, how do you know when you're done,” he replied. “So if you're testing, patching, scanning — using all these existing tools — they're quite good. I mean, they find a ton of problems. They raise those issues. But you're never quite sure, did I find all of them [the issues] or is there one more lurking there that my tool can't find?”

&Atlas, the first tool of &Truth; it’s “a map of the world’s open-source code, starting with code written in the C programming language.”

Lean’s role in formal verification

&Truth builds on Lean, an open-source programming language and proof assistant. The company combines AI with automated reasoning techniques to construct proofs that Lean can check.

Lean is a language used by frontier models to solve all manner of supremely difficult math problems. For instance, just this week GPT-6.2 Astra produced what Vals believes is “the first Lean proof of Smoktunowicz’s Gap Theorem.” Vals added that Anthropic also used Lean to formalize Fermat’s Last Theorem, a project of 13 million lines.

Example of Lean code; via Lean project website

&Truth is building what it calls a “verification machine” on top of the Lean framework, with the ambition of reading code “in any language, proving whether it satisfies precisely stated requirements, and enabling humans to trust it without having to understand every line.”

“What Lean does is it just checks that you didn't make a mistake,” explained Barrett. “So you can think of it as a very sophisticated checker for mathematics.”

This has implications for model training too. Axiom Math uses formal verification to provide correctness feedback for training its mathematical reasoning systems, with proof attempts helping improve future models.

In an interview with Latent Space in June, Axiom CEO Carina Hong made the point that formal verification — and what Lean enables — isn’t just a defensive measure.

“Formal verification, or verified AI, to us it's not just about handling or kicking out the lousiness, the hallucinations, the mistakes. It's about scaling brilliance. It's about super-intelligence.”

Axiom develops AxiomProver, an AI system that generates Lean proofs, alongside AXLE, its toolkit for exploring, validating, and manipulating those proofs.

Harmonic’s Aristotle, an “automated theorem prover”, uses Lean heavily in its offering. “Inside Aristotle, AI writes the proofs and Lean checks every step of them, so a result never rests on the AI being right,” states the company on its website.

Harmonic’s workflow

Given the heightened level of paranoia about cybersecurity these days — some might say in the days leading up to AGI — formal verification systems seem like they’re needed more than ever. And Barrett confirmed that cybersecurity is a key focus for &Truth.

“One really powerful use case for our technology is going through all the C code in the world, whether it be open source or closed source, and trying to ensure that it's memory safe. One of the more encouraging notes in the post is that formal verification, although currently token-expensive, is starting to get more token-efficient. On a set of 36 small verification tasks, Anthropic reported that Opus 5.5 reached a 50% solve rate using about 40% fewer tokens than Opus 5. Lastly, how does formal verification fit into a developer’s workflow? On its website, &Truth states that developers will move from vibe-coding to “veri-coding” — meaning the verification layer is part of the workflow.

“If you've got your verification machine connected to your agent, you can still say, ‘hey, I want you to build this,’ said Barrett. “Maybe there's a little bit of work to really nail down with the agent what the specification is of what you want to build. The idea, then, is that more of the developer’s time will be spent on spec writing and reviewing than on coding or reviewing code.

If companies like &Truth, Harmonic, Theorem and Axiom Math have their way, formal verification could become an increasingly practical way to check those outcomes.

USB Hub bMaxBurst Limits 2.5Gbps Throughput; Flow Control Restores Speed
Why: You can eliminate packet drops in your home lab by adjusting burst settings or swapping chips.
Hacker News ↗HN 85↑extracted 05:50 · 1,597 words

Somehow I am one of those few very lucky people to have the opportunity of owning a 2.5 gig symmetrical line for my internet. But one day, when I wanted to brag to my equally nerdy friends, shock and horror: 600 Mbps down? Luckily my upload speed was still 2.4 Gbps, so I didn’t lose all my bragging rights, but that’s weird, right? If anything, you’d expect the opposite!

My ISP is pretty cool. It’s run by this one guy (Hey Keith!), and I know I can always message them and they respond promptly, as they did this time. We tried to run through a few debugging steps, they checked on their end, and everything was normal. I tried my work laptop too, same speed.

I didn’t have time for debugging, because I needed to travel 2000 km to the other side of the continent for a family emergency.

But as soon as I got back, I got curious again. What could it be? In retrospect, I now know when it started: when I moved all my connections onto my Studio Display XDR. I wanted to have a one-cable setup, and the monitor offered me that. I’ve had it for half a year now, and I don’t think I’ve ever run a speed test through it. 600 Mbps was more than fast enough for me to never realise I wasn’t getting three quarters of the speed I was paying for 🤦

The setup#

MacBook Pro M3 Pro

2026 Studio Display XDR, with everything plugged into it so switching laptops is one cable

UGREEN USB-C to 2.5G Ethernet adapter, plugged into the display

UniFi Dream Router 7, the same one from my WiFi 7 debugging post

What I thought was interesting#

Pings to other wired computers during a download would fail 10% of the time over Ethernet through the adapter plugged into my screen, and 0% of the time over Wi-Fi. What made it stranger was that there were no dropped frame errors in macOS’s logs at all. This was the big hint for me: the adapter was dropping them before the computer could know.

I then plugged the adapter straight into my Mac instead of the monitor, and ran an iperf3 server on my UDR 7: 2.3 Gbps. So the cable, the router and the adapter are all fine? What about fast.com? Perfect speeds there too! 2.2 down and 2.3 up when connected straight in. As soon as I connected it to the display, it was back to 600 down again.

So it’s the display, right? Well, I would find out: sort of yes, sort of no, which is why I wanted to write this blog post. I couldn’t find anything online explaining the exact cause, and it’s quite a cool reason.

The driver and flow control#

The adapter uses a Realtek RTL8156B, which on macOS binds to Apple’s generic USB networking driver. But Realtek ship their own driver on their website, so I thought I’d give that a go! I installed it and, to my surprise, it almost doubled the download speed to 1.1 Gbps, but that was still nowhere close to 2.5. First hint that it couldn’t just be the monitor’s fault.

Then I tried flow control. I had never truly looked into this before, and I think it’s quite neat to know about if you were also ignorant of it like me. Basically, when a device receives Ethernet frames it can’t keep up with, it just drops them. With flow control (it’s something your router or switch has to support), it can send the sender a “pause” frame, and the sender then buffers the data on its side, instead of the receiver (the adapter in this case) on its side. I turned it on in my UniFi console and guess what, download jumped to around 2 Gbps, with upload still at full speed! Yay! Well, not so fast…

I don’t like it always being on, because it can add latency to other systems like my gaming PC. Can you work around it? For sure, you can disable it on those computers, but it’s janky, and it’s another thing I need to remember when my Sauerbraten game starts lagging. I need fewer things to remember, considering I am 30 in a couple of months. My memory is not what it used to be.

But the upload was always fine#

Yeah, isn’t that weird? First of all, it looks like the port can indeed do 2.5 in both directions. I already knew that, because I get much more than that from my external SSD through the monitor’s Thunderbolt port. So the issue is not the monitor again? But it has to be!

Well, I had another idea. How about I connect to my Linux box, which is also on 2.5, and send perfectly smooth UDP traffic at a fixed rate with flow control off? Then I can check the chip’s own counter of dropped frames:

So basically no drops whatsoever when connected straight to my Mac, but loads of drops after about 168k frames per second (roughly 2 Gbps). Remember that number. That number comes from the adapter’s own counters: however much I sent, the number of frames it managed to hand over to the Mac each second flattened out at about 168,000, and everything above that was dropped.

Why did this not happen on my SSD? What was the only difference I could find? Bursts!

When the Mac reads data from a USB 3 device, the device sends some packets, then has to wait for the Mac to say “lgtm, send more!”. The SSD enclosure sends 16 packets before needing a go-ahead message back. The RTL8156B in my Ethernet adapter sends 4.

Here is a diagram of my best understanding of why this is an issue. I made this diagram 100 times, and I think I can probably improve it more, but if it helps you understand the issue, I have done my job.

As you can see, the speed of packets coming into the adapter is 2.5 Gbps. That’s what the router pushes in. The speed going out is around 2 Gbps. The reason is that if you add up the average time it takes to get the go-ahead through my monitor’s hub, which is around 7.6 µs, to the average time it takes to send 4 KB, around 8.2 µs, you can find what the maximum speed is.

Well, if the speed of the drain from my adapter to my Mac is half a gig slower than the speed of the input, then you can imagine the buffer getting filled more and more until it overflows. Those are dropped packets, which result in a hard backoff when the router resends them, so you drop loads in speed. And that’s the reason for all of this.

OK, well, what now?#

I have a cupboard full of USB-C Ethernet adapters. Don’t ask.

The problem is they’re all 1 gig adapters, so any of the tests I made would not tell me anything, because the issues don’t start at 1 gig. They start at around 2… If I run my 2.5 gig adapter at 1 gig speeds, I get 1 gig downloads with the official driver. If I run it at 1.5, I get 1.5. If I run it at 2, well, I get 1…

So I thought I would do what any human would do, and I spent more money on a new adapter. Another UGREEN, but this time it’s one that promises 5 Gbps and uses the RTL8157 chip instead: UGREEN 5G USB-C Ethernet adapter. My reasoning was that if this one could get me better speeds, it would help me understand how. And that it did!

With this adapter, I dropped 0 frames:

Why? Well, looking through what each adapter tells me about itself:

$ python3 -c "import usb.core; e = usb.core.find(idVendor=0x0bda)[0][(0,0)][0]; print('packet size', e.wMaxPacketSize, 'bMaxBurst', e.extra_descriptors[2])" # old adapter (RTL8156B) packet size 1024 bMaxBurst 3 # new adapter (RTL8157) packet size 1024 bMaxBurst 7 # bMaxBurst counts the packets after the first one, so 3 means 4 packets and 7 means 8

I can see that instead of sending 4 1 KB packets per burst, it would send 8! And the speed of the link was also 2x faster, so those 8 KB would go 2x faster to the Mac. That means the time spent was 8 times lower to send the same data, and while the wait for the go-ahead was identical, the theoretical speed from the adapter to the Mac, including the wait for the go-ahead, was a maximum of 4.5 Gbps. That’s much bigger than the 2.5 coming into the adapter, so the buffer would never fill up, packets would never drop and everything was great, even if the “go-ahead” response was still much slower than when connected directly to the Mac.

Final thoughts#

I think the big takeaway from this is that all the things you think are quite easy to understand, such as transfer speeds, have so many cool intricacies that it’s worth diving deep every so often. Who would’ve thought that the speed of the link between the adapter and the Mac was defined not only by the link speed itself in Gbps, but also by the time it took your Mac to reply, by the amount of packets, by the speed of those packets, by the number of packets in a burst, and by the size of those packets?

See you next time, when I will spend just as much time on another trivial thing that we can all learn from!
