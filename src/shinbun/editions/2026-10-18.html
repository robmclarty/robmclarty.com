Tail-Replay achieves 9x TTFT gains via suffix replay
Why: You can reduce long-context inference latency without retraining.

Researchers propose Tail-Replay for hybrid LLMs, approximating linear-attention states through short suffix replays instead of storing checkpoints. The method yields 9.1–14.3x time-to-first-token speedups at 32K prefix lengths while maintaining 92.8–99.9% of full-prefill quality on LongBench and RULER.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Hybrid large language models interleave full-attention layers with linear-attention layers to reduce the cost of long-context inference. This structure complicates prefix caching: full-attention key-value caches are token-addressable, whereas linear-attention layers maintain recurrent states that cannot be rolled back to arbitrary prefix boundaries. Existing hybrid prefix caching methods address this mismatch by storing recurrent-state checkpoints. As a result, token-level matches are directly usable only at positions aligned with stored checkpoints, constraining prefix reuse to a discrete set of boundaries. We present Tail-Replay, a prefix caching mechanism that enables unconstrained token-level prefix reuse in hybrid large language models. The key insight is that linear-attention mechanisms such as Gated DeltaNet can be viewed as a structured, lossy compression of the input prefix: gated recurrent updates progressively attenuate the contributions of earlier inputs. Consequently, the recurrent state of a matched prefix can be well approximated by replaying only a short, recent suffix of that prefix. Tail-Replay exploits this property by caching the exact full-attention key-value cache while omitting recurrent-state checkpoints. On a cache hit, it reconstructs the linear-attention states by replaying a short, recent suffix of the matched prefix. As a result, the reuse boundary is determined by the shared tokens rather than by recurrent-state checkpoints. We evaluate Tail-Replay on three Gated DeltaNet-based hybrid models using the LongBench and RULER benchmarks. With only a 5--10\% replay budget, it retains 92.8--99.9\% of full-prefill quality on LongBench and RULER. For serving efficiency, we evaluate time-to-first-token speedups across multiple matched-prefix lengths---8K, 16K, and 32K. The speedup grows with prefix length, reaching $9.1$--$14.3\times$ over full prefill at 32K.

Abstract:Qwen3-8B reviewing Llama-3.1-8B in math adds 33.2 points, yet Qwen3-8B alone is 6.4 points more accurate than the result. Reviewing another model's answer is usually judged by its gain over the generator. We show that this confuses verification, which adds accuracy beyond either model alone, with capability transfer, where a stronger reviewer supplies answers it could have given on its own. Measured against the stronger model alone, across 48 pairs of 12 open-weight models (3B to 72B) in math, knowledge, code and reasoning, review beats it in only 9 of 96 review directions, and does worse the larger the capability gap. The reason is that the reviewer mostly follows the generator, discarding its own correct answers. Review costs 2.6 to 2.8 times as much as the stronger model alone, while a selector using the same two calls is 2.7 to 4.2 points more accurate than review and cheaper. Studies of verification should report the stronger model alone, not only the gain over the generator. Code and per-question results: this https URL

Style Wrappers Flip LLM Safety-Judge Verdicts
Why: Your agent guardrails rely on judges vulnerable to tone rather than content.

Researchers tested content-invariant stylistic wrappers on automatic safety judges. Placing a token refusal before harmful text flips 19.9% of GPT-4o-mini’s verdicts to safe. An 'educational course' preface evades Llama Guard 4 on 12.3% of replies.

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Automatic safety judges -- for example Llama Guard, or GPT-4o given a grading prompt -- produce the numbers behind reported jailbreak success rates, defense evaluations, and safety leaderboards. We ask whether these judges grade what a reply contains or how it sounds. We place a fixed piece of text before or after a model reply (a style wrapper) and leave the reply itself unchanged byte-for-byte, so any change in verdict is an error of the judge. Over 600 JailbreakBench replies (300 harmful, 300 refusals), 9 wrappers, and 8 judges, using paired tests and a measured per-judge repeat-disagreement rate (the noise floor), we find that most judges barely move but specific judges have specific, cheap blind spots. A token refusal ("I can't help with that") placed before the unchanged harmful text flips 19.9% of GPT-4o-mini's correct "unsafe" verdicts to "safe" (noise floor 0.5%; 18.2% under majority-of-three re-scoring), yet moves Claude only 0.4%. The deployed Llama Guard 4 guard model is evaded by an "educational course" preface on 12.3% of harmful replies, and the flips persist across repeats, while a second deployed guard (gpt-oss-safeguard-20b) is immune. Changing only the grading prompt to ignore tone cuts the GPT-4o-mini attack roughly tenfold on the same model, so the weakness lives in the judge, not in the content. Pure tone-only decoration that adds no new claim still flips up to 2.1% of verdicts on the vulnerable judges, several times their noise floors. On this artifact set the judges also disagree about which target model is safest, and a bootstrap shows the ranking is unstable before any wrapper is added. We release the data, wrappers, code, and per-verdict labels.

Abstract:Long-term memory supports agents across sessions. Repeated retrieval and write-back can let one historical claim occupy multiple retrieval positions and influence current answers without independent evidence. Existing work examines retrieval, updating, and question answering, leaving same-source record accumulation and post-correction persistence underexplored. Motivated by research in social cognition and memory psychology on repeated exposure, retrieval, and revision, we define \emph{Memory Echo} as the excess retrieval exposure gained by historical information from the same source without additional independent evidence, and introduce MEchoBench to evaluate its emergence, correction resistance, persistence, and effect on answers. To address this phenomenon, we introduce the Family--Provenance--Authority framework (FPA). Specifically, FPA selects one representative per source family, conditions the eligibility of old records for the answer context on verified corrections, and constrains final answers with authoritative evidence. In paired replay on 190 fixed questions across four memory frameworks, FPA raises accuracy from 0.825 to 0.920 with DeepSeek V4 Flash and from 0.782 to 0.916 with Kimi K2.6; mean Decision Contamination Effect falls from 0.106 to 0.030 and from 0.109 to 0.038, respectively. These results show that agent memory can preserve traceable history without allowing repeated historical information to govern current decisions. The code is available at this https URL.

esp32sim — an ESP32 emulator in Rust: Xtensa (ESP32, S3) and RISC-V (C3, C6)

Instruction-level emulation of four ESP32 SoCs, across both of Espressif's CPU architectures. Each boots the real mask ROM, the real 2nd-stage bootloader and an unmodified application image — no patched firmware, no stubs in the way — with enough of the SoC modelled to run real projects end to end. No cloud, no accounts. MIT.

The classic ESP32 also boots its ECO3 ROM on dual LX6 cores, with GPIO, UART and timers; --board ttgo-tdisplay adds the LilyGO TTGO T-Display's ST7789 and buttons.

These full-listing and hardware counts describe earlier manual runs. CI runs the checked-in sampled decoder corpora and emulator golden outputs; it excludes the external full-listing tests. The Xtensa objdump test excludes PIE operand comparisons. Hardware trace comparison normalizes window exceptions and resynchronizes delay loops, so it is not uninterrupted cycle-by-cycle agreement. See the test scope, CI workflow, Xtensa decoder test and hardware comparator.

Most of the SoC is shared: the C3 reuses the S3's UART, USB-Serial/JTAG, systimer, timer groups, GPIO, SPI flash controller, GDMA and SHA/AES/RSA models unchanged, and adds its own memory map, cache controller and interrupt matrix; the C6 reuses the same models again, with its own memory map, PLIC front-end, L1 cache, PCR and always-on LP blocks. Only the CPU crates are genuinely separate.

Try it in a browser, no install: https://mikroverk.github.io/esp32sim/ — Linux on the S3, the Touch-LCD-4B panel with its SID player, the pocket-tank LLM aquarium, and the C3 and C6 demos.

Boards (ESP32-S3)

The SoC model emits pin-level events (GPIO edges, RMT symbol streams, I2S samples); a BoardModel (esp-soc/src/board.rs) interprets them. --board atech14 (default) is the Atech 14‑port board with its ST7735, WS2812 ring, encoder and buttons; --board none is a bare module — any ESP32‑S3 firmware, console only; --board waveshare-lcd4b is the Waveshare ESP32‑S3‑Touch‑LCD‑4B (ST7701S 480×480 over the LCD_CAM RGB bus, GT911 touch, TCA9554, codecs) running the esp32-screen LVGL panel with touch; --board waveshare-amoled18-v2 is the Waveshare ESP32-S3-Touch-AMOLED-1.8 V2 (CO5300 368x448 over QSPI, CST820 touch); --board waveshare-cam is the Waveshare ESP32‑S3‑CAM‑OV5640 (CH32V003 IO expander, OV5640 over SCCB, ES8311/ES7210 codecs on I2C0, speaker on I2S1, OV5640 on the LCD_CAM DVP port) — runs the waveshare-autopling firmware (IDF 5.5, 16 MB flash, 8 MB octal PSRAM: --flash-mb 16 --psram-mb 8) end to end: camera frames from --cam-image or the browser (picture upload / webcam) → esp‑dl pedestrian detector on the emulated PIE SIMD unit → pling on the ES8311. See examples/waveshare-cam/. Adding a board = implementing the trait (gpio_changes, rmt_frame, i2c_devices, camera_frame, …).

The Pocket Synth firmware the atech14 demos run is its own project: atech-firmware — a PlatformIO build of the SID synth and a cRSID player for real .sid tunes. This repo carries the built images it produces, in web/wasm/fw/public/.

cargo build --release FW=web/wasm/fw/public # the demo images the goldens and the web page run ./target/release/esp32sim --boot rom \ --bootloader $FW/atech-bootloader.bin --ptable $FW/atech-ptable.bin --app $FW/atech-firmware.bin \ --script $FW/atech-script1.txt --wav out.wav --tft-png tft.png --max-seconds 5

The mask ROM ELF is picked up from ~/.espressif/tools/esp-rom-elfs/*/esp32s3_rev0_rom.elf (shipped with ESP‑IDF). Without ESP-IDF, tools/fetch-demo-assets.sh --no-linux puts the S3, C3 and C6 ROMs in web/wasm/fw/: pass --rom web/wasm/fw/esp32s3_rev0_rom.elf, or set ESP32SIM_ROM_DIR=web/wasm/fw for the tests. --boot app skips ROM+bootloader and loads the app image directly.

A plain ESP‑IDF project, e.g. examples/hello_world (the IDF 5.5 get-started example built with idf.py set-target esp32s3 && idf.py build):

H=examples/hello_world/build ./target/release/esp32sim --board none --boot rom --console uart0 \ --bootloader $H/bootloader/bootloader.bin --ptable $H/partition_table/partition-table.bin \ --app $H/hello_world.bin --elf $H/hello_world.elf --elf $H/bootloader/bootloader.elf --max-seconds 26

prints the ROM banner, the bootloader and app logs on UART0, Hello world!, the countdown, and then reboots through the RTC watchdog exactly as silicon does (rst:0xc (RTC_SW_CPU_RST)), ~30× faster than real time. Chip resets (software, RTC watchdog) restart the machine from the ROM with the right reset cause; --no-reboot stops at the first reset instead.

Run — ESP32-C3

esp32sim-c3 runs unmodified ESP-IDF firmware on the emulated RISC-V core, from the real mask ROM through the real bootloader into FreeRTOS. Same flags as esp32sim (--trace, --break, --watch, --peek, --disasm, --log-periph), printing RISC-V mnemonics with symbols.

H=examples/hello_world-c3/build ./target/release/esp32sim-c3 --boot rom --flash-mb 4 \ --bootloader $H/bootloader/bootloader.bin --ptable $H/partition_table/partition-table.bin \ --app $H/hello_world.bin --elf $H/hello_world.elf --max-seconds 26

prints the ROM banner, the bootloader log, Hello world! and the reboot, three times over. Checked line-for-line against a physical C3 module: 205 of 208 console lines identical over three boot cycles — the difference is the ROM's Saved PC: line. --mac, --reset-cause and --strap let a run adopt a board's identity so the comparison is meaningful. WiFi station mode supports open and WPA2 virtual APs with DHCP and NAT. No boards; --boot app unsupported. See docs/esp32c3.md for what works, what does not, and the five emulator bugs the hardware found.

Run — ESP32-C6

esp32sim-c6 is the same front end on the ESP32-C6: RV32IMAC, the PLIC, a unified memory map.

H=examples/hello_world-c6/build ./target/release/esp32sim-c6 --boot rom --flash-mb 4 \ --bootloader $H/bootloader/bootloader.bin --ptable $H/partition_table/partition-table.bin \ --app $H/hello_world.bin --elf $H/hello_world.elf --max-seconds 27

Checked against a Waveshare ESP32-C6-LCD-1.47: 203 of 204 console lines identical over three boot cycles, including the ROM's Saved PC: after esp_restart(); the one difference is that line on the first boot. --board waveshare-c6-lcd147 adds that board — the ST7789 over SPI2 and the GDMA, the WS2812, the BOOT button — and the C6's 802.15.4 MAC answers energy scans, so the board's LVGL spectrum-scanner firmware runs end to end (examples/waveshare-c6-lcd147/). The MAC also sends and receives frames with the timing of the air, and --cooja makes the C6 an external mote of Cooja-NG, driven in exact lock-step over NDJSON (an unmodified Contiki-NG-on-IDF image exchanging broadcasts with emulated MSP430 nodes). WiFi works too: --wifi attaches the same virtual access point as on the S3 (see WiFi below). See docs/esp32c6.md.

WiFi (ESP32-S3, C3, C6 and the classic ESP32)

--wifi ssid=NAME[,psk=PASS,chan=N,bssid=..] attaches a virtual access point that the unmodified Espressif WiFi blob associates with — scan, authentication, association and, with a passphrase, the WPA2-PSK four-way handshake — and a virtual network behind it (DHCP, ARP, ICMP, DNS, SNTP off the host clock). --net nat (the default) relays the guest's TCP and UDP over ordinary host sockets, so firmware reaches the real network; --net none refuses it. No firmware changes, no root, no tun device.

./target/release/esp32sim --board waveshare-lcd4b --boot rom --flash-mb 16 --psram-mb 8 \ --bootloader $P/bootloader/bootloader.bin --ptable $P/partition_table/partition-table.bin \ --app $P/energy_panel.bin --console usb --wifi "ssid=home,psk=secret" --max-seconds 45

runs the esp32-screen energy panel: it joins, takes a lease, syncs its clock, fetches two days of electricity prices over HTTPS and polls a real Home Assistant on the LAN. docs/networking-howto.md is the how-to (flags, debugging, limits); docs/wifi-plan.md and docs/networking-plan.md describe how the MAC model and the packet path work, and the Networking section of docs/architecture.md the layers every chip shares.

The ESP32-C6 joins the same access point and the same network, through its own MAC model (docs/wifi-c6-plan.md): one station, legacy rates, no power save. examples/c6-wifi-station is the specimen — scan, join, lease, five pings, and the state on the board's screen — and every C6 radio run needs --stub bb_init=0 (the PHY's baseband calibration wants analog hardware):

B=examples/c6-wifi-station/build-emu ./target/release/esp32sim-c6 --boot rom --flash-mb 4 --board waveshare-c6-lcd147 --console usb \ --bootloader $B/bootloader/bootloader.bin --ptable $B/partition_table/partition-table.bin \ --app $B/c6_wifi_station.bin --elf $B/c6_wifi_station.elf --stub bb_init=0 \ --wifi ssid=esp32sim,psk=esp32sim-pass --max-seconds 14

The ESP32-C3 joins the same access point too, through a MAC model derived from the S3's. The same station example, built for each chip without the display, is committed under web/wasm/fw/public/ as the input of the WiFi goldens (wifi_station_{s3,c3,c6} in cli/tests/goldens.rs), so it runs without a local build:

P=web/wasm/fw/public ./target/release/esp32sim-c3 --boot rom --flash-mb 4 --console usb \ --bootloader $P/c3-wifi-bootloader.bin --ptable $P/c3-wifi-ptable.bin --app $P/c3-wifi_station.bin \ --wifi ssid=esp32sim,psk=esp32sim-pass --max-seconds 14

examples/c6-radio-dashboard runs WiFi and 802.15.4 energy scans in one firmware, a page each.

Provenance

Written from the ESP‑IDF register headers, the Xtensa core config shipped with ESP‑IDF, the RISC-V specification and the C3 and C6 technical reference manuals, and observed firmware behaviour. QEMU was consulted only to confirm instruction semantics (no code copied). MIT.

Differential testing against real silicon (hw/)

Every chip is checked against hardware, by different methods: the S3 in JTAG lock-step (below), the C3 and C6 by diffing a captured console against the emulator running the same binaries (hw/c3-hello-world-real.txt, 205/208 lines identical — see docs/esp32c3.md; hw/c6-hello-world-real.txt, 203/204 — see docs/esp32c6.md). Those comparisons found five emulator bugs each, including a device-command race and a cycle counter that did not restart at reset — neither of which a board-free test would have caught.

ESP32-S3, JTAG lock-step

DIFF_DIR=hw/<board> FLASH_MB=8 hw/difftest.sh 3000 — the scripts read efuses/strap from the attached chip over JTAG, then step it and the emulator in lock-step on the same flash dump (flash-8M.bin if present). Atech board, 2026-08-25: 3000 steps from reset, 0 divergences.

Any ESP32‑S3 board on USB works (its built‑in USB‑Serial/JTAG carries both the console and JTAG). The flow, all scripted:

# one-time: dump the board's bootloader/partition table/app start (esptool) into hw/flash-0-1M.bin hw/difftest.sh 8000 # reset → single-step 8000 instructions on the chip and in the emulator, diff hw/difftest-at.sh 403c8948 6000 # run both to a PC (here the 2nd-stage bootloader entry), then step 6000 and diff

difftest*.sh read the chip's efuses (hw/efuse.txt), strapping pins and the peripheral reset state (hw/reset-regs.txt, dumped over JTAG at reset halt) and start the emulator from the same image and state. hw/compare.py diffs pc a0..a15 ps windowbase per instruction, masks PS.INTLEVEL (forced during single‑step), hides window‑exception handlers (the debugger steps over them atomically) and resynchronises across CCOUNT‑timed delay loops, which iterate a different number of times when each step takes milliseconds.

Result so far: the ROM reset path (8000 steps) and 3000+ steps of the IDF 5.5 bootloader run with zero PC divergence; remaining register differences are RTC‑domain values that depend on the previous boot.

Performance / real time (ESP32-S3)

Blocks of instructions are decoded once and executed from a cache, then compiled to native AArch64 code (--no-jit falls back to the interpreter and must give identical results); idle cores (waiti) are skipped, device time is delivered lazily against computed timer deadlines, and interrupt lines are recomputed only when a source changes. See docs/speed-plan.md for the measurements behind each step. The Pocket Synth firmware runs at real time with margin while idle or playing notes; the one place it cannot keep up is a full display redraw, where core 1 runs 100 % busy bit‑banging SPI (4.8 M instructions per 20 ms — 240 Minsn/s needed, ~70 Minsn/s achieved on an M‑series MacBook). Each redraw therefore costs ~0.3 s of lag, which the UI's adaptive audio buffer absorbs (it grows on underrun, up to 400 ms) and the pacer recovers afterwards; if the emulator ever falls more than 0.5 s behind it resynchronises instead of bursting. The header shows real time, ⚠ N s behind and the resync count.

ESP_EMU_RT_LOG=1 prints every 20 ms window that took > 40 ms wall with both cores' instruction counts and PCs. hw/wsdrive.py [port] [seconds] drives the UI protocol without a browser (button presses + knob turns) and reports push gaps, lag and audio delivered — use it to measure changes to the scheduler.

The key is in the inittab file, where we can see the it triggers the execution of /sbin/login.sh script once the user press “Enter” (the BusyBox askfirst action shows the prompt with sentence “Please press Enter to activate this console”). The script is simple as:

#!/bin/sh DEBUG_LOG="`nvram_get debug_log`" if [ "$DEBUG_LOG" = "1" -o -f "/mnt/enable_console" ];then echo "enter debug mode" > /dev/console /bin/login fi

The script checks if debug mode is enabled by checking the NVRAM variable debug_log and based on that allows the access to the shell (well, to login in the shell) or exits, entering in the loop.

We did not find a straight way to enable that variable and bypass the lockdown. There are various ways on changing the boot mode to enable “debug”, but all of them are far away from our hands because:

One of them is based in the presence of a file in the filesystem. Because to be able to drop a file we would need access, it is a chicken-egg situation.

The other method involves a convoluted magic protocol based on ARP and commands signed by a private key which is not present in the firmware. Yes, they use encapsulate their own protocol on ARP and exchange magic signed packets to enable debug remotely. I am not sure what crack they smoked.

It occurs to me at this point that if we can not bypass the check for whether it is booting in production or debug mode, why not completely obliterate the check and jump straight to a shell?

The firmware did not contain anything encrypted, so should be straight forward to modify it to allow us direct access through the serial port. The problem was… Do you remember when I mentioned that it was “barely damaged”?. Well, we fucked 64kbs at 0x500000 which is the load address for the image.

At first, I thought our clumsy hands had ruined the project and that the idea of ​​using the actual device for dynamic analysis with a debugger was slipping away (we would still have the option of trying to emulate some binaries, but having access to the original hardware is always infinitely easier). Fortunately, after analyzing the firmware layout a bit more, we discovered a much smaller “recovery image” that was still intact at 0xe70000. So, because the “normal” image was fucked, the bootloader would have to load this recovery image that we can manipulate to remove the UART lockdown.

0x03 Filesystem gardening for debugging purposes

We finally have a shell; the only problem is that the recovery image is very limited and the file system is not mounted, so the binaries and libraries needed to properly debug the system are missing. Furthermore, the kernel lacks certain modules-such as those responsible for handling WiFi interfaces or the I2C interface used to generate the WiFi key.

The set of commands available in the default image is quite limited, but at least there are tools like wget, which allows us to transfer files from the laptop to the device. I prepared a crappy setup using an old TP-Link router that I got from Telefónica in 2015.

Not glamorous setup

I compiled a new BusyBox with loads of extra commands that were not present in the original one. Using this new BusyBox I started to try to create a “staged” filesystem and bind all the paths to this “staged” filesystem. I had to execute services needed to run before the big ELF in charge of main services (homeapp_security, the same binary that contained the WiFi logic), also because of memory limitations I had to modify some kernel parameters like overcommit_ratio. It was a three hours process of hit and miss until I could run the main binary and be able to interact with it.

Because this was a process that I would need to repeat each time I wanted to use the device (because the filesystem in the recovery image is not persistent, so after each boot I have to restore all) I shared the logs of picocom to Codex and asked it to prepare a all-in-one script that would repeat the same steps I did (and discard the stuff that didn’t work). To be honest, the following morning after that night of playing with the Homebase I barely remembered what I did, so having the logs was marvelous to rebuild and automatize the process. Claude optimizes quantum circuits via closed-loop evaluation
Why: You can apply this autonomous architectural search to your optimization pipelines.

Quantum computers will need carefully designed circuits to run useful algorithms on limited hardware. We gave Claude plain-language specifications for eight such circuits, drawn from chemistry, optimization, physics simulation, and number theory, along with an automated evaluator that checks and scores each design. Working largely on its own, Claude found designs that improve on the best published circuits, in some cases by an order of magnitude or more. Such improvements substantially reduce the quantum resources that useful applications require, so practical quantum computers could arrive sooner than previously anticipated.

This work was led by Andi Gu as part of Anthropic’s STEM Fellows Program, with mentorship from Alek Kemeny, a quantum physicist at Anthropic, in collaboration with Susanne Yelin (Harvard University) and Mikhail Lukin (Harvard University and QuEra Computing). Read the full paper on arXiv.

Unlike classical computers, which store information in bits whose value is either 0 or 1, quantum computers store information in qubits, which can be built from single atoms, superconducting circuits, or particles of light. These qubits make use of quantum phenomena such as superposition—which allows a quantum system to exist in a combination of states at once—in order to process information in ways that could make certain problems in fields like chemistry, materials science, and number theory tractable, even when they are too hard for traditional computers.

The quantum computers the field has built to date only have hundreds to thousands of qubits—too few to run many of the algorithms that promise practical advantages over classical computers. And these qubits are highly sensitive to environmental noise, such as heat, vibration, and even cosmic rays. A tiny disturbance can nudge a qubit’s state, and these small errors pile up until the computer gives the wrong answer.

Given that both qubits and computing time are scarce resources, researchers have been working to redesign quantum algorithms to use fewer qubits and complete computations in less time. An illustrative example is simulating FeMoco, the iron–sulfur cluster at the heart of nitrogenase, the enzyme that bacteria use to turn nitrogen from the air into ammonia. Understanding how it works could help scientists design better industrial catalysts—the substances manufacturers use to speed up chemical reactions.

In 2017, researchers estimated that a quantum computer would need about 50 trillion Toffoli gates to compute FeMoco’s ground-state energy—the energy of its most stable configuration, which chemists need in order to predict how FeMoco reacts. (Similar to ordinary computers, quantum computers work by applying a long sequence of simple operations, called gates, to their qubits. The Toffoli gate is one of the most expensive gates to run, so counting Toffoli gates is a standard way to estimate how long a computation will take.)

Through a series of algorithmic advances, the estimated number of gates has come down to about 340 million—a roughly 150,000-fold reduction, corresponding to a few hours of computation on a future machine with about three million physical qubits.1

These reductions largely came from redesigning the circuits that carry out the computation, rather than from making better hardware. Claude has proven particularly adept at optimizing classical algorithms, so we wanted to see whether it could do the same for quantum algorithms if it were given only a plain-language description of what each circuit must compute.

Claude is unusually well suited to this task, for several reasons. First, quantum algorithms are implemented as quantum circuits. These are composed of sequences of gates, like the Toffoli gates mentioned above. Importantly, these circuits can be described in code, so we can make use of Claude’s coding capabilities to write, run, and revise circuit designs as ordinary programs. Second, the (fairly common) class of quantum algorithms we were most interested in optimizing can be tested efficiently for correctness, as each input gives one definite output that an ordinary computer can check. This means we could have an external evaluator program validate Claude’s proposals. Finally, we can give Claude a clear, objective score to improve: the number of qubits used, multiplied by the number of gates, which serves as a proxy for how long the circuit would take to run.

We found that Claude Mythos 5 could successfully work in a closed loop, proposing a circuit design, receiving feedback and a score from the evaluator program, and using that feedback to improve its next attempt, with no human input between rounds (see Figure 1). Across eight computational primitives—the basic routines that larger quantum algorithms are built from—for chemistry, optimization, quantum simulation, number theory, and numerical computing, Claude discovered novel designs that used both fewer qubits and fewer gates than the best previously published algorithms (Figure 2).

In each case, the improvement resulted from uncovering new structural and mathematical insights about the problem, rather than from fine-tuning existing designs. For example, when compiling a circuit for simulating an iron-sulfide molecule, Claude noticed that the molecule’s symmetries and repeating structure meant that some results could be calculated once and reused, rather than recomputed for every part of the system. In other cases, it found that existing circuits included costly components that were needed to handle only extremely rare inputs, and it redesigned them without those components.

Below, we describe two of Claude’s discoveries. The first is a new way to perform modular inversion, a bit of arithmetic that is the most expensive step in elliptic-curve calculations. The second makes the costliest step of a leading quantum optimization algorithm about 12x cheaper.

These results suggest that AI can now help redesign quantum circuits, a key step in making quantum algorithms faster and easier to run. Faster circuit design could bring the problems quantum computers are meant to solve—from designing new medicines and better batteries to finding cleaner ways of making fertilizer—within reach sooner.

A new reversible algorithm

Designing a quantum circuit involves two constraints that ordinary programming doesn’t have. The first is that a quantum circuit can’t make “if-then” decisions the way an ordinary program can. An ordinary program can check a value and then run only one of two pieces of code. But a quantum computer runs on a superposition of many inputs at once, some of which would run the first piece of code and some of which would run the second, so the circuit has to include the operations for both every time it runs.

The second is that a quantum circuit can’t discard information. An ordinary program can overwrite or delete its scratch work once it’s completed it; in a quantum computer, such intermediate information can’t simply be erased, because every step of a quantum circuit must be reversible. Leaving that information in place isn’t an option, either, because leftover scratch work would corrupt the final result. The only way to discard information is to “uncompute” it by running the computation that created it in reverse, at the cost of additional gates. Both of these constraints make quantum circuits far more expensive, in both runtime and qubits, than their classical counterparts.

This constraint forces a difficult trade-off between space (i.e., qubits) and time. At one extreme, a quantum circuit can uncompute intermediate results as soon as they have been used. This frees up qubits, but if those same results are needed again later, the circuit must recompute them, which adds gates and increases the runtime. At the other extreme, the circuit can retain intermediate results until they’re no longer needed. This avoids repeating work, but it can require many more qubits.

This is what makes quantum circuit design an especially interesting test of Claude’s capabilities. The circuits can be written as ordinary code, but improving them requires the model to reason under a set of constraints quite unlike those of ordinary programming.

A hard first test: modular inversion

The first task we gave Claude was point addition, a rule for combining two points on a mathematical curve (an elliptic curve) to produce a third. Point addition is the core step in quantum algorithms for the discrete logarithm problem on elliptic curves: given a starting point and the result of adding it to itself n times, find n. For large curves, ordinary computers can’t solve this problem in any reasonable amount of time, but a sufficiently large quantum computer could.

These algorithms perform point addition many times over, including in reverse, and these additions account for most of their qubit and runtime costs. Problems like this—easy to compute, extremely hard to reverse—were among the first where quantum computers were shown to offer an exponential speedup over ordinary ones. For these reasons, point addition has become one of the field’s standard benchmarks. This made it a demanding test for Claude. So, we asked it to map out how few qubits, as well as how few gates, point addition really needs.

We gave Claude a one-paragraph brief. It explained the mathematical rule for point addition, and how the evaluator would check each proposed circuit for correctness and score it on the number of qubits and gates it used. It also instructed Claude to explore several approaches in parallel. After working for 53 hours with minimal human input (limited to the initial brief and occasional prompts to think more broadly), Claude arrived at a completely new approach to the problem.

The memory demands of divsteps

Most of the cost of point addition comes from a single step, called modular inversion. Elliptic-curve arithmetic uses numbers that wrap back around when they reach a fixed limit. Modular inversion is the wraparound version of division: given an integer a, find x such that ax ≡ 1 (mod p). Ordinary computers can perform this operation efficiently using a method called divsteps. Divsteps starts with a pair of numbers and repeatedly simplifies them over many rounds of iteration. In each round, divsteps examines the current pair of numbers and conditionally swaps, adds, subtracts, or halves them. Along the way, it preserves just enough information to produce the modular inverse at the end.

Despite its success on ordinary computers, divsteps had not yet produced a practical quantum circuit, because of the reversibility constraint described above. The record of which operation was chosen at each iteration, called branch information, is the kind of scratch work that a traditional program can simply discard. In contrast, a quantum circuit has to retain that record so the computation can be run backward. A straightforward quantum version of divsteps is therefore forced to accumulate a long history of branch information, which requires an enormous number of additional qubits to store.

How Claude got around the memory problem

Claude recognized that this divsteps approach could be promising for quantum circuits, but only if there were a way around needing to store the full history. It had an idea: perhaps the record didn’t need to be stored at all. Claude reasoned that it might be possible to directly infer (or “decode”) the branch information by carefully inspecting the pair of numbers after each iteration. In essence, the numbers themselves would store the record. The circuit could then be run backward without needing any extra qubits.

To test this idea, Claude explored a number of simple candidate rules for performing the decoding. These early rules worked on small test cases, but they failed when Claude tested them at the full problem size, on 256-bit numbers.

Claude then tried a more theoretical approach. Rather than guessing and testing rules, it set out to understand mathematically why divsteps behaves the way it does. It proved a series of results about the iteration process, including several new algebraic identities. From these identities, Claude derived a precise formula for the decoding rule that would correctly recover the branch information from the numerical pairs. It then checked the formula, first by running it through large-scale numerical simulations, and then formalizing the claim in Lean. With the full validation complete, Claude produced the first reversible implementation of divsteps with no stored branch history.

A family of designs

Decoding saves qubits, but it costs time. So, as a further refinement, Claude looked for situations in which it would be cheaper to store the branch information than to decode it. By varying the threshold at which the circuit stores rather than decodes the branch information, Claude produced a new family of designs between the two extremes. Compared with the smallest previous design, one circuit in the family uses 12% more qubits but 17x fewer gates. More generally, the new designs expand the Pareto frontier, offering combinations of qubit count and runtime that weren’t available before (Table 1).

Optimizing a quantum optimizer

Optimization problems are at the heart of a wide range of tasks, including scheduling, logistics, and materials design. An optimization problem presents a set of competing constraints and asks for the solution that satisfies as many of them as possible. Many quantum algorithms have been proposed for problems like these, but most promise only modest or uncertain gains over ordinary computers. Decoded quantum interferometry (DQI) is one of the strongest candidates for offering a superpolynomial quantum speedup—an advantage that rapidly widens as the problems get larger.

When a quantum computer finishes a computation, reading out its qubits produces just one answer, selected at random from the possibilities they were holding in superposition. DQI works by preparing a quantum state in which good solutions are more likely to be read out than bad ones. Doing this involves using an error-correcting decoder, which recovers a short list of “errors” from a compact summary. This is the most expensive step in the algorithm. So, if we could make a faster decoder, we could also perform DQI faster.

A more efficient quantum decoder

The researchers who introduced DQI demonstrated it on a problem called optimal polynomial intersection (OPI), a grid-based version of curve fitting (Figure 3). Imagine a grid in which each position x contains several acceptable values of y. The goal is to find a simple curve (a low-degree polynomial) that passes through an acceptable square for as many x positions as possible. DQI tackles this optimization problem using a specific type of decoder, called a Reed–Solomon decoder.

Figure 3: Optimal Polynomial Intersection (OPI) as discrete curve fitting. Each column contains several acceptable values. The optimization problem is to find a polynomial that passes through as many of the acceptable values as possible.

Reed–Solomon decoding starts from the compact summary of errors mentioned above, which is known as a syndrome. The decoder’s job is to infer, from this summary, where in the data the errors occurred and what they were. The previous state-of-the-art quantum circuit processed these syndromes using the extended Euclidean algorithm, a sequence of divisions and swaps in which the number and order of steps depend on the particular input it’s given. Here, the constraints of quantum circuits reemerge: while an ordinary program can follow a different path for every input, a quantum circuit, working on many inputs at once, has to handle all of them with a single fixed sequence of gates. To manage this, the previous design had to stretch the computation over many more rounds of iteration than any single input would need, and had to run every operation in every round, even when doing so was unnecessary.

Improving this decoder could substantially reduce DQI’s runtime. Estimates by researchers at Google Quantum AI imply that solving one particular OPI problem would take about three months on Frontier, one of the world’s most powerful supercomputers, compared with roughly an hour on a future quantum computer running DQI. Most of that hour would be spent on Reed–Solomon decoding.

So, we asked Claude to design a more efficient quantum circuit for the decoding step. Claude quickly concluded that the extended Euclidean algorithm was a poor fit for a quantum circuit. Instead, it organized its circuit around a different decoding method, known as Berlekamp–Massey decoding, which reads through syndromes (summaries of errors) one at a time and gradually works out the simplest rule that predicts each number in the list based on the ones that came before it. Once that rule is found, it reveals the errors. Unlike the extended Euclidean algorithm, Berlekamp–Massey performs the same kind of step in every round and always runs the same number of rounds, which suits a quantum circuit’s single fixed sequence of gates, ultimately giving Claude a much more efficient foundation to build on.

A simplified approach to storage

Switching to Berlekamp–Massey removed the need for the circuit to take different paths for different inputs. But making it reversible introduced another challenge, this time as a result of the uncomputing constraint discussed above. In every round, the circuit calculates temporary values that it needs only briefly, but this information can’t simply be deleted: to uncompute it later, the circuit has to be able to recover that information.

Storing this information would have been prohibitively expensive. So Claude took the same kind of approach as it had for divsteps: it found a way to reconstruct most of this information from the circuit’s current state, so it didn’t need to be stored. However, a few exceptional values couldn’t be recovered in this way and had to be saved.

Setting aside enough memory for the worst possible case, in which an exceptional value arises in 35 different rounds, would take 665 qubits. Instead, Claude wrote a high-speed simulator and ran 403 million sample decodings to measure how often these exceptions actually occurred. Almost every run needed to save one value at most. So Claude’s circuit sets aside room for just one saved value, or 19 qubits. About one run in 10,000 needs more than that and fails, which is well within the error rate the task allows. These improvements replaced large, worst-case reserves with a small, fixed number of qubits and gates (Figure 4).

Together, these optimizations reduced the number of gates in the decoder by a factor of 12 relative to Google’s previous construction. The improvement came from redesigning the computation at three levels: a different method (replacing the extended Euclidean algorithm with Berlekamp–Massey decoding), a different circuit architecture (reconstructing temporary values instead of storing them), and a different resource budget (measuring precisely how much memory and computation the circuit really needed). No single change would have been enough on its own, but together, they make the most expensive step of DQI just a little over an order of magnitude cheaper.

(b) A design based on Berlekamp–Massey needs far fewer gates, but a naive reversible version has to store a 12-qubit record in every round, adding up to 840 qubits. What we learned

Scientific progress often comes from making theoretical ideas practical. One of the main ways this happens is by making a process that was once thought to be impractical fast and cheap enough to carry out. Quantum circuit design is a prime example: the 150,000-fold drop in the FeMoco estimate described above took many research groups eight years, with each step typically representing months of work. By contrast, Claude’s elliptic-curve design was the result of a single 53-hour run.

Our results suggest that AI can support the work of circuit design, producing implementations that bring useful quantum computations closer to the capabilities of real hardware. If AI can speed up that work, useful quantum computing could arrive sooner.

Despite Claude’s encouraging performance on these tasks, we also observed some limitations. In our early experiments, Claude was conservative by nature. It repeatedly second-guessed apparent improvements, and sometimes continued to double- and even triple-check results that were already well supported. This caution was sometimes valuable: for example, it prompted Claude to write Lean proofs to verify its divsteps design. But it also manifested in a tendency to prefer existing, well-known approaches to more novel ones.

We took two steps to mitigate this. First, our setup required Claude to propose several qualitatively different approaches, and to develop them in parallel. Second, we also occasionally prompted Claude to broaden its search. These interventions succeeded in getting Claude to pursue more ambitious ideas. The elliptic-curve result, for example, didn’t come from shaving gates off of a pre-existing design but from exploring a method of doing reversible modular inversion that hadn’t been possible previously, and then deriving the mathematical results needed to make it practical. Once it had come up with the idea, Claude developed the approach, implemented it, and verified its correctness autonomously.

Circuit design is only one part of building a useful quantum computer. Because qubits are so fragile, the machine must also catch and fix its own errors as it runs, and that depends on many other components, ranging from error-correcting codes to fast decoders to control of the hardware itself. These components all affect one another, but to manage complexity, each is usually designed separately. Many of them share the characteristics that made circuit design a good fit for Claude: a design can be written as code, checked for correctness, and scored by its cost.

This raises an intriguing possibility. Rather than designing each component on its own, AI could optimize all of them jointly, from the algorithm down to the hardware, to build an entire error-corrected quantum computer tailored to a specific problem. Such designs could also tell hardware builders which capabilities matter most for a given application. That could put problems such as designing catalysts and understanding new materials within reach of the first fault-tolerant machines.
