OpenCode Leads Cross-Model Agent Harness Performance
Why: Use this framework to tune your agent architecture and select the best tool integration.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 05:00 · 275 words

An analysis of 52,168 trajectories across four harnesses reveals that execution profiles vary significantly by model. OpenCode ranked first in cross-model performance, while OpenCode also led in cross-model fit.

Read the extracted text ↓

Authors:Shao Zhang, Hangfan Zhang, Yuanyuan Shi, Hao Li, Min-Le Su, Kangcong Li, Jingping Wang, Chunjiang Mu, Chen Zhang, Yang Chen, Bo Zhang, Shuyue Hu

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:LLM agents combine models with execution harnesses, so agentic benchmark performance reflects their joint behavior. We argue that harnesses should therefore be evaluated through their interactions with underlying models. We develop a framework combining five complementary measures: (i) Valid Completion Rate (VCR), how reliably agents finish normally and deliver evaluable results, irrespective of task correctness; (ii) Model-Harness Fit, how well a harness supports task achievement through valid completion for a given model; (iii) Cross-Model Fit, a harness's overall suitability across evaluated models; (iv) Interaction Residuals, pairing-specific execution strengths and weaknesses hidden by component-level summaries; and (v) Token Load, the token demands of agent execution, including failed attempts. We evaluate four general-purpose harnesses (OpenCode, Codex CLI, Pi CLI, and DeepAgents) with four core models, plus two fixed native model-harness systems, on six benchmarks covering context management, tool use, long-context planning, multimodal interaction, and artifact handoff. Including native references and stress conditions, 111 evaluation settings yield 52,168 trajectories. Harness rankings vary across models and benchmarks; task performance and valid completion can favor different pairings, and similar task scores can accompany distinct execution profiles. OpenCode ranks first in cross-model performance, while DeepSeek V4 Flash Preview maintains consistently high Mean VCR across harnesses on the shared four-benchmark panel. These findings support evaluating model-harness interactions, training transferable harness-use capabilities in models, and harness designs adapted to the model and task.

Self-Summarization Preserves Constraints Better Than Aggressive Compaction
Why: Your agents silently drop constraints during compaction and you need to know how bad it is.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 05:00 · 302 words

An evaluation of OpenCode on long-horizon benchmarks reveals that compaction silently drops task constraints, with one-third of anti-cheating rules lost in ProgramBench. While self-summarization consistently outperformed aggressive methods, user instructions dropped six times more often than build specifications across tested models.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Long horizon agentic tasks are now pushing LLM agents past their context window. To continue the task, the agent scaffold has to free up the context window, a process known as context compaction. Compaction is generally lossy by design: agents can silently lose task constraints, instructions, or their reasoning behind earlier decisions. Although compaction is a standard component of coding agent scaffolds, it has not been studied systematically with frontier models on realistic, long horizon agentic tasks. Previous work evaluate smaller open weight models on toy or synthetic trajectories. We fix the agent scaffold (OpenCode), implement five compaction strategies inside it, and evaluate three frontier models on three long horizon benchmarks (PaperBench, ProgramBench, PostTrainBench). For the two 1M context models, the compaction threshold does not measurably affect the task performance, whereas GPT-5.3-Codex, the model with the smallest context window, performs worse at lower thresholds on PaperBench and, less clearly, on PostTrainBench. No compaction method outperforms default self-summarization, and methods that discard more content cost some models up to 20 points while leaving other models the same. Compaction can, however, silently remove task constraints: on ProgramBench, the first compaction summary already drops about one third of the task's anticheating rules on average, and this rises monotonically with the number of compactions. On real user-agent sessions from SWE-chat, instructions about how the agent should work and what it should not do are dropped around 6 times more than specifications of what to build. We argue that current benchmarks are compaction-friendly: i.e., the objective is fixed and the agent's progress stays on disk, so most of what compaction discards can be recovered, except constraints that don't exist anywhere else.

Self-Directed Repair Persists Agent Lessons via Low-Rank Adapters
Why: You get durable state for long-horizon learning without expensive retraining.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 05:00 · 262 words

A new mechanism distills agent reflections into lightweight adapters stored in a persistent library, improving success rates by 8.9–17.0 percentage points on GAIA and Terminal-Bench 2.1. Unlike temporary in-context memory or full fine-tuning, the approach routes relevant corrections to future tasks without overwriting base weights.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Intelligent agents should learn from their mistakes so that experience leads to continuous improvement. Yet today's agents recover from a mistake only temporarily: in-context reflections are easily forgotten across sessions and compete for limited context, while full-parameter fine-tuning is expensive and can interfere with existing capabilities. We introduce Self-Direction, a mechanism for agents to learn from mistakes that combines the benefits of temporary in-context retries and policy updates. When an agent fails a task, it reflects on its own trajectory in order to extract a lesson and immediately distills it into a lightweight low-rank adapter. By keeping the original weights frozen, Self-Direction prevents adapter training from overwriting base-model parameters while training additional parameters equivalent to ~0.02% of the base model's parameter count for each adapter. The agent then saves these adapters in a persistent library of lessons, automatically routing them to solve related problems later. Experiments with agents based on open-weight models from the Qwen family across open-ended research (GAIA), tool-use workflows (Claw-Eval), and terminal tasks (Terminal-Bench 2.1) show that our method improves overall success rates by 8.9 to 17.0 percentage points and performs competitively with frontier systems. We find that Self-Direction reduces repeated mistakes across future encounters of a task, nearly doubling the reliability of an agent's corrections. Learned lessons also transfer: a handful of adapters generalize to dozens of new tasks without additional training. Ultimately, this self-directed repair transforms everyday mistakes into lasting lessons.

Message passing optimizes post-training quantization over discrete codebooks
Why: Your deployments incur lower accuracy loss at extreme bit-widths using this technique.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 05:00 · 201 words

MPQ reformulates row-wise post-training quantization as posterior inference, using vector approximate message passing to preserve soft information before final hard commitment. The method refines solutions via block coordinate descent, demonstrating improved perplexity and zero-shot accuracy on Llama, Qwen, and Mistral at 2-4 bits.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Post-training quantization (PTQ) is a key technique for efficiently deploying large language models, as it compresses weights into low-bit representations without retraining. Existing PTQ solvers, including GPTQ, LDLQ, and block coordinate descent, commit each weight block to a single codeword before its neighbors are considered, leaving near-optimal alternatives unexplored. In this work, we cast row-wise PTQ as posterior inference over discrete codebooks, in which existing solvers correspond to a zero-temperature, hard-decision limit retaining only the top codeword per block. We propose Message-Passing Quantization (MPQ), which preserves soft posterior information across blocks during inference and commits to hard codewords only at the output stage. MPQ first uses vector approximate message passing to compute approximate blockwise posteriors, then refines a hard-decision initialization by exact block coordinate descent. Across 2-bit lattice and 3- and 4-bit scalar quantization on dense language models from Llama, Qwen, and Mistral families, MPQ improves perplexity and zero-shot accuracy over strong hard-decision baselines, and composes favorably with preprocessing techniques such as activation-aware scaling and incoherence processing. Our implementation is available at this https URL.

Tiny interpreters reveal fundamental complexity and foster mastery.
Why: You value minimal core languages and readable primitives for building robust abstractions.
Hacker News ↗HN 189↑extracted 05:00 · 2,418 words

Page started: 2025-06-02

Page published: 2026-07-28

My Implementing a Forth article got some great feedback, particularly around the subject of tiny Forth implementations. (And it was an excuse to list some of the tiniest Forths I’ve seen.)

I know I’m not alone in seeing the appeal of tiny Forths, tiny languages, tiny programs, and just small stuff in general. What’s up with that, anyway?

The appeal of tiny programs

Tiny programs are approachable.

If I see that something is 200 lines of code, I’m much more likely to read it than if it’s 2,000 or 20,000 lines.

What’s more, if it’s a program that does something interesting in 20 lines when I was expecting 2,000, then I’m going to be very, very interested to read those 20 lines!

When I was researching DOM diffing libraries prior to creating RetroV, one of the repos I looked at was ijk by Luke Jackson.

I was pretty used to looking at fat JavaScript libraries at that point, so when I cracked open the source file for ijk, I was really surprised. Here’s the source: index.js (githubusercontent.com).

Just 25 lines! I was instantly intrigued.

More recently, one of the responses to my aforementioned Forth article was Philippe Brochard with a 46 byte "Forth".

46 bytes!

It’s so small, I can just put the hexdump right here:

50b8 8e00 31d8 e8ff 0017 003c 0575 00ea 5000 3c00 7401 eb02 e8ee 0005 0588 eb47 b8e6 0200 d231 14cd e480 7580 c3f4

Sure, very few people jump at a chance to read raw machine code. But that’s exactly my point. Something this small doesn’t feel so scary, right? I mean, 46 bytes. If you put the effort into it, you know you can figure out how it works eventually.

This next one almost serves as a counter-example because the code is so dense and frightening, but I can’t help mentioning it anyway.

One of my favorite software stories is Roger K.W. Hui’s Appendix A "Incunabulum" from An Implementation of J in which he describes Arthur Whitney writing an array-based language interpreter,

"…​on one page and in one afternoon — an interpreter fragment on the AT&T 3B1 computer. I studied this interpreter for about a week for its organization and programming style…​"

Hui can list the entire interpreter in the appendix because it’s just that single page, 122 lines of incredibly cryptic C, which you can view here:

I haven’t personally tackled that puzzle yet, but it’s in my unwritten list of dense computer things that I would print out and take with me if I suddenly found myself cast out of society and forced to go live alone up on a mountain or something.

(By the way, J is in the family of array languages which include APL. If you enjoy brevity, cryptic programming puzzles, and high performance numerical computing, these languages have got you covered!)

Update July 2026: Speaking of Arthur Whitney, Alex (alexalejandre.com) sent me a link that I knew I’d have to add here. Check this out: https://fall.tr/kos

It’s a transcript of a Geo Carncross talk describing an operating system, kOS, featuring Whitney’s k array language, kdb database, and a GUI and window manager in a 90 Kb bootable image. Individual source code stats are just as impressive (such as 60 lines of C for the aforementioned GUI and window manager). And apparently kdb is 100 lines of C? Is that right? Even Whitney C, that’s still impressive. Now I’m even more intrigued by kdb than ever! And if any of that seems interesting, you’ll definitely enjoy the article Impending kOS (vector.org.uk).

Something else that would certainly need to be in the collection I take to that lonely mountain would have to be The Most Beautiful Program Ever Written, according to the title of a talk by William Byrd. Spoiler alert: It’s this Lisp interpreter in Lisp:

(define eval-expr (lambda (expr env) (pmatch expr [`,x (guard (symbol? x)) (env x)] [`(lambda (,x) ,body) (lambda (arg) (eval-expr body (lambda (y) (if (eq? x y) arg (env y)))))] [`(,rator ,rand) ((eval-expr rator env) (eval-expr rand env))])))

Recreation or fundamental truths?

Now, we all know that code golfing (wikipedia.org) is a programmer’s recreational game, and just for fun, right?

But is any of this actually useful?

I say yes. And I’ll go one step further: They are meaningful.

When you see a ray tracer that fits on a business card (fabiensanglard.net), it makes you realize that a ray tracer is something that can fit on a business card!

That’s an actual fact about the minimum complexity of ray tracers.

"…​the length of a shortest computer program (in a predetermined programming language) that produces the object as output."

(I learned about Kolmogorov complexity in a book about Kurt Gödel and it was my favorite thing in what was otherwise a pretty dry and depressing book.)

The meaning I get from this is that code golfers, for the benefit of the whole human race, produce versions of programs that represent the minimum complexity of those programs. Way to go code golfers!

What do we know about the fundamental complexity of the Lisp programming language?

That’s a fact about Lisps with garbage collection. When you strip everything else away, you can get the essentials down to 436 bytes!

I take comfort in this because that means the concept can be boiled down to this hand-held size. And that means I can probably fit it in my head.

Thanks to Frank Force, we know that a 3D browser game can fit in 2Kb of (insanely compressed) JavaScript source: A 3D game in 2Kb of JS (frankforce.com)

And we haven’t even scraped the surface of what the demoscene has to offer for computers going back to the 1980s. Here’s a good place to start: https://demoscene.assembly.org/

Mini-things are fun and useful and meaningful.

My point here is that you don’t have to actually study the intricate clockwork puzzle of a miniaturized/golfed program to benefit from it. The miniature simply serves to prove that the concept can fit on a business card and it can fit in your head. Go ahead and find a readable version to study instead. (The miniature will likely have many things to teach, but some of them will be about how to make miniature programs.)

Small languages

Small programs are interesting. But I’m also interested in small programming languages.

Generally speaking, the smaller the language, the less expressive it is.

One of the most well-known, least expressive languages is assembly.

Assembly languages are very syntactically and conceptually simple. There’s not much language at all: it’s mostly a rigid sequence of opcode mnemonics. Even with macros, writing in assembly is mostly about understanding the puzzle of a particular CPU’s instruction set architecture, or ISA. (You can prove this by comparing the syntax of assembly for CISC and RISC processors. The puzzle is totally different, while the language remains practically unchanged.)

An honorable mention must be made here for SNOBOL, which is kind of like assembly language both for the rigid syntactic reasons listed above and also because it presents a similar control flow challenge. Like a CPU, it only understands jumps and call/return. (In every other way, SNOBOL is wildly different, being a string matching and replacing language. It’s weird and fun and highly effective.)

Moving on to syntactically tiny "high-level" languages, these three are brilliant:

Forths win just about any "smallness" contest. The only syntactical construct is the space character. Forths are mind-bendingly flexible and powerful in a way that is downright disturbing.

Lisps, (especially Scheme?), have small core languages with very simple syntax and enormous expressive power and flexibility.

Tcl deserves to be mentioned with Forth and Lisp. Tcl is wild because everything is a string in a way that is hard to appreciate at first. You can construct your own language in Tcl just as you can with Forth or Lisp.

Forth, Lisp, and Tcl only require you to understand a few core ideas and give you limitless power in return. You could fit the syntactic core of these languages on an index card or less.

And yet, to wield these tools effectively requires a massive shift in thinking if you’re used to "normal" Algol-like procedural languages.

So how about something that is both small and simple and doesn’t require a blood sacrifice?

I think the Lua programming language fits the bill. Check out how small the core language is:

I’ve got the printed book version of this document and it’s a thin book! The Lua language is covered in the beginning and it is just 27 pages. 27 pages! I have game manuals longer than that.

The C language is pretty small. I don’t think it’s super easy to program effectively and safely with C, but there’s no denying it’s compact and pretty expressive for its size.

Newcomer Zig is C-like. But I think it’s actually quite large and you need to know a lot of it to use it effectively. (I’m also a fan of Zig and think it’s totally worth it. The size is there for a reason.)

JavaScript has a core language that is quite small. Probably on par with Lua, actually. Functions, arrays, and objects (used as dictionaries) are pretty much all the JS I use and I like it that way!

As with the natural human languages, the trade-off with a bigger programming language is, hopefully, greater expressivity.

Small or expressive. Which way should you lean?

In a wonderful talk by David Ungar (wikipedia.org) titled Self and Self: Whys and Wherefores, he laments the mistake of Self’s multiple inheritance:

"It’s far better to make a system a little too simple so a person has to write extra stuff, even write it twice with a comment that says, 'Look over here,' than to have a system that acts in strange ways and nobody can understand why. So, simplicity trumps expressiveness."

Got that? Here’s the bullet from the slide to help you remember:

I think small languages can also be fairly expressive, but only with very, very careful design. Recall Forth, Lisp, and Tcl.

Microworlds: The idea of small, self-contained, learnable programming environments also has a David Ungar connection I discovered while watching his talk. See "ARK" on my Microworlds card. (Smith and Ungar co-created the Self language (selflanguage.org), which is a notable milestone in programming and still under active development to this day.)

Languages which come with full-featured interactive IDEs are obvious potential microworlds.

But I also think that traditional text file-based programming can give you the microworld experience as well. For that, a language needs high quality documentation that installs alongside the compiler/interpreter. And it should strive to have error output that can be understood by beginners, including explanations of what went wrong and hints for how to fix it or or where to find more information.

Libraries: Another easily overlooked aspect of a programming language’s apparent size is its "standard library" of functions, data structures, and methods.

I think function libraries are subject to the exact same size versus expressiveness challenge.

For example, learning the Ramda (ramdajs.com) functional programming library for JavaScript was very much like learning a new programming language and I never managed to learn all of it. The composability of Ramda and the consistency of its curried functions was extremely eye-opening to me. My advice: A little bit of Ramda goes a long way!

The feels: Concision in our tools has a certain quality that goes beyond utility. Little things just spark joy, you know?

Why are small things so delightful?

The appeal of tiny things in general

I’ve always had a fascination with miniatures, dioramas, and tiny scale models.

I know I’m not alone!

Stephanie M. Langin-Hooper, author of Figurines in Hellenistic Babylonia writes in Tiny Things and Why We Love Them (cambridgeblog.org):

"…​the act of shrinking a life-size thing to a smaller scale has an influential effect. The object is now cute, more accessible, more delicate, more 'squee'. It is also more helpless, and thus controllable – comforting and completely non-threatening due to its diminutive size.

Which is exactly the reaction I have when I see a tiny bit of code that does something useful.

Also, I’m super okay with "controllable" in the context of a computer, but the feeling of that paragraph changes a bit if we’re talking about, say, dolls.

Small scale helps make "big" things approachable:

"What might be threatening to think about in the real-scale world can be more manageable and more conceivable in miniature."

Small things, "allow low-stakes experimentation," which is why we often build tiny models of things before we scale them up.

Simon Garfield, author of In Miniature: How Small Things Illuminate the World has many of the same answers. He writes in Small world: Why we love tiny things (theguardian.com):

"The answer lies in our desire for mastery and elucidation. The ability to enhance a life by bringing scaled-down order and illumination to an otherwise chaotic world – a world over which we may otherwise feel we have little control – cannot be overvalued. […​] At its simplest, the miniature shows us how to see, learn and appreciate more with less."

I think this is a big part of what I get out of Assembly Nights with the small scale and orderly logic and internal consistency. Unlike the vast, fathomless Real World, an offline computer is a fantasy place where I can believe I could understand everything if I just tried hard enough for long enough.

"The satisfaction of observing small things becomes a desire to make small things, and both stages address the human need for comprehension and order. We live in a huge and doomy universe, and controlling just a tiny scaled-down part of it restores our sense of order and worth."

I’ve been studying computer history for a little while and I’ve only scratched the surface. But one of the things I’ve been finding is that a lot of the "big" stuff in our field are just accumulations of lots of "little" victories as individuals have solved "little" problems in new and different ways.

"We create small universes in which we may bury ourselves to the exclusion of all else. Blocking out real life for a while – always the prerequisite of the dedicated domestic hobby, from doll’s house modeller to jigsaw enthusiast to adult book colourist – may be contemplative, meditative, blinkered and essential. The people crouching over tiny details as if the world depended on it are only doing it because their world does depend on it."

PHP Critical RCE Fixes Address OOM Corruption Vectors
Why: Update your runtime immediately; AI-assisted discovery accelerated the patch cycle for these two CVEs.
Hacker News ↗Hacker NewsSimon WillisonHN 816↑extracted 05:00 · 1,832 words

Earlier today, the PHP project released PHP 8.5.12, 8.4.27, 8.3.36, and 8.2.35. These releases fix two critical vulnerabilities that, under the right conditions, allow an unauthenticated attacker to execute code on servers running PHP. If you run PHP, it is imperative that you update now. The announcement on php.net contains and references technical details on the vulnerabilities, attack vectors, and instructions on how to update on which systems.

This post isn't about how to update. It's about how these vulnerabilities were found and fixed, what has changed in how PHP security is handled over the past two years, and what we need from the organizations that depend on PHP to keep PHP's security work going.

CVE-2026-109618 / GHSA-w8hw-w9fx-j79w: RCE via OOM corruption (out-of-memory): If a request reached PHP's memory_limit at exactly the wrong moment, PHP could leave partially initialized internal data behind and later treat it as valid, corrupting memory. On Apache with mod_php, an unauthenticated request can trigger this before any application code runs, and full remote code execution was demonstrated with default settings. Remote code execution was also confirmed on PHP-FPM through application-level code paths.

CVE-2026-109619 / GHSA-6wmf-4rv5-p3rp: RCE via stale global state: A fatal error at the wrong point could leave internal state pointing to memory that belonged to a previous request. A later request handled by the same worker could then use that freed memory. A use-after-free and worker crash were confirmed, and the reporter demonstrated remote code execution against a real-world application running on Apache with mod_php.

Both issues sit in parts of PHP that nearly every request touches: memory management and error handling. Their central place in PHP is what made both vulnerabilities serious, and also so hard to fix without breaking compatibility, hurting performance, or forcing changes on the large ecosystem of third-party extensions.

A first external audit and increased security focus

In 2024, PHP underwent its first external security audit in years. The PHP Foundation secured funding from the Sovereign Tech Agency to organize this audit through OSTIF, and it was carried out by Quarkslab. While fixing one of the issues Quarkslab reported, a PHP core developer contracted by the PHP Foundation found the first signs of the OOM problem behind CVE-2026-109618. In 2024, a potential exploit for the OOM problem was theorized, but no concrete vectors were found and other concrete issues with clear impact overshadowed these concerns.

To fix the OOM problem, the PHP security team developed several competing approaches, but each came with its own trade-offs: backward compatibility breaks, new denial-of-service potential, or requiring changes to many third-party extensions which seemed impossible to coordinate. The PHP security team, operating on consensus, struggled to choose between these and feared the repercussions of shipping the wrong choice. Combined with the lack of a known path to remote code execution and the small team of core developers with the necessary deep knowledge of the engine occupied with many responsibilities, this decision process caused work on a fix to stall and restart repeatedly over months. We won't pretend otherwise: the delay was a structural failure, caused by both the security team's decision process and its lack of resources. The security team has since started changing its processes, and that work continues, so that decisions can be made by majority and compromises are encouraged.

Building a sustainable professional security organization

Over the last months the way PHP security work is organized has changed considerably. In May 2026, the PHP Foundation created the Ecosystem Security Team, which works alongside the PHP security team on triage, coordination, and hardening. Volker Dusch's work as AI Security Engineer in Residence at the PHP Foundation has significantly improved communication and collaboration between the PHP security team and the Foundation. Alexandre Daubois joined the PHP Foundation in July to help the PHP security team triage, handle, and fix security reports and Daniel Scherzer joined in September to take on hardening work.

Earlier this year, we worked with Alpha-Omega to scan PHP with Anthropic's Claude Mythos. But since the Mythos scan, we have added other tools, including OpenAI's Daybreak Red for validating reports and exploits, alongside sanitizer builds and reproduction environments that match our CI on every platform PHP supports.

The PHP Foundation got the PHP project into the Patch the Planet program, as part of which security firm Calif began an audit of PHP on July 30th, 2026. On August 11th researcher Ryan A at Calif reported an unconditional remote code execution with the same root cause as the OOM problem we had found in 2024. OpenAI independently reported the same root cause on the same day, followed by further attack vectors from OpenAI on September 18th and from Wiz on August 24th. More recently, Trail of Bits has also been auditing PHP. The reports from Calif, OpenAI, and Wiz made the full impact of the OOM problem clear, and from August 11th onward, fixing it became the top priority for all of the PHP Foundation's team working on security. Without the Foundation’s funding it would have been impossible to have multiple people work on this issue full time, eventually through weekends and around the clock to coordinate and communicate with other involved parties.

The growth of AI-assisted research also means the PHP security team receives far more reports than before. As Alexandre Daubois described last week in How the PHP Security Team Handles Your Reports, the team has published 15 CVEs since July 1st and worked through more than 100 reports that turned out to be invalid, duplicates, or not reproducible. AI helps the team validate reports and group reports sharing the same root cause, but a person makes every call and works on every patch.

Leak and disrupted disclosure

The coordinated disclosure of these vulnerabilities worked well until 2 days ahead of the targeted release date.

On Tuesday, October 20th, at around 15:00 UTC, the two CVE IDs began circulating on online forums and social media. They came with a wrong release time and a wrong CVSS score of 10.0 (the actual score is 9.2). No technical details leaked. In response, the PHP project brought its pre-announcement forward and published it at 19:30 UTC. It also moved the release from October 22nd, 14:00 UTC, to October 21st, 14:00 UTC. Distributors were still packaging the fix, so releasing immediately would have left many users without an easy way to update. Moving the release forward by one day was the best compromise between acting quickly, giving people time to prepare, and making sure everyone had a simple update path when the vulnerabilities were disclosed.

As a result of the leak and pre-announcement around 20:00 UTC, Calif, one of the security firms that had reported the issues, mistakenly published a video. It showed their proof of concept working against a number of targets and revealed a few additional, though limited, details about the vulnerabilities. Copies of the deleted video started circulating on social media soon after. The video contributed to accelerated development of various PoCs (exploitation proof of concepts), which then increased circulation on October 21st.

Mitigation advice we started providing to a few critical infrastructure providers around Oct 20th 23:00 UTC, may have leaked as well. The PHP security team closely monitored and inspected appearing PoCs after the leak but did not identify any functional exploits at the time of release/public disclosure.

On October 21st at 07:41 UTC, more than six hours before the coordinated release, Chainguard unilaterally broke the embargo and published built images containing the fix. Like other distributors, Chainguard had received the patch in advance so its users could update the moment the vulnerabilities were disclosed. Any attacker could now study the fix in binary form while every other PHP user was still waiting for the release. This disregarded weeks of work by many parties toward a coordinated disclosure.

A cautiously optimistic outlook

Today's release shows the progress we’re making in how PHP security is handled. Once the full impact of the OOM problem was understood in August, the security team, the foundation, and the release managers delivered a fix for this deeply rooted problem across all supported branches, and distributions and LTS vendors received the patch ahead of time so their users can update quickly.

We are confident that a serious vulnerability with a fix stuck on hard trade-offs would now reach the PHP Foundation leadership much sooner, as we’re changing processes so leadership can help steer the situation to a timely resolution. We are now prioritizing measures to make sure vulnerabilities that need similarly complex fixes get resolved faster when they are reported in the future. The PHP Foundation and the PHP security team learned a lot in the last weeks and will work to turn their experiences into lasting repeatable processes.

What we need from you

PHP runs a large share of the web, including government services, online stores, banks, media sites, and much of the software that businesses rely on every day. Yet security work on PHP itself still depends on a small group of foundation-funded developers and a little volunteer help.

Tools now find bugs faster than ever, for defenders and attackers alike, and the number of reports will keep growing. What tools cannot do is decide on the right fix for a long-standing design decision in the engine, weigh the compatibility trade-offs, and review the patch. Making those decisions takes experienced engineers who know the entire PHP codebase, and today there are too few of them.

A core part of the PHP Foundation's mission is to grow the number of engineers with deep knowledge of the PHP codebase and give the security team the resources it needs. We can only achieve this goal with funding from those who depend on PHP:

Companies that build products and services on PHP: please sponsor the PHP Foundation at a level that reflects how much you rely on it.

Hosting providers, cloud platforms, and distributions that deliver PHP to millions of users.

Governments and public institutions whose services run on PHP. The German Sovereign Tech Agency's funding of the 2024 audit shows what public investment can achieve, and we need more of it, on a sustained basis.

Sponsoring the PHP Foundation is the most direct way to ensure structural issues like the OOM vulnerability are addressed sustainably, before they turn into emergencies. Become a sponsor at thephp.foundation/sponsor, and if your organization would like to talk about a larger commitment, get in touch.

On the foolishness of "natural language programming".

Since the early days of automatic computing we have had people that have felt it as a shortcoming that programming required the care and accuracy that is characteristic for the use of any formal symbolism. They blamed the mechanical slave for its strict obedience with which it carried out its given instructions, even if a moment's thought would have revealed that those instructions contained an obvious mistake. "But a moment is a long time, and thought is a painful process." (A.E.Housman). They eagerly hoped and waited for more sensible machinery that would refuse to embark on such nonsensical activities as a trivial clerical error evoked at the time.

Machine code, with its absence of almost any form of redundancy, was soon identified as a needlessly risky interface between man and machine. Partly in response to this recognition so-called "high-level programming languages" were developed, and, as time went by, we learned to a certain extent how to enhance the protection against silly mistakes. It was a significant improvement that now many a silly mistake did result in an error message instead of in an erroneous answer. (And even this improvement wasn't universally appreciated: some people found error messages they couldn't ignore more annoying than wrong results, and, when judging the relative merits of programming languages, some still seem to equate "the ease of programming" with the ease of making undetected mistakes.) The (abstract) machine corresponding to a programming language remained, however, a faithful slave, i.e. the nonsensible automaton perfectly capable of carrying out nonsensical instructions. Programming remained the use of a formal symbolism and, as such, continued to require the care and accuracy required before.

In order to make machines significantly easier to use, it has been proposed (to try) to design machines that we could instruct in our native tongues. this would, admittedly, make the machines much more complicated, but, it was argued, by letting the machine carry a larger share of the burden, life would become easier for us. It sounds sensible provided you blame the obligation to use a formal symbolism as the source of your difficulties. But is the argument valid? I doubt.

We know in the meantime that the choice of an interface is not just a division of (a fixed amount of) labour, because the work involved in co-operating and communicating across the interface has to be added. We know in the meantime —from sobering experience, I may add— that a change of interface can easily increase at both sides of the fence the amount of work to be done (even drastically so). Hence the increased preference for what are now called "narrow interfaces". Therefore, although changing to communication between machine and man conducted in the latter's native tongue would greatly increase the machine's burden, we have to challenge the assumption that this would simplify man's life.

A short look at the history of mathematics shows how justified this challenge is. Greek mathematics got stuck because it remained a verbal, pictorial activity, Moslem "algebra", after a timid attempt at symbolism, died when it returned to the rhetoric style, and the modern civilized world could only emerge —for better or for worse— when Western Europe could free itself from the fetters of medieval scholasticism —a vain attempt at verbal precision!— thanks to the carefully, or at least consciously designed formal symbolisms that we owe to people like Vieta, Descartes, Leibniz, and (later) Boole.

The virtue of formal texts is that their manipulations, in order to be legitimate, need to satisfy only a few simple rules; they are, when you come to think of it, an amazingly effective tool for ruling out all sorts of nonsense that, when we use our native tongues, are almost impossible to avoid.

Instead of regarding the obligation to use formal symbols as a burden, we should regard the convenience of using them as a privilege: thanks to them, school children can learn to do what in earlier days only genius could achieve. (This was evidently not understood by the author that wrote —in 1977— in the preface of a technical report that "even the standard symbols used for logical connectives have been avoided for the sake of clarity". The occurrence of that sentence suggests that the author's misunderstanding is not confined to him alone.) When all is said and told, the "naturalness" with which we use our native tongues boils down to the ease with which we can use them for making statements the nonsense of which is not obvious.

It may be illuminating to try to imagine what would have happened if, right from the start our native tongue would have been the only vehicle for the input into and the output from our information processing equipment. My considered guess is that history would, in a sense, have repeated itself, and that computer science would consist mainly of the indeed black art how to bootstrap from there to a sufficiently well-defined formal system. We would need all the intellect in the world to get the interface narrow enough to be usable, and, in view of the history of mankind, it may not be overly pessimistic to guess that to do the job well enough would require again a few thousand years.

Remark. As a result of the educational trend away from intellectual discipline, the last decades have shown in the Western world a sharp decline of people's mastery of their own language: many people that by the standards of a previous generation should know better, are no longer able to use their native tongue effectively, even for purposes for which it is pretty adequate. (You have only to look at the indeed alarming amount of on close reading meaningless verbiage in scientific articles, technical reports, government publications etc.) This phenomenon —known as "The New Illiteracy"— should discourage those believers in natural language programming that lack the technical insight needed to predict its failure. (End of remark.)

From one gut feeling I derive much consolation: I suspect that machines to be programmed in our native tongues —be it Dutch, English, American, French, German, or Swahili— are as damned difficult to make as they would be to use.

μRepRap - The RepRapMicron

The µRepRap is intended to be a RepRap (a replicating 3D printer) capable of micron (1/1000th of a millimetre, or 1µm) and sub-micron fabrication, made with only common and easily accessible materials. This degree of accuracy was initially made possible by 3D printed microscope platforms designed by The OpenFlexure Project. Variations more suitable for prototyping and fabrication have since been developed, but kudos to them. Expectations for the initial μRepRap prototype were to demonstrate repeatable positioning to better than 3µm on a work area 10mm across and to produce a probe tip in lieu of a print head that is suitable for manufacture in a simple home workshop. This goal has effectively been achieved.

0.5mm "Benchy" 3D test model printed with μRepRap, 4μm layer height

PIKA Version

"PIKA" V0.02 hardware is producing repeatable positioning of approximately 1μm and reliable 10μm or better accuracy in an approximately 8mm cubic volume. Using a fine probe etched with the PIKA mechanism itself, UV resin droplets as small as 4μm have been deposited. With a fibre optic probe 9μm resin lines have been cured. This is the current recommended build.

PIKA V0.02 Complete Circa May 2026

MAUS Version (Deprecated)

This was devised as a testbed for flexure components, and is only mentioned because it has been thoroughly documented with a bill of materials etc. It was updated to V0.05 to give compatibility with PIKA Axis Drivers, but the full documentation is for the "MAUS C" V0.04 (essentially a debugged V0.03). This achieved approximately 5μm positioning best case over a 4mm square, though motion is not linear. Maus-C-Build-Process instructions have been created. If you want to download the pre-built STL files go here to Printables and day-to-day progress is detailed on the RepRap blog.

MAUS 'L' V0.05 μRepRap, in development Feb 2026

Overview

Microelectronics and Micro-electromechanical Systems (MEMS) are essential components of most of the electronic wizardry we use in our everyday lives, whether we realise it or not. In the way that RepRap brought Open 3D fabrication to the masses, the aim of the µRepRap Project is to bring users the same capabilities on a much smaller scale and allow those components to evolve in the same way. The solution is effectively a very precise and compact CNC machine.

The objective has two related parts: To create a macro scale 3D printed micron fabrication and manipulation system, and while doing this develop the techniques needed to reproduce a similar system printed at the micron scale.

Background

A 1.3mm "Hello World" mechanically etched in Sharpie Marker on glass slide mid-2025

Precision manufacturing began somewhere in the 1700's, and the first micron scale electronic devices were fabricated from silicon in the 1960’s. The Intel 4004, for example, was made with a feature size of 10μm. Ever since then the technology focused largely on silicon, with fabrication systems becoming ever more complex, esoteric, and costly. The techniques used are difficult for the average hobbyist to manage, and in many cases are downright dangerous.

In biology and medicine, equipment to measure and manipulate objects on the micron scale are relatively common - though these devices tend to be large, specialized, and expensive. Recently though, microscope platforms capable of sub-micron resolution were developed by The OpenFlexure Project, and these have created an interesting opportunity for developing micron scale fabrication.

If micron scale manufacturing can be achieved by RepRap-like technology, it is likely that these fields will be advanced in the same way that desktop fabrication was by the RepRap. The biological sciences will gain from inexpensive, rapidly-evolving equipment. The microelectronics field will regain the potential for independent communities develop on the micron scale, and break away from its fixation on silicon as its main platform. As with 3D printing, there will certainly be new developments in fields that do not currently even exist.

There is also the possibility (and intent) that this technology could replicate in the future at a yet smaller scale.

Requirements

Micron scale 3D printing has many of the same requirements that The RepRap Project developed when initially printing on the macro scale:

A 3-axis positioning system

CAD/CAM software

Axis zeroing sensors

A deposition system

Building material

In addition there is the practical aspect that humans are unable to directly manipulate micron scale assemblies and sub-assemblies. Novel systems are therefore needed to:

Detach printed items

Transport items

Rotate and position items

Conduct micron scale maintenance tasks

Operate and interface with items

Similar issues will exist if and when a micron scale system is developed.

Optics

While conventional optics are readily available to allow humans to initiate and inspect the fabrication processes, it is likely that some customisation of the optics will be desirable.

For the moment two USB microscopes are sufficient. One to observe the initial Z probe height during setup, and one for the XY positioning of the probe when commencing a print. A minimum of 8Mpixel resolution is recommended. Additionally a conventional bench microscope of 100x to 200x will be needed to examine the output.

"Hello, World" and a 100μm square set in resin using UV and a 125/9 optical fibre

Additionally, an optical fibre may be attached as the probe. This can be used to cure UV resin by projecting a sufficiently powerful UV source down the probe. This section describes how a 125/9 optical fibre patch lead and 3W UV LED module can be attached to the μRepRap as a print head.

3-Axis Control

The axes are driven by 400-step NEMA17 stepper motors, but 200-step motors will get quite close to 1μm. The driving hardware and firmware is left to user preference, though existing developments assume interactive control using GCODE on a serial connection. Arduino GRBL is adequate.

Probe Print Head

The probe is used as a Z touch sensor, engraving tool, and deposition head. A robust probe for initial testing can be made from a 0.5mm hypodermic needle or 0.25mm accupuncture needle. These are sufficiently robust to use subtractively on resist-covered surfaces such as Sharpie marker on a glass slide.

For fine deposition an electrochemically etched wire point is recommended. Instructions for fabrication with minimal equipment are in the Probe documentation. The PIKA is capable of etching its own probes.

Building Materials

Additive fabrication is currently implemented by transferring minute quantities of "Top Coat" UV nail gel resin with the probe. The reservoir is a smeared layer of resin. The probe accepts this resin by capillary action. When depositing, capillary action forms relatively consistent droplets. The number of consecutively deposited droplets and their consistency depend on the qualities of the resin and probe, but sub-15μm droplets are achievable.

Other resins and ionic gels may also be used. A UV LED in the print bed operated by the hardware controller sets the resin.

Note: Thin layers of 3D printer UV resin do not readily set in air as oxygen neutralises the activated photoinitiator.

Other substances may be transferred. They key limitation is that their solvent should not evaporate during deposition.

Materials that readily adhere to the resin (which may be partially cured first) such as gold leaf may be added.

Summary

The current μRepRap 3D printer is intended to provide a platform for further hardware and software development on the micron scale.

Much work remains to be done in determining what can be printed and in what materials. If objects cannot be printed high enough, or with overhangs, folded structures, hinges, and flexures will need to be developed to produce complex 3D shapes and mechanisms.

It is hoped that μRepRap may be used to eventually construct useful devices in its own right, and an ecosystem of fabrication utilities that operate on a yet smaller scale.
