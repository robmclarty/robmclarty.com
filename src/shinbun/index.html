Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Modern language models (LMs) are post-trained and deployed as agents inside a "harness", but standard harness designs accumulate context that the neural network is forced to generalize to, making training for long-horizon tasks expensive and time-consuming. We study an alternative emerging path: training inherently neurosymbolic LM harnesses, which externalize and manage context programmatically to compose existing LM capabilities. We hypothesize that training around these harnesses can offer a better return on compute by enabling compositional generalization, as they can learn to decompose complex problems so that every observation their neural LM receives is locally in-distribution, even when the full task is novel. We find that harnesses like the Recursive Language Model (RLM) learn such decompositions readily under reinforcement learning post-training: (1) we demonstrate that training only on short tasks can generalize to tasks 2-64$\times$ longer while the base neural LM improves less, and (2) training on one domain transfers to novel domains that share latent structure. On messier, complex tasks, we find that we can target individual LLM calls within a harness by generating and training on easier, synthetically generated surrogate environments with fake tool / sub-calls, a technique we call surrogate harness training. From this finding, we train RLM-27B on synthetic data, and show it improves Qwen3.8-27B in the same RLM harness on a hard suite of long-context tasks like CTC-Bench and ExpertLongBench by 26.9% on average while using $\sim$2$\times$ fewer tokens at inference time. Finally, we show that harnesses can compose over these newly learned decompositions using recursive sub-harness calls.

Read the extracted text ↓

Authors:Yuzhou Chen, Licheng Pan, Zhixuan Chu, Yunsheng Lu, Yichen Sun, Han Zheng, Yan Wang, Bingyu Zhu, Haiwen Hong, Longtao Huang, Kui Ren

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Long-horizon large language model (LLM) agents distill past trajectories into reusable experiences. Misleading or adversarial guidance in source trajectories can corrupt these experiences, allowing harmful instructions to persist across tasks and influence future agent actions. Assessing the risks of potentially corrupted experiences poses three challenges: (1) modeling provenance and execution dependencies before experience reuse, (2) learning task-conditioned risk from trajectory outcomes, and (3) repairing risky experiences at online inference. To address these challenges, we present ExpGuard, a framework for retrieval-triggered experience risk assessment and online repair. Specifically, to tackle challenge (1), ExpGuard integrates experience semantics with provenance and execution dependencies in a task-conditioned heterogeneous graph to predict attack and task-failure risks before experience reuse. To enable task-conditioned risk supervision and tackle challenge (2), we conduct matched counterfactual replays of each original experience and its minimally neutralized counterpart from the same task and environment checkpoint, deriving separate risk labels from differences in attack success and legitimate task completion. To tackle challenge (3) at inference time, ExpGuard repairs candidate experiences flagged as high-risk by the graph-based predictor at retrieval and re-predicts their risks before they enter the agent's active experience context. Extensive experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of ExpGuard in identifying risky experiences and defending agents against experience corruption.

Abstract:In the manufacturing domain, equipment maintenance tasks depend on expert knowledge. Their documentation is often stored in heterogeneous formats and sources. The enhancement of LLMs with Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is promising for bridging the scattered knowledge, but traditional vector-based RAG shows weaknesses with two characteristics which are common in real maintenance queries: complex causal chains and cross-domain synthesis. To address this, we propose SuSi-RAG, a hybrid Graph RAG solution, that matches a user query and each maintenance record using subgraph similarity. Authors:Haowen Hou, Gen Li, Ruilin Li, Yicheng Liu, Zhen Huang, Shuai Dong, Congcong Wang, Kaiwen Tuo, Hongrui Wu, Zelin Peng, Kanghui Tian, Ji Zhao, Dianyi Wang, Jiaqi Wang

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Fully online on-policy distillation (OPD) is costly for long-horizon agents because each update depends on fresh environment rollouts. Prefix replay offers an efficient alternative by sampling current-student responses from stored histories, reducing idle time for long-tail rollouts and allowing each collected trajectory to support multiple updates. However, our analysis of the complete prefix replay pipeline identifies two limitations in long-horizon settings. In trajectory collection, teacher-only trajectories miss student-induced histories, while broader history coverage can reduce teacher reliability. In trajectory reuse, fixed position-based schedules cannot identify critical decisions that may occur at any trajectory depth. We introduce CARE-OPD (Collect for coverAge, Replay with Evidence) to address both limitations. Student-seeded teacher completions and response-level confidence gating expand history coverage while filtering unreliable updates. Hindsight replay allocation then uses downstream execution evidence to select consequential turns. Across long-horizon tasks Software Engineering and Deep Research, CARE-OPD outperforms online OPD and prior prefix replay baselines. On SWE-bench Verified, it raises task success to 59.5% while reducing end-to-end training time by 45% and environment actions by 44% relative to asynchronous online OPD.

A Rust library for building communications applications. SIP, RTP, WebRTC, QUIC/WebTransport/WebSocket conversations, Media over QUIC, and voice-AI agents share one call model, and the whole stack is in the box: no external SIP server, media server, or RTP engine to install and wire together.

What rvoip is

rvoip is a library, not a server you deploy next to your application. Signalling, media, codecs, NAT traversal, security, and the conversation model are all Rust crates in this workspace, so a call goes from your code to the wire without a separately installed SIP proxy, media server, or RTP engine. The examples below run on a laptop with nothing installed but Rust; the same crates carry the release-gated interoperability evidence against Asterisk, FreeSWITCH, Jambonz, Kamailio, and OpenSIPS. Carriers evaluating standards coverage, performance, and reliability should start with For carriers.

Historical performance: published 0.5.0

One AWS c8i.4xlarge (16 vCPU, 8 cores, Intel Xeon 6, 32 GiB) running a single rvoip process, measured with the RFC 7501/7502 benchmark:

At 2x capacity, rvoip keeps admitting calls at its max CPS, refuses the excess with fast 503 responses, and recovers cleanly once the load drops.

Max CPS (calls per second; RFC 7502's Session Establishment Rate) is the highest rate with zero failed calls over a trial of at least 180 s and 50,000 call attempts.

rvoip was measured as a terminating endpoint: it answers each call and echoes its media. A relaying B2BUA or SBC handles two call legs per session.

G.711 media is passed through; nothing is transcoded.

Concurrent calls is the CPS times the 10 s call length, not a measured maximum; long-call capacity, where most SBC datasheets quote their concurrency, has not been measured yet.

The server is a cloud VM, not an appliance.

p50 and p99 setup are the RFC 6076 session request delay measured at 90% of max CPS, recorded in whole milliseconds (so 1 ms means 1 ms or less); n/m = not measured.

The UDP + RTP max CPS was confirmed twice rather than three times.

The release check also passed at 5% above the TLS and UDP signaling max CPS, so those two figures may be slightly understated.

Not yet measured: B2BUA/relay mode (two legs per call), registrations, transcoding, WebRTC (DTLS-SRTP), and failover.

Full method and per-trial data: RFC 7501/7502 benchmark report.

Five-minute start

1. Add one dependency.

[dependencies] rvoip-sip = "0.5.1" tokio = { version = "1", features = ["full"] }

Upgrading an existing application from 0.3? The 0.4 migration guide has before/after code for every breaking change.

2. Make a call. Bob answers, Alice dials, Alice hangs up. Everything runs on loopback, so this works on a laptop with no PBX, no account, and no network setup.

use std::time::Duration; use rvoip_sip::{Config, Endpoint, EndpointProfile}; #[tokio::main] async fn main() -> rvoip_sip::Result<()> { let bob = tokio::spawn(async { let mut bob = Endpoint::builder() .name("bob") .profile(EndpointProfile::Custom(Config::local("bob", 5071))) .build() .await?; let incoming = bob.wait_for_incoming().await?; let call = incoming.answer().await?; call.wait_for_end(None).await?; bob.shutdown().await }); tokio::time::sleep(Duration::from_millis(300)).await; let alice = Endpoint::builder() .name("alice") .profile(EndpointProfile::Custom(Config::local("alice", 5070))) .build() .await?; let call = alice .call_and_wait("sip:bob@127.0.0.1:5071", Some(Duration::from_secs(10))) .await?; call.hangup_and_wait(Some(Duration::from_secs(5))).await?; alice.shutdown().await?; bob.await.unwrap() }

3. Or run the same program from the checkout.

git clone https://github.com/eisenzopf/rvoip.git && cd rvoip cargo run -p rvoip-sip --example endpoint_local_call

From here, examples/ walks forward one step at a time: real microphone audio, registering to a PBX, hold/DTMF, transfers, SRTP and TLS, an IVR, a B2BUA, then browser and voice-AI gateways.

You need one crate

The workspace contains 47 crates. You import one of them; it pulls in everything it needs. The rest are internal layers you never name in your own Cargo.toml.

Two shortcuts:

rvoip-sip is the release-gated product. If your application is telephony, start and stay there. It is the only crate with a signed interoperability attestation (see evidence).

rvoip is the facade. It re-exports rvoip-sip as rvoip::sip and adds the shared Orchestrator plus optional transports and extensions behind Cargo features. Its feature table and the deployment bundles (bundle-sip-endpoint, bundle-carrier-sip, bundle-browser-gateway, bundle-ai-conversation, bundle-full-pure-rust, bundle-full-native) are the only feature lists you need.

Deployment backends (Keycloak, LDAP, Redis, Postgres vCon storage, audit and SIEM sinks, SAML, SCIM, WebAuthn, STIR/SHAKEN, IMS AKA) are separate rvoip-* extension crates you add only when you operate that backend. The extension catalog lists them.

Pick your path

For JavaScript UCTP control, see the experimental browser/Node WebSocket client. It is available as source with TypeScript declarations; the package is not yet published to npm. Application-profile dispatch is a separate opt-in server extension that stock servers do not enable.

Path 1 — SIP telephony (rvoip-sip)

Four API surfaces, from simplest to most control. Most applications use the first one.

Register an account on a PBX and place a call through it:

use std::time::Duration; use rvoip_sip::{Endpoint, EndpointProfile, Result}; # async fn example() -> Result<()> { let mut endpoint = Endpoint::builder() .name("alice") .account("1001") .password("secret") .registrar("sips:pbx.example.com:5061") .profile(EndpointProfile::AsteriskTlsSrtpRegisteredFlow) .build() .await?; endpoint.register().await?; let call = endpoint .call_and_wait("1002", Some(Duration::from_secs(30))) .await?; call.send_dtmf('1').await?; call.hangup_and_wait(Some(Duration::from_secs(5))).await?; endpoint.shutdown().await?; # Ok(()) # }

Answer calls and react to key presses, the shape of an IVR or auto-attendant:

use rvoip_sip::{CallHandlerDecision, CallbackPeer, Config, Result}; # async fn example() -> Result<()> { let peer = CallbackPeer::builder(Config::local("ivr", 5120)) .on_incoming(|call| async move { println!("incoming call from {}", call.from); CallHandlerDecision::Accept }) .on_dtmf(|call, digit| async move { if digit == '0' { call.transfer_blind("sip:operator@127.0.0.1:5122").await?; } Ok(()) }) .on_ended(|call_id, reason| async move { println!("call {call_id} ended: {reason:?}"); Ok(()) }) .build() .await?; peer.run().await # }

Security and transports are configuration, not different APIs: SDES-SRTP in 07-secure-call-srtp, TLS in 08-tls-transport, DTLS-SRTP, G.729, AMR-NB/WB, and Opus behind Cargo features. The full surface is documented in the SIP crate README.

Path 2 — Browser and cross-transport gateways (rvoip + app)

RvoipApp declares transports, roles, and routing through one builder and runs SIP, WebRTC, and UCTP listeners on the shared Orchestrator:

rvoip = { version = "0.5.1", features = ["app"] }

use rvoip::app::*; # async fn run() -> rvoip::app::AppResult<()> { let app = RvoipApp::builder() .webrtc( WebRtcConfig::ws("127.0.0.1:8081") .allow(Role::Customer, [Capability::Text, Capability::Voice]), ) .sip( SipConfig::bind("127.0.0.1:5060") .domain("callcenter.local") .allow(Role::Employee, [Capability::Voice]) .registrar_users([("alice", "password123")]), ) .employees(EmployeePolicy::named(["alice"])) .customers(CustomerPolicy::webrtc_only()) .assignment(AssignmentPolicy::fixed("alice")) .on_message(|ctx, msg| async move { ctx.reply("Alice", format!("Alice received: {}", msg.text)).await }) .build() .await?; app.run().await # }

Start from 12-customer-escalation-sip-webrtc: a browser chat escalates to a SIP agent's phone. For SIP into Amazon Connect, see 13-sip-to-amazon-connect.

Path 3 — Voice AI (rvoip + vapi or voip-3)

Hosted agent. rvoip-vapi speaks Vapi's bidirectional raw-audio WebSocket directly. Your SIP or WebRTC caller is bridged to the agent through the Orchestrator; the agent joins as its own AI participant with typed events, control messages, and supervised teardown. Start from 14-vapi-agent, one server that accepts either kind of caller. Attaching an agent to a caller you already hold:

use std::sync::Arc; use rvoip::Orchestrator; use rvoip::core_traits::ids::ConnectionId; use rvoip::vapi::{VapiAdapter, VapiApiKey, VapiAssistant, VapiCallOptions, VapiConfig}; # async fn attach(orchestrator: Arc<Orchestrator>, caller: ConnectionId) # -> Result<(), Box<dyn std::error::Error>> { let key = VapiApiKey::new(std::env::var("VAPI_API_KEY")?)?; let adapter = VapiAdapter::new(VapiConfig::new(key))?; let options = VapiCallOptions::new(VapiAssistant::saved(std::env::var("VAPI_ASSISTANT_ID")?)); // Joins a distinct AI participant, originates the agent leg, bridges audio. let mut call = adapter.attach_agent(&orchestrator, caller, options).await?; call.say("One moment while I look that up.", false, true).await?; let _outcome = call.wait().await; # Ok(()) # }

Your own pipeline. rvoip-harness gives you ASR, TTS, dialog, and recording provider traits, and rvoip-vcon emits signed vCon records of the conversation. 11-ai-harness-demo runs the whole loop with deterministic providers, so you can see the shape before you plug in a real model.

How it fits together

Every transport turns into the same objects, so an application written against the conversation model does not care whether a leg arrived over SIP, a browser, or QUIC. That is what lets one Orchestrator bridge them.

┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ Your application │ │ softphone · PBX · contact center · browser gateway · AI agent │ └───────────────────────────────┬───────────────────────────────────┘ │ rvoip-sip / rvoip (+ features) ┌───────────────────────────────▼───────────────────────────────────┐ │ Shared conversation model (rvoip-core) │ │ Orchestrator · Conversation · Session · Participant · Connection │ │ routing · admission · bridges · media graph · events │ └──────────────┬────────────────┬────────────────┬──────────────────┘ │ │ │ ┌──────▼──────┐ ┌──────▼──────┐ ┌──────▼───────────────┐ │ SIP + RTP │ │ WebRTC │ │ UCTP │ │ UDP/TCP/TLS │ │ ICE/DTLS │ │ QUIC/WebTransport/WS │ └──────┬──────┘ └──────┬──────┘ └──────┬───────────────┘ └────────────────┼────────────────┘ │ ┌──────────────────▼───────────────────┐ │ Optional products and extensions │ │ Vapi · AI harness · vCon · MoQ │ │ Amazon Connect · identity · audit │ └───────────────────────────────────────┘

Words you will meet in the API:

Conversation — the thing people are in together. It outlives any one connection.

Session — one participant's time in a conversation.

Participant — a human or an AI agent. Roles (Agent, Observer, customer) can change without moving connections.

Connection — one transport leg: a SIP dialog, a browser peer connection, a UCTP session.

Stream — the audio (or video, or data) flowing on a connection. Bridges join streams across connections, transcoding when codecs differ.

Adapters depend on the shared ConnectionAdapter trait; the core never imports a transport. The design docs go deeper, starting with INTERFACE_DESIGN.md and CONVERSATION_PROTOCOL.md.

Clay wants you to get about the same results whether you use its own agent or point Claude Code at Clay. Usage is already split close to 50/50 between Clay’s agent and outside harnesses. In this episode, we chat with Jeff Barg, Head of AI at Clay, about what it takes to build go-to-market infrastructure that any agent can use.

Jeff joined Clay when it acquired Avenue, the ops alerting startup he cofounded. At the time, Clay had about 50 people, and Avenue had five. AI was already a big part of how Clay thought about its product, but nobody there worked on AI. Since then, the business has grown roughly 10x, and the team is up to around 600 people. In September, Clay raised $115 million at a $7.1 billion valuation.

By Jeff’s napkin math, it would cost about $1,500 to check all 40 million companies in Clay’s database against your ideal customer profile with a classifier like TypeSafe’s Jev. Models have been acing Clay’s lead-qualification evals for about a year now. Jeff sees more value in using frontier models for orchestration and deep research. We also talk about why customers hate picking a model, how Clay plans to pick one for them, and why the team still runs its agents on a system it built with BullMQ, without Temporal or LangGraph.

Jeff tells us what happened when OpenAI called off Clay’s ChatGPT apps launch with two days to go. We get into why customers hated Workflows at first, how agents came to build most workflows, and when post-training pays off. We also talk about why agents still can’t debug other agents, even as Clay’s agent can count its YC customers in about a minute.

Sculptor started as a tool loop. Now it's a coding agent.

When Jeff took over as Head of AI, one engineer was doing all of Clay’s agent work. He soon left to start his own company. Claude Code changed what Jeff wanted to build. His first launch was Sculptor, which shipped in September 2025 on models from the Sonnet 3.5 era. It ran a set of tools in a loop with a system prompt the team had spent a lot of time tuning. It was great at adding columns but terrible at booking ten qualified meetings.

The evals didn’t catch much. Clay used an LLM to judge which tools Sculptor called. That missed things people actually did with it, like asking it to analyze a table or giving it an enterprise table with millions of rows.

Jeff took away three lessons. Coding agents are really good at Bash and Python. Sculptor’s tools were too limited to do much by stringing them together. And the agent couldn’t see CRM data or campaign results. So Clay rebuilt Sculptor as a coding agent on the Pi harness. It runs the Clay CLI in a cloud sandbox. A new data layer called Audiences matches records across the CRM and Gong so the agent can see them together.

Most companies post-train too early

For Jeff’s first eighteen months at Clay, the team talked about training its own models. It only started to look viable in the past couple of months. Jeff sees two ways to bring costs down. First, swap in an off-the-shelf open-weight model, which is already much cheaper than Opus. Then you can post-train a smaller model on your own tools and queries until it does as well as Opus on your tasks.

Jeff sees companies make two mistakes here. One is trying to beat the frontier models, which is still really hard even in a single domain. The other is starting before you spend enough on inference for the savings to matter. Jeff puts that threshold somewhere between $10 million and $100 million.

$1,500 to score every company in the world

Clay hasn’t put TypeSafe’s Jev into production yet, but Jeff has run the numbers. Clay’s database has about 40 million companies. A LinkedIn page is roughly a thousand tokens, and an ideal customer profile adds about 500 more. Ask a classifier whether each company is a good fit, and the bill for all 40 million comes to around $1,500. Today, most customers qualify a list of maybe 10,000 companies. At these prices, you can start with the entire market.

Jeff puts a good enrichment budget at $1 to $10 per qualified lead and $10 to $100 per booked call. He thinks qualification itself is close to solved. Models have been scoring about as high as they can on Clay’s evals for roughly a year. Just about any model from the past year can pull a company’s latest funding round. Frontier models still matter for deep prospecting research and for orchestration, where Jeff says the best plays can be ten to a hundred times as effective as an average one.

Customers hate the model selector

Customers complain when they see Clay’s model selector. They can’t check the evals for their use case every time a new model ships, and they shouldn’t have to. Clay is about to launch an automatic router that picks a model for each task. There’s no fancy branding. Finding Apple’s CEO is a very different job from finding the CEO of an obscure local company.

The selector isn’t going away. Agency owners and power users still want control. Everyone else just wants to get on with their work. Clay has to earn their trust before they’ll leave it on auto.

No Temporal, no LangGraph

Clay built most of its agent stack itself. It uses BullMQ to keep agent jobs running, Postgres and ClickHouse for product data, and Snowflake for analytics. Jeff isn’t sure how BullMQ will hold up if usage grows another tenfold, but it’s held up so far. Clay recently shipped 25 to 30 new integrations. Contracts and testing took longer than the engineering work.

Where Clay did run into trouble was scale. People run an agent per row, so if someone starts a run on a table of 50,000 companies, inference demand spikes all at once. Clay brought in LangSmith to see what was happening. The team also built a way to throttle requests and to pause and resume work when a provider goes down or starts rate-limiting. The harder part is keeping things fair. Someone running a workflow for ten people still needs it to run when an enterprise customer is enriching a million rows.

Built for every agent, not just Clay's

Clay plans to ship a sandboxed agent that’s free to use any of Clay’s tools and building blocks. Jeff wants someone using Clay from Claude Code to get about the same results. People should be able to use Clay wherever they already work. Usage is already much closer to a 50/50 split between Clay’s agent and outside harnesses than Jeff expected.

The push came from inside Clay. Its own go-to-market team lived in Cursor and wouldn’t leave it, so they asked for a CLI. The MCP team switched over to build it, and Clay’s sales team now does deal prep in Codex. So why build a first-party agent at all? Plenty of customers live in the Clay UI and want an agent right there.

Keep building when the plan blows up

Jeff thinks it takes more courage to change plans at 600 people than it did at 50. Clay was a launch partner for ChatGPT apps. In the middle of the quarter, Clay put about a quarter of its engineers on the integration for a month. Two days before launch, OpenAI postponed it indefinitely. Clay kept building on the assumption that OpenAI would change its mind. Three or four days later, it did.

When Clay first showed customers Workflows, they were confused. Some were upset. They wanted their spreadsheet. Now agency owners like it, and a growing share of the work that used to run in tables runs in Workflows. Most workflows are built with the CLI or the agent. CEO Kareem Amin spent five years building Clay when almost nothing was working. Jeff still sees that persistence in the company today.

Agents still can't debug agents

What Jeff wants most is a model that can read a long agent trace and tell him where the run went wrong. Today’s models tend to flag whichever step took a long time, even when the mistake was somewhere else. Clay would like to pull traces from bad runs, have an agent review them and use what it finds to improve the agent automatically. That still doesn’t work, and Clay writes a lot of its skills by hand.

Models still make basic mistakes, like retrying the same CLI command with the same inputs over and over. But when Jeff wanted to know how many YC companies use Clay, he asked the agent and had the answer in about a minute. A year ago, he would’ve had to scrape the batch, get access to Snowflake and cross-reference the two by hand.

Magmide proposes Liquid Types and Wasm for verifiable programming
Why: Your FP pipeline gains a pragmatic tool against LLM-induced decay
Hacker News ↗HN 185↑extracted 04:00 · 971 words

Because of LLMs, the discipline of software engineering is in crisis. Quality is nose-diving, and I see no reason why it won't keep getting worse. Software engineering was struggling before LLMs (opens new window). The default was to be sloppy and poorly considered, and now everyone is frantically accelerating those same terrible practices to LLM speed.

Something has to change. I've been preoccupied with this for a while (opens new window), but much more qualified people finally fully agree with me (opens new window). The only way forward is to fully embrace formal verification (opens new window), all the way down to the foundations of our civilizational software stack. We have to post-hoc verify the things we can, and rewrite the rest.

But that's easier said than done. Existing formal verification tools suffer from massive usability (opens new window) and understandability (opens new window) problems, and have failed to make any kind of noticeable impact on the broader culture of software engineering.

It might be tempting to say that LLMs will actually save us in this regard, by allowing humans to create formally verified code without needing to understand its fundamentals in any way. This is pure cope. Merely having excellent proof assistants and LLMs will never be good enough. Even in a world where something like Lean is extremely scalable and LLMs can write all proofs, it doesn't matter if they aren't adopted by actual industry engineers as they build the real apps we actually use. For provably correct code to defend us from the LLM future:

It needs to be possible to formally verify code that runs in any environment, from web browsers down to embedded systems.

Industry engineers need to know provably correct code is even possible and be comfortable enough with it to at least tell their LLMs to write it.

Proof assistants guarantee that proofs are correct, but correctness alone is not enough. Someone has to read the theorem statements and confirm they capture the intended mathematics. This is the human contract that no amount of automation can eliminate.

This is what most voices are missing in this discussion: a safe future isn't going to be delivered by a small priestly class of formal methods researchers, too much code must be produced in too many contexts. The entire discipline of software engineering has to grow up and demand that all code we produce is at minimum provably safe. This requires many different projects and bets that attack this imperative of cultural change from different directions.

§ The long winding road of Magmide

My chosen angle is Magmide, a project whose goal is to create a new formally verifiable programming language intended for immediate adoption by practitioners. However when I first had this idea my specific plan was woefully over-ambitious and unrealistic, since I wanted to make a whole new proof assistant and an assembly-level base language so the system could be verified all the way down to the metal.

I pursued it for a time as a startup, and I met with some investors and was applying for an SBIR grant. Then I realized some obvious problems with my approach (opens new window):

It didn't make sense to build a new proof assistant when Lean has been making such rapid progress.

An assembly-level language was insanely difficult and wasn't necessary to gain adoption from practitioners.

I didn't have the appetite to pursue some strained business model for a project that ultimately makes most sense as a pure public good.

So now after a few years pursuing other projects and letting all these ideas marinate, I've arrived at a much simpler and more pragmatic vision of what Magmide could be. If I started working on it today, it would:

Compile to WebAssembly using the Garbage Collection (opens new window) features.

Have a Rust-inspired type system but without the difficulties of the borrow-checker.

Start with basic and more easily learnable SMT powered (opens new window) Liquid Types (opens new window) and Flow Effects (opens new window).

Over time integrate Lean as the proof layer, and use some separation logic paradigm like Iris (opens new window) to bootstrap the language and verify its own implementation.

This design is specifically intended to appeal to actual engineers and "meet them where they are". The roadmap would start from a simpler more mainstream design and go "bottom up" by adding more and more proving capabilities, rather than going "top down" by starting with the most formally powerful system and then seeking to make it more usable.

A possible analogue for the adoption of Magmide is Deno (opens new window): Deno is "just javascript/typescript", but it adds a runtime layer of permissions checking. Engineers who care about sandbox safety have enthusiastically adopted Deno, and Flow Effects would offer strictly more security.

§ Nothing will save us but us

I continue to stand behind basically all the arguments I made in the Magmide repo (opens new window) (aside from the specific architecture). Formal verification must become the mainstream of software engineering, and we have to embrace a wide diversity of approaches to make that happen. Software engineers aren't a monolith, and not every cultural pocket will respond to the same new tools and resources.

And you know what, LLMs will probably help a lot! But we can't just wait around for an all-knowing machine god to save us from ourselves. If we bet our whole future on ASI making all our problems go away and then that doesn't happen (opens new window), history will look back on us even more foolish and disastrous than all the millennialists (opens new window) who merely wasted their own time and resources.

It is our responsibility to meet the challenges of our age with full agency and responsibility, and treat the emergence of a beneficent super-intelligence as an improbable jackpot.

AI agents automate NixOS infrastructure with Go tooling
Why: Your custom tools and self-hosted stack become maintainable again.
Hacker News ↗HN 784↑extracted 04:00 · 1,497 words

I’ve been self-hosting things for many years. There were times when I was quite enthusiastic about it, constantly trying out new software and tinkering with my infrastructure. But at some point, that enthusiasm faded a bit.

I never really stopped self-hosting, but I became less inclined to experiment with new things. Maintaining what I already had running was enough for me.

Over the last few months, however, I’ve found myself getting increasingly excited about self-hosting again. And, somewhat ironically, AI is a big reason for that.

Less friction

Most of my infrastructure configuration lives in Git repositories, mainly in the form of Docker Compose files, scripts, and other configuration files. This makes it possible to use AI coding agents to work on my infrastructure in much the same way as they would work on any other software project.

Whenever I want to try a new service, adjust an existing configuration, or automate something, I can now do so with considerably less effort.

In the past, I would sometimes put off certain changes simply because I didn’t feel like spending an evening reading changelogs and documentation or troubleshooting issues. It’s not that I couldn’t have done these things before. But often, the amount of effort required just wasn’t worth it to me.

Now, even relatively spontaneous ideas often end up becoming reality.

Trying things I always found interesting

There are also technologies that I’ve always found interesting but never felt knowledgeable enough about to really make use of them.

NixOS is a good example. I’ve always liked the idea of declaratively configuring an entire system; I even used it on my laptop during my Linux honeymoon phase. But getting everything configured the way I wanted was quite a hurdle. With the help of AI, I’m now making much more extensive use of NixOS on my home server.

For my VPS infrastructure, I’ve also started building my own Debian-based bootc image (after thinking about it for some time).

Having my entire operating system configuration defined in a Git repository and automatically building a bootable container image from it is something I find really cool. But it’s also something I probably wouldn’t have invested enough time in to get working on my own before. Even with the help of AI, it still took me half a day when I set it up a few months ago. Without AI, it would probably have taken me at least a week.

AI makes experimenting with unfamiliar technologies much more approachable. I don’t necessarily need to understand every little detail before I can get started. I can learn about things along the way, ask questions, and gradually improve my configurations.

Automating almost everything

Something I’ve also finally managed to do is automate most of the build, update, and deployment processes for my infrastructure.

A lot of the work involved in self-hosting isn’t particularly exciting. Keeping track of new releases, updating container image tags, building images, and deploying configuration changes are all things that need to be done regularly.

I still remember what updating a service used to look like: bumping the version and adjusting the Docker Compose file on my laptop, pushing the changes to the Git remote, SSHing into the server, pulling the changes, and finally deploying them. Now, a single Git push is enough. If a push is even necessary.

With Renovate, I no longer need to actively keep track of most software updates. It creates pull requests for me, and often, all I need to do is review and merge them. I can even do that on my phone while sitting on the sofa.

Pushing changes or merging a pull request triggers the corresponding GitHub Actions or Forgejo Actions workflows, which take care of building and deploying everything.

Of course, there’s nothing particularly groundbreaking about any of this. DevOps has been around for a long time, and none of these automation tools require AI. But AI has helped me put all these pieces together in a way that I previously never quite got around to doing.

And the nice thing about automating these tasks is that the benefits extend well beyond the initial setup. I now have less tedious work to do every time I want to change something or update a service.

Back to more powerful hardware

As I’ve started hosting more things again, I’ve also moved more of my services from my VPS to my home server.

For a while, I was using an HP t640 thin client as my home server. However, with more things I wanted to run, I decided to switch back to my ASRock DeskMini A300, which had been sitting unused for some time. I’m thinking about repurposing the t640 as a “router on a stick”, though.

I even took the opportunity to upgrade its CPU from an AMD Ryzen 5 2400G to a Ryzen 5 PRO 4650G and replaced the stock AMD cooler with a quieter Noctua cooler. Noise was the main reason I switched to the t640 in the first place.

It’s nice to put some hardware I already owned back to use rather than buying an entirely new machine. I also managed to get both the CPU and the cooler for a good price through a classifieds site.

Having some additional computing power available gives me more room to experiment. And there’s something quite satisfying about having a small box at home running a growing collection of services that I use in my everyday life.

Building my own tools

But it’s not just about self-hosting existing software. AI has also changed how I approach problems for which there isn’t an existing piece of software that does exactly what I want.

Previously, I would often spend quite some time searching for a suitable open-source project, comparing different alternatives, and figuring out how to make one of them work for my particular use case.

These days, for smaller problems, building my own solution has become a much more attractive option.

I’ve recently built tools to automatically download invoices, e-papers or Linux ISOs.

I can build (or have AI build) these tools in Go, my preferred programming language, and tailor them to my exact needs.

They don’t need to support every possible use case, have an elaborate user interface, or be particularly interesting to anyone other than me. I don’t need to turn every little tool into a proper open-source project with extensive documentation and support for other people’s use cases. They just need to solve my problem.

What I particularly enjoy is how well this complements my increasingly automated infrastructure. Building a small tool has become easier, and so has building a container image for it, deploying it, and keeping it running.

The entire process, from having an idea to having something actually running on my home server, requires much less effort than it used to.

Just because I can doesn’t mean I should

Of course, there are downsides to all of this.

Every additional service is another thing that needs to be maintained. AI-generated software isn’t exempt from bugs, security issues, or technical debt. And having an AI agent make changes to my infrastructure doesn’t absolve me of the responsibility of understanding those changes.

There’s also the temptation to build or deploy things simply because I can. When implementing an idea becomes so easy, it’s perhaps more important than ever to occasionally ask myself whether it’s actually worth implementing in the first place.

On the other hand, self-hosting is a hobby for me. Not everything I do needs to be economically sensible or particularly useful. Sometimes, experimenting with something new is the whole point.

And with much of the routine maintenance automated, adding another service doesn’t necessarily mean adding as much recurring work as it used to.

A somewhat ironic development?

I find it somewhat ironic that AI, a technology largely dominated by big tech companies, is helping me become a little less dependent on those very companies in other areas of my digital life.

I’m aware that this also creates a new kind of dependency. But the software and infrastructure configurations I create with the help of AI can continue to exist independently of the AI services I used to create them. And since I mostly use open-weight models, I have a wide range of providers to choose from.

What I appreciate most, however, isn’t necessarily the time I’m saving. It’s that I’m more inclined to actually pursue the little ideas I have.

I’m trying out technologies I’ve been curious about for years. I’m automating things that I’ve wanted to automate for a long time. I’m building little tools for myself. I’m tinkering with hardware again.

AI hasn’t fundamentally changed why I like self-hosting. I still enjoy having control over my digital life, learning new things, and tinkering with technology.

It’s just removed a lot of the friction that made me lose some of my enthusiasm for it over the years.

And now, I’m having a lot of fun with it again.
