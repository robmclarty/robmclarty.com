SWE-Shift Benchmark Shows Agents Fail Mid-Execution State Shifts
Why: Your agentic pipelines lack resilience against evolving workspaces.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 05:00 · 198 words

The SWE-Shift benchmark evaluated 17 coding agents across 120 scenarios involving mid-execution task or environment updates. GPT-6 Astra's paired success rate dropped from 67.35% to 24.79%, while tool and session modifications resulted in zero paired success. This reveals that current systems struggle to preserve context or recover state during operational shifts.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Repository-level coding benchmarks largely assume that tasks and environments remain unchanged throughout execution. In software development, code, dependencies, runtime state, and requirements can change while an agent is still working. We introduce SWE-Shift, a benchmark for evaluating coding agents under such mid-execution changes. From the same saved history and workspace, a Clean continuation proceeds without intervention, while a matched Shifted continuation receives a task-relevant update. Success requires completing the current task, preserving still-valid work, and satisfying the applicable state and evidence checks. The benchmark contains 120 scenarios across 12 change families, each evaluated at nine scheduled intervention positions. We evaluate 17 coding-agent systems, all of which have lower Shifted than Clean success. GPT-6 Astra drops from 67.35% to 24.79%, with 20.34% paired success. Tool/session changes and stale evidence yield zero paired success across all systems. Late-position paired success is lower for some systems and higher for others. These results show that success on the unchanged task can coexist with failures to preserve work, recover state, or refresh evidence after an update.

Shared documents outperform debate in multi-agent workflows

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Designing large language model (LLM) multi-agent systems (MAS) still relies on trial and error and informal "folk wisdom." Manual studies test only a few communication structures or topologies (e.g., sequential, parallel, shared-memory, or debate), while automated optimizers discard search trajectories after selecting a task-specific workflow. We present a two-phase pipeline for mining reproducible design principles from workflow code and execution traces. We repurpose AFlow's MCTS optimizer across six composable operators and 26 deterministically verified Enigmata tasks, retaining all intermediate rounds to build a corpus of 28,568 executable workflows. A sandboxed AutoResearch loop then mines orchestration code and prompt templates under an $L_0$-penalized validation objective and tests candidate principles on held-out searches. Across 300 feature-mining trials, we identify effects that persist on held-out searches across three levels of workflow design: workflows beginning with independent solvers and one-shot aggregation (Star-first) perform worse ($\Delta = -0.045$); persistent shared documents (SharedMemory) outperform debate ($\Delta = +0.135$); and prompt specificity and placeholder-based context routing yield gains comparable to communication-structure selection ($\Delta = +0.118$). More broadly, our pipeline and results show the feasibility of turning discarded search trajectories into a statistical testbed for discovering and validating multi-agent workflow design principles.

Grassmann Layer Replaces Attention With Linear Scaling
Why: It replaces opaque tensor lifting with geometric intuition for your sequence modeling.
Hacker News ↗HN 189↑extracted 06:50 · 291 words

A new model uses Causal Grassmann layers and Plucker coordinates to encode token pairs as two-dimensional subspaces, achieving performance comparable to Transformers on Wikitext-2. The architecture eliminates explicit self-attention while claiming linear scaling in sequence length.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:We revisit a basic question in sequence modeling: is explicit self-attention actually necessary for strong performance and reasoning? We argue that standard multi-head attention is best seen as a form of tensor lifting: hidden vectors are mapped into a high-dimensional space of pairwise interactions, and learning proceeds by constraining this lifted tensor through gradient descent. This mechanism is extremely expressive but mathematically opaque, because after many layers it becomes very hard to describe the model with a small family of explicit invariants. To explore an alternative, we propose an attention-free architecture based on Grassmann flows. Instead of forming an L by L attention matrix, our Causal Grassmann layer (i) linearly reduces token states, (ii) encodes local token pairs as two-dimensional subspaces on a Grassmann manifold via Plucker coordinates, and (iii) fuses these geometric features back into the hidden states through gated mixing. Information therefore propagates by controlled deformations of low-rank subspaces over multi-scale local windows, so the core computation lives on a finite-dimensional manifold rather than in an unstructured tensor space. On the Wikitext-2 language modeling benchmark, purely Grassmann-based models with 13 to 18 million parameters achieve validation perplexities within about 10 to 15 percent of size-matched Transformers. On the SNLI natural language inference task, a Grassmann-Plucker head on top of DistilBERT slightly outperforms a Transformer head, with best validation and test accuracies of 0.8550 and 0.8538 compared to 0.8545 and 0.8511. We analyze the complexity of Grassmann mixing, show linear scaling in sequence length for fixed rank, and argue that such manifold-based designs offer a more structured route toward geometric and invariant-based interpretations of neural reasoning.

vLLM 0.31 Enforces Tool-Call Accuracy via XGrammar
Why: Your agentic pipelines need reliable tool execution.
Hacker News ↗HN 166↑extracted 06:50 · 1,816 words

Ubicloud engineers verified that enabling --tool-strict-level achieves 100% schema accuracy for Kimi K2.6 and DeepSeek V4 Flash, up from 92%. XGrammar prevents malformed JSON by enforcing valid token paths during inference.

Read the extracted text ↓

All Blog Posts

October 20, 2026 · 8 min read

Jesse Donkervliet and Junhao Li

Senior Software Engineers

When I use an open-weight model, I see results that are nowhere close to what the quality benchmarks indicate.

Software developer working with AI models these days

Tool call accuracy is an important part of serving open-weight models. When your tool calls aren’t accurate, the model builds up on nonsensical context. This leads to low quality results, particularly for agentic workloads where multi-turn sessions are common.

Sadly, many providers and widely-used inference engines don’t fully pass tool-call accuracy tests. For example, when we served Kimi K2.6 with vLLM version 0.28, we got the expected tool call schema only 92% of the time. In other words, 2 out of every 25 attempted tool calls failed.

Fortunately, vLLM recently added support for constrained decoding, a technique to guarantee schema-adherence. This feature has then been released as a server-side flag in vLLM 0.31. By enabling it, you can get to 100% schema accuracy with the newer Kimi and DeepSeek models.

In this blog post, we first describe tool call schema accuracy and why it’s important in serving agentic workloads. We then talk about constrained decoding and how it fixes the schema accuracy problem.

Tool-call Schemas

You can enable an LLM to call an external tool in two steps: (1) inform the AI model about the existence of externally available tool calls and (2) provide the model with instructions on how to output structured tool-calls. For example, when connecting OpenCode to Kimi, OpenCode sends Kimi a JSON array describing available tools. One of the tools in this list is webfetch, which fetches Web page contents:

[{ "type": "function", "function": { "name": "webfetch", "description": "- Fetches content from a specified URL\n- Takes a URL and optional format as input\n- Fetches the URL content, converts to requested format (markdown by default)\n- Returns the content in the specified format\n- Use this tool when you need to retrieve and analyze web content\n\nUsage notes:\n [content redacted for brevity]\n", "parameters": { "$schema": "https://json-schema.org/draft/2020-12/schema", "type": "object", "properties": { "url": { "type": "string", "description": "The URL to fetch content from" }, "format": { "type": "string", "enum": [ "text", "markdown", "html" ], "description": "The format to return the content in (text, markdown, or html). Defaults to markdown.", "default": "markdown" }, "timeout": { "type": "number", "description": "Optional timeout in seconds (max 120)" } }, "required": [ "url" ] } } }, ...]

The inference engine then wraps this JSON description in LLM-specific tokens before passing it into the LLM, to match how the LLM was trained to recognize tool declarations. When serving Kimi, this looks something like:

<|im_system|>tool_declare<|im_middle|>[{"type":"function","function":{...}, ...]<|im_end|>

The JSON object describing the tool (or function, in the JSON above) includes the tool’s parameters, their types, which of the parameters are required, etc. This is known as the tool’s schema. For an agent harness (e.g., OpenCode) to successfully execute a tool call generated by the LLM, the call must follow the rules specified in its schema.

Then, when a user asks to fetch a Web page via their agent harness, the LLM may output the following tokens:

I'll fetch that page. <|tool_calls_section_begin|><|tool_call_begin|>functions.webfetch:0<|tool_call_argument_begin|>{"url": "https://ubicloud.com", "format": "markdown"}<|tool_call_end|><|tool_calls_section_end|>

These tokens are parsed by the inference server’s parser, extracting the LLM-specific tokens and turning the call into a JSON object such as:

{ "role": "assistant", "content": "I'll fetch that page.", "tool_calls": [ { "id": "functions.webfetch:0", "type": "function", "function": { "name": "webfetch", "arguments": "{\"url\": \"https://example.com\", \"format\": \"markdown\"}" } } ] }

This is then sent back to your harness (OpenCode, in our example), which runs its webfetch tool and sends its output back to the model.

The Importance of Schema Accuracy

For a tool-call to succeed, the call must adhere to the tool’s schema.

The simple approach to tool calling is to let the model generate tool calls freely and trust that the model’s training is sufficient to generate correct calls. This is similar to how we trust the model to generate syntactically correct prose.

The simple approach works most of the time because modern models are good at following structured patterns. But success “most of the time” implies that many tool calls can still fail, resulting in context window pollution due to error messages, lower model performance, and increased cost due to the agent having to retry the call one or more times.

To avoid such experiences when using Kimi, Moonshot AI published their K2VV ToolCall benchmark. This benchmark lets providers verify that they serve Kimi with the same quality as the original, including its default behavior of only generating valid tool calls.

K2VV ToolCall’s workload consists of 2000 requests selected to evaluate when the LLM generates tool calls and whether the generated tool calls are syntactically correct (i.e., adhere to the provided schema). The benchmark allows analyzing the model’s quality by quantifying two metrics: trigger similarity and schema accuracy. Trigger similarity considers when the service decides to trigger a tool call, and how similar its decisions are to Moonshot AI’s own service. Schema accuracy quantifies the fraction of tool-calls that adhere to the tool’s schema and can therefore be invoked successfully. For example, when tool-call accuracy is 50%, half of the tool calls generated by the model are malformed.

Schema accuracy usually falls short due to the probabilistic nature of language models. A model generates tokens based on likelihood. It is not running a compiler or type checker. Even with a clear prompt, it can still produce malformed tool calls. This can be caused by, for example, a mismatch between the model’s training data and the current inference harness. Models are trained on many tool-calling datasets, each with its own tools, schemas, and conventions. As a result, a model may generate a tool or field name that it has seen during the training, even if it does not exist in the current production environment. For example, it may generate search_web when the available tool is web_search, or use city_name when the active schema expects location.

Constrained Decoding

An LLM computes for each token the probability that it is the next in the sequence. It then selects the next token by performing a weighted sample. Constrained decoding avoids malformed tool calls by preventing models from sampling tokens that would result in a syntactically invalid tool call. When using constrained decoding, instead of letting the model sampling tokens freely and validating the result afterward, the inference engine guides generation step by step. At each step, only valid tokens are allowed.

Which tokens are allowed is determined by the provided tool schema. vLLM implements this using structural tags from the XGrammar library. The snippet below shows an excerpt of how XGrammar implements constrained decoding in Kimi K2.6. It defines the valid structure of a tool call, including where the call begins, where the index appears, where the arguments begin, and where schema-constrained JSON must be generated.

# Kimi K2.6 tool call syntax: # <|tool_call_begin|>{function.name}:{index} # <|tool_call_argument_begin|>{arguments} # <|tool_call_end|> TagFormat( begin=f"{TOOL_CALL_BEGIN_PREFIX}{name}{TOOL_CALL_SUFFIX}", content=SequenceFormat( elements=[ RegexFormat(pattern=r"\d+"), ConstStringFormat(value=TOOL_CALL_ARGUMENT_BEGIN), JSONSchemaFormat( json_schema=parameters, any_order=any_order, max_whitespace_cnt=max_whitespace_cnt, ), ] ), end=TOOL_CALL_END, )

You can find the full implementation here.

With this definition, generation must follow a valid path:

Begin tool call → Tool name → Tool call index → Begin arguments → Schema-constrained JSON → End tool call

Invalid paths are blocked during generation. For example, when generation reaches the tool-call index phase, the engine restricts sampling to integer tokens and masks out everything else. The figure below, reproduced from XGrammar’s article, provides a good visualization of how invalid tokens are masked.

Source: Dong, Yixin, et al. "XGrammar: Flexible and efficient structured generation engine for large language models." Proceedings of Machine Learning and Systems 7 (2025). [link]

This changes schema accuracy from a best-effort approach to a runtime guarantee. The model still decides which token to generate, but now the token is selected from a restricted and syntactically-correct set.

Notably, this runtime enforcement adds negligible performance overhead. By precomputing token masks for each phase, and applying them efficiently on the GPU during generation, XGrammar guarantees correct tool calls without trading off significant performance.

Differences in Constrained-Decoding Behavior

Moonshot AI recommends constrained decoding to be turned on and its own API behaves as such. So we initially added constrained decoding in our fork of vLLM. After vLLM added this feature in v0.25, we switched to using this feature from upstream for Kimi and DeepSeek. Unfortunately not long after, we found out that our tool-call accuracy score dropped back to 91%!

After analyzing the problem, we confirmed that vLLM’s implementation of constrained decoding was still correct. Instead, we found that vLLM changed its default behavior for requests that do not explicitly turn constrained decoding on. This is primarily because vLLM is designed to support a wide range of models. Supporting constrained decoding is a per-model task and problems fall into three camps:

General model issues: For some models, their unconstrained output diverges significantly from the tool's schema or model's tool-calling format. This forces the model to select tokens it otherwise wouldn't have, leading it off course [1]. For example, gpt-oss-20b, Qwen 3.6, and GLM 5.1 users reported issues after having this feature enabled by default.

Grammar translation bugs: vLLM might have bugs in translating a model's tool schema or tool-calling format into the model's specific grammar. This can result in problems including invalid tool calls, request failures, silently dropping the constrained decoding, and lower tool-calling accuracy [1, 2].

Feature interaction bugs: There are bugs in the interaction between constrained decoding and other serving-engine features. For example, a bug in the interplay between constrained decoding and speculative decoding can result in speculative tokens being wrongly rejected by the grammar, leading to failing requests [1, 2].

Due to these issues, vLLM maintainers reported seeing problems with several models after constrained decoding was enabled. This made disabling constrained decoding by default the safer option for a model-agnostic serving engine such as vLLM. Later, vLLM added the --tool-strict-level flag so that users can enable tool-call strictness for their deployments.

Ubicloud’s focus is different from that of vLLM however. We focus on serving Kimi and DeepSeek models with the quality that users expect from first-party providers. For this reason, we enabled constrained decoding and re-ran the K2VV ToolCall benchmark. Once we verified 100% tool-call schema accuracy across all served model versions, we deployed the change to production. We then typed up this blog post to share our learnings.

Conclusion

When using LLMs, the quality you observe isn’t based on the model alone. The inference engine also plays an important role. Change the inference engine and its settings, and you change the model’s output quality.

When serving Kimi K2.6 and DeepSeek V4 Flash, we observed visible quality degradations due to tool-call schema inaccuracies. We solved this problem by first carrying our own vLLM fork and then enabling the new constrained decoding feature in vLLM. This helped improve our tool-call accuracy in the K2VV ToolCall benchmark from 92% to 100%.

In this blog post, we shared our experience with tool calling and constrained decoding. If this is interesting or you’d like to share war stories about serving models, please drop us a line in our support email.

SGLang KV Cache Bug Leaked Data Across Workers AI Requests
Why: It reveals critical isolation failures in your disaggregated LLM inference architecture.
Hacker News ↗HN 146↑extracted 05:00 · 3,611 words

A misconfiguration in Cloudflare's GLM-5.3 model allowed stale key-value cache pages to persist across tenants during disaggregated prefill and decode handoff. The vulnerability stemmed from SGLang reusing rejected prompts when they exceeded the 681,280-token threshold. Cloudflare patched the configuration and merged fixes into SGLang PRs #36988 and #38935.

Read the extracted text ↓

On September 8, 2026, we were alerted by a security researcher, Gadi Evron from Knostic, to a tenant isolation issue affecting the GLM-5.3 Workers AI model (@cf/zai-org/glm-5.3) starting on August 27, 2026. We have remediated the issue, and we restored the model on September 9. We have confirmed that no other model hosted on Workers AI was affected.

Between August 27 and September 9, a small number of customers using the GLM 5.3 Workers AI model received responses that contained data from other customers' requests made to the same model shortly before theirs. In total, 155 responses were affected, all to prompts longer than 681,280 tokens.

After excluding our own testing and the researcher's, the requests we identified are consistent in timing and volume with ordinary long-context use, not with someone repeatedly exploiting the bug. We found no evidence of deliberate exploitation.

Today we are publishing this report alongside a post from SGLang, the open-source inference engine that we use, as a coordinated disclosure. This post describes the bug that caused this issue, our response timeline, and the subsequent hardening we have conducted on our production SGLang deployments. We also detail our continued efforts with the upstream maintainers of SGLang to address this and similar bugs.

What happened

GLM is a text generation model from Z.ai that is built for agentic coding workflows; it is one of the many models available through Workers AI. Gadi Evron submitted a report on September 8 through our HackerOne program stating that very long prompts sent to @cf/zai-org/glm-5.3 on Workers AI sometimes returned text that belonged to other users' sessions. Cloudflare reproduced the issue the next morning and took the model offline within 40 minutes of declaring an incident. We then deployed a corrected configuration and a patched inference engine, and restored service the same day.

This specific report of cross-request KV cache exposure was the result of two overlapping issues. The first was a misconfiguration of the open-source inference engine, SGLang, used by Cloudflare. We deployed GLM 5.3 with a 1,048,576-token context window, but the KV cache pool on our prefill GPUs only had room for 681,280 tokens. We were aware of this mismatch before launch. We expected the engine to return an error for any prompt it could not process, and our long-context tests returned successful responses, so we did not believe this to be an issue.

The second was a bug in how SGLang handled the resulting over-length requests in disaggregated prefill/decode mode. A prompt that did not fit the pool was internally marked as rejected and replaced with a one-token placeholder, but instead of aborting the request and returning a client error, prefill generated the KV cache for the placeholder prompt and transferred it to the decode node. The decode node received a "transfer complete" message and assumed that prefill overwrote the KV cache from previous requests with the full prompt for this request, when in fact it left the majority of the previous state intact. The decode node subsequently generated a response while attending over stale cache pages that still held data from earlier requests. As a result, responses to prompts longer than 681,280 tokens could contain data from other users' GLM 5.3 sessions.

Immediate remediation

After disabling the model, we took the following actions to correct this vulnerability before restoring public access to it:

To fix our configuration, we made the pool hold the full context. We adjusted our GLM 5.3 configuration to match our GLM 5.2 configuration, which freed enough GPU memory that the prefill KV pool now holds about 2.04 million tokens, roughly twice the 1,048,576-token context length. A prompt that fits the context window now always fits the pool, so the rejection path is never reached for a valid request. This alone prevents the bug from being exercised.

To fix the engine, we also made the rejection path safe. Relying on pool size alone is fragile: any change to quantization, tensor parallelism, CUDA graph sizes, or a future model version changes the pool size in a way that's easy to miss. Furthermore, there may be other situations in which the prefill scheduler can reject a request and trigger the same bug. To prevent this, we also patched the engine so that a request carrying a pending rejection is retired at the disaggregation admission queue, with its original error, before any bootstrap, allocation, or KV transfer happens. We proposed this upstream as #38704, which also had the decode node verify that the number of tokens transferred matches the number it reserved. The fix was already on SGLang main in #36988, merged September 6 for a related failure path but not yet released. The maintainers also merged #38935, which rejects these requests earlier, following our approach.

We validated both changes against the researcher's reproduction script and our own canary-marker tests before restoring the model.

Background: how we serve GLM 5.3

Workers AI runs GLM 5.3 with SGLang in a disaggregated configuration: a pool of prefill GPUs processes the prompt and produces the model's KV cache for it, then hands that cache over remote direct memory access (RDMA), via Mooncake, to a pool of decode GPUs that generate the response. We have written about this architecture before in our posts on high-performance LLMs and smaller, faster, safer models.

The KV cache in GPU memory is a shared pool of fixed-size pages. Each request is assigned pages when it is admitted and releases them when it finishes. Pages are not currently zeroed between requests; the next request that gets a page simply overwrites it. This approach is common among inference engines and is necessary to achieve the best performance, but it means that the inference engine is responsible for tracking ownership of these memory pages. KV cache page ownership and lifetime tracking has to deal with not just shared ownership within a node (KV cache offload and token generation both access the memory with different lifetimes), but also ownership that is technically shared with the remote prefill node (which holds a simultaneous mutable reference to the page).

Furthermore, the size of this pool is not a configuration value. SGLang sizes it at startup from whatever GPU memory remains after loading the model weights. The model's advertised context length is a separate configuration value. Nothing forces the two to agree, and it is our job to check that they do.

Issue one: misconfiguration

On our GLM 5.3 prefill nodes with our configuration, the pool came to 681,280 tokens while the model's context length is 1,048,576 tokens. The selected KV pool size is printed by SGLang in logs during startup and exposed as a metric. We were aware of this size mismatch; however, during pre-launch testing we observed successful HTTP status codes and a streaming body with no error frames during benchmarking. We assumed that the engine was capable of gracefully handling the difference between the KV cache pool and requested context window via streaming or some other mechanism.

This was one critical gap in our testing: we only validated HTTP response codes and engine performance at long contexts, without validating model output and coherence. We now check KV pool size against context length directly, from the running engine, before any disaggregated deployment change ships, rather than inferring it from response codes.

Issue two: SGLang bug

In v0.5.21, the SGLang team has now addressed this bug in how it was handling prompts longer than the KV pool size. Here’s what was happening before.

When a prompt arrived that was longer than the KV pool could hold, SGLang's scheduler rejected it. The rejection path (Req.set_finish_with_abort) recorded the error to be returned to the client and swapped the prompt for a one-token placeholder so that the request could drain through the normal batch machinery without allocating real memory. On a single-node deployment this worked: the placeholder ran, the batch finished, and the client received an error.

In disaggregated mode such as we operate, the admission queues on both prefill and decode checked whether a request was finished, not whether it was rejected. A rejected request reported itself as live. So:

Bootstrap. The router told the decode node the prompt length (8 pages in the diagram). Decode reserved 8 KV pages for the request.

Prefill admission. The prefill admission queue admitted any request where req.finished() was False. A rejected request stored its rejection in to_finish, not finished_reason, so finished() still returned False. The rejected request was admitted.

Prefill forward pass. Rejection had already replaced the prompt with a one-token placeholder. Prefill ran a trivial forward pass on that one token.

KV transfer. Prefill sent one page of KV to decode and signaled that the transfer was complete.

Decode admission. Decode's admission queue had the same finished() check, so it accepted the handoff.

Decode attention. Decode started generating and attention read all 8 reserved pages. Only page P0 had been written by this request. Pages P1 to P7 still held what earlier requests left in them: other users' prompts and partial generations.

Response. The client got HTTP 200 instead of HTTP 400. prompt_tokens matched the long prompt and the reply was coherent. It arrived faster than an 8-page prefill should take, and it contained content from other users' KV pages.

Every request over the threshold behaved this way. This bug had been reported to SGLang by another user as issue #30233 on July 6, 2026, which Cloudflare noticed while investigating this incident. We had been flagging these types of issues with SGLang since v0.5.10 and this security incident sped up work that Cloudflare had already started in collaboration with the SGLang team. The patch for this issue was released in SGLang v0.5.20, with follow-ups in v0.5.21 and v0.5.22, and is available for all other operators dependent on SGLang to use. We highly recommend all operators update SGLang dependencies to v0.5.22 or later. For more details on specific PRs, read on below.

GLM 5.3 was the only model affected by this bug. Every other disaggregated deployment on Workers AI has KV pools larger than its context length on both prefill and decode, so no request could reach the rejection path. We have applied the engine fix and the hardening described below to every model we serve with SGLang, and our deployment checks require them for every new deployment.

Ongoing hardening effort

This bug belongs to a class of KV cache memory-safety bugs that Cloudflare has been working to address for several months. In each case, a page is freed, reused, or read while something else still has a claim on it. We see two common causes.

The first is page lifetime within a single node. Several tasks on a decode node can hold the same page at once: token generation, offload to host memory, the KV transfer receiver, and the abort and error paths. If one of them frees the page while another is still using it, a new request can be given that page while it is still being read or written. Our earlier fixes for decode offload (#24226, #30899) and double release on grammar errors (#30937) were of this kind.

The second is a race across nodes. When a request is aborted while prefill is still writing its KV cache to decode over RDMA, the writes already sent cannot be recalled. If decode frees those pages right away, those pages can be given to another request, and the late writes land in that request's KV cache. Cloudflare has contributed to the effort to fix this race through proposed fixes (#31357, #38961) and a reproduction script (#32564). In both cases the result is corrupted output, or one user's data in another user's response, with no error raised. The bug in this incident was different: a rejected request went through the handoff every time, rather than by a timing accident. But it had the same result: decode reading pages this request never wrote. None of these covered the failure in this incident in which prefill rejected the request, and decode never learned of it.

Since the incident, we have:

Extended deferred release to hold prefill's source pages as well, and to cover every failure type, not just aborts. We proposed it upstream as #38961, which was superseded by #40645, #41402, and #41404.

Deployed bounded Mooncake transfer batches (#32758, from the SGLang community), so no single transfer call can outlast the release timeout.

Changed our load balancer so a client disconnect no longer aborts a request mid-transfer. It holds the prefill connection until prefill finishes and the decode connection until the first token, then closes both.

Added a check to every disaggregated deployment change: KV pool size measured against context length, deferred release on both roles, transfer batch bounds inside the release timeout, and all required patches applied.

All of these changes were released to our disaggregated production deployments.

What we recommend to other operators

If you run SGLang in disaggregated prefill/decode mode, we recommend the following:

Measure your KV pool and compare it to your context length, on every prefill and decode node, from the engine's startup log, after every change to weights, quantization, parallelism, or memory fraction. If the pool is smaller, lower the context length to fit. Do not rely on a proxy to enforce the limit, and do not assume the engine will reject what does not fit. This is the check we did not enforce.

Upgrade to a build that includes #36988 and #38935 (v0.5.22 or later), so an over-length request is rejected before handoff regardless of pool size. This is the engine-side fix, and it is the backstop for the day your check in step one is wrong.

Run with deferred KV release on both roles (default after #41023), and bound your Mooncake transfer batches so a single timed-out call cannot leave an unbounded tail of writes.

Turn on corruption detection where your model supports it. --disaggregation-enable-kv-checksum and --kv-canary do not yet cover every model architecture. Check your logs to confirm requests are being verified. We are working with the SGLang team to extend both checks to more architectures, including GLM 5.3, GLM 5.3 Flash, and DeepSeek V4.

Ensure your proxy is not generating unnecessary aborts. Configure your load balancer to allow requests to proceed up to the first token, so that you do not trigger an abort that requires cross-node coordination.

Longer term

Integrity by construction

Configuration checks and lifecycle fixes reduce the number of ways a page can be misused. They do not make misuse impossible, and this incident is a reminder that the next bug will have a shape we have not seen. The durable answer is for the engine to detect, at read time, that a request is about to attend over a page it does not own, and fail that one request rather than return someone else's data.

We built a prototype of this over the summer and described it in August. Every physical KV page carries a tag that changes when the page is reallocated, each request records the tags it expects, and the mapping is checked before decode reads the cache. In our tests it cost under 1% in throughput and tail latency. We tested it on GLM 5.2 in mid-August, but disabled it shortly afterwards due to reliability issues.

The prototype was also tied to specific attention backends and model architectures, so it could not be a general fix. We are working with the SGLang team to build a production version into the engine that works across every model, rather than maintaining Cloudflare-specific patches. The per-transfer checksum in #39500 is a first step: it validates the handoff from prefill to decode. Page-level tags go further, because they check every read, including reads long after the transfer.

Integrity by mathematical impossibility

RDMA copies data directly from one node’s memory to another’s. It’s vital for the two machines to agree on where the data goes, how large it is, and when it’s finished; any mismatch can cause the receiver to read the wrong data. This bug is an example of a consistency failure in a distributed system, in which the two machines had inconsistent views of the state of that system.

Elsewhere in Cloudflare, we have put effort into proving the safety of distributed systems using formal verification methods. Topaz enables conflict-free IP selection for our authoritative DNS servers. Models of Meerkat’s QuePaxa implementation have demonstrated consistency bugs that were subsequently fixed.

We are now directing our experience in formal methods to KV cache transfer engines. This will start with modeling and proving the safety of an RDMA-based KV cache transfer protocol. The goal is to model our production SGLang’s distributed KV cache system using Mooncake, then track and fix any divergence from the safety properties we want to guarantee. The aim is to prove — as far as possible — that a bug like this cannot occur again. Realistically, we expect gaps in what the provers can tell us, but proving the rest of the system allows us to more closely review and monitor those gaps.

Abstract:Multi-server MCP agents create an information-flow control (IFC) problem: faithful tool composition can turn individually benign read/write permissions into cross-boundary credential propagation -- a structural side effect of workflow topology, not necessarily malicious model behavior. Specialized CNN beats GPT-6 and Claude on skin cancer diagnosis
Why: Evidence of LLM visual limits for your tooling decisions.
Hacker News ↗HN 660↑extracted 06:50 · 1,205 words

I am a dermatologist. I tested the ability of GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1 to diagnosis skin cancer and found that they were less accurate than I expected. I compared to a 10-year old CNN and to my surprise found that this was superior.

This is a melanoma.

Figure 1. Invasive melanoma on the ear, under 0.8 mm thick. Both LLMs called it a seborrhoeic keratosis and said it could be discharged. The CNN called it a melanoma. Clinical photograph on the left, dermoscopic image on the right. Images: MILK10k, MILK study team, CC BY-NC.

I showed these two images - a clinical photograph and a dermoscopic (skin microscope) image - to GPT-6 Astra and Claude Fable 5.1, two of the most capable AI models available in October 2026. Both said it was a seborrhoeic keratosis, a harmless warty growth, and that the patient could be discharged. I then showed them to fine-tuned version of Inception v3, a convolutional neural network (CNN) first published by Google in 2015 and fine-tuned here on public dermatology images. The CNN got the right answer based on the dermoscopy view.

It was one of 200 biopsy-proven lesions I gave to all three. The CNN named the right diagnosis (out of 6 possiblities) for 125 of them (62%). The two frontier LLMs named it for 84 and 85 (42%). A ten-year-old network, fine-tuned for about twenty minutes, beat both of them at recognising skin cancer.

The test#

The lesions come from MILK10k, a public dataset released through the International Skin Imaging Collaboration in 2025, in which each lesion is photographed twice: a clinical close-up and a dermoscopic image taken through the illuminated magnifying lens dermatologists use. I drew 200 lesions at random, all confirmed by histopathology:

100 that should not be discharged: 30 melanomas, 25 basal cell carcinomas, 25 squamous cell carcinomas (including Bowen’s disease) and 20 actinic keratoses.

100 that are safe to discharge: 50 benign naevi (moles) and 50 seborrhoeic keratoses.

Every lesion was biopsied, including the benign ones, so these are harder to diagnose: a mole that somebody decided to cut out is one that worried a clinician.

Basal cell carcinoma on the trunk of a 75-year-old man. All three models answered correctly.

Invasive melanoma under 0.8 mm thick, with very little pigment. GPT-6 Astra called it a basal cell carcinoma with 95% confidence. Claude Fable 5.1 and the CNN called it a melanoma.

Seborrhoeic keratosis on the leg. Both LLMs called it an actinic keratosis and the CNN a squamous cell carcinoma: all three were wrong, though all three would have kept it rather than discharge it.

Figure 2. Three more of the 200 test lesions. Clinical photograph on the left, dermoscopic image on the right. Images: MILK10k, MILK study team, CC BY-NC.

The LLMs were given both images and nothing else, and asked, as a clinician might use them, for the most likely of the six diagnoses (or “unsure”), a confidence, a second choice, and whether the lesion could be safely discharged with no treatment, biopsy or follow-up. Both models got the same prompt at default settings, and neither refused a case.

The CNN is deliberately not state of the art. I used Inception v3 pretrained on everyday photographs and then fine-tuned all layers on 68,011 dermoscopic and clinical images from the ISIC Archive on a single H100 GPU. These 200 test lesions, and every other photograph of the same lesion or patient, were removed from its data (including the validation set) before training, so it had never seen them, and they were not used to choose the model or set its threshold. It scored both images of each lesion, combined them with most of the weight on the dermoscopic image, and chose from the same six diagnoses.

You can try the CNN yourself. Remarkably the model is small enough to run entirely in a phone browser using WebGPU. Nothing is uploaded, and its random sample button draws from these same 200 lesions.

Diagnosis: 62% against 42%#

Figure 3. Correct first-choice diagnosis for the two LLMs and the CNN, which chose from the same six diagnoses. Labels show correct answers out of the number of lesions with that histology.

The CNN was right more often overall and on most diagnoses. Allowing a second choice, it reached 173 of 200 against 134 and 138. It named 13 of the 30 melanomas against 8 and 11. The biggest gap was on the harmless lesions: it recognised 37 of 50 moles and 26 of 50 seborrhoeic keratoses, against 16 and 13 for GPT-6 Astra and 33 and 6 for Claude Fable 5.1. It was one short of GPT-6 Astra on basal cell carcinoma and behind both LLMs on actinic keratosis.

The models also failed differently. Each LLM had a favourite answer: GPT-6 Astra called 75 lesions basal cell carcinoma when 25 were, including 14 of the 30 melanomas, and Claude Fable 5.1 called 53 lesions actinic keratosis when 20 were. The CNN’s errors on melanoma went the other way: of the 17 it got wrong, it called 8 a naevus and 6 a seborrhoeic keratosis.

Caveats#

Small numbers. Two hundred lesions are enough to show the pattern but not to rank close results, and 30 melanomas give wide uncertainty on the figure that matters most. Each LLM case was run once; answers vary between runs.

Hard cases. Every lesion was biopsied. On ordinary images from the archive the CNN separated lesions that should and should not be discharged far better (area under the ROC curve 0.96, against 0.86 here).

Contamination. MILK10k has been public since 2025, so the LLMs could have seen it in training; if anything that would have helped them. The CNN certainly had not.

Not state of the art. Inception v3 is a baseline. State of the art skin cancer diagnosis AI uses ensembles of images transformers trained on more data, and the regulated products built on them perform considerably better.

Asked as a patient#

Asked as a patient instead, with the clinical photograph alone and the question “do I need to see a doctor?”, both LLMs advised seeing a doctor in almost every case, harmless lesions included: for 99 and 98 of a further 100 biopsy-proven melanomas.

Conclusion#

A small, old, specialised network using an outdated architecture recognises skin lesions far better than two frontier LLMs.

The cases, prompts, every LLM reply and the scoring code are in one download, and the CNN is here.
