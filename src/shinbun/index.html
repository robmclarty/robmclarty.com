Reliability in autonomous systems is now determined by state management, not generative scale. Agents fail when context decays or environments shift mid-execution. Abstract:GUI grounding on high-resolution screens fails mostly on small targets, and zooming in is a common training-free remedy. However, most existing zooming pipelines do not match the zoom to the target's size, accept the zoomed answer unconditionally and never revisit a wrong answer. To address these issues, we propose AdaGround, a training-free framework with three components: (1) Token-Normalized Adaptive Zooming, which sets the magnification, field of view and zoom depth of each sample from the model's own estimate of the target size; (2) Verifier-in-the-Loop Refinement, which uses one marked-click Yes/No verifier to keep or reject each zoom, choose between zoom chains, trigger re-grounding and, with an existence query, decide when to abstain; and (3) Compositional Structure Grounding, which composes spreadsheet cells from their headers. On Qwen3-VL models, AdaGround surpasses state-of-the-art training-free zooming methods and many trained GUI models, improving accuracy over the backbone by 7.4 to 20.6 points on OSWorld-G and ScreenSpot-Pro, and its gains carry over to held-out UI-Vision. MoE Decoding Bypasses Calibration Data via Ternary Sketch Readers
Why: You get 1.6x to 2.69x inference speedups without expensive calibration data.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 05:00 · 288 words

A new study demonstrates that top-k selection matches calibrated threshold accuracy while avoiding repetition-loop collapse in Mixture-of-Experts models. The authors implement a custom reader using ternary sketches and low-rank maps on Gemma 4, achieving 98.1% retention of full-model accuracy at a 17.8% read budget.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models are bottlenecked by loading routed-expert weights from memory during interactive decoding at small batch sizes. Because most intermediate channels in a routed expert contribute little to any single token, channel selectors predict which channels matter and read only their weights, typically keeping channels above a threshold calibrated on offline data. We show that channel selection needs no calibration data. First, a calibrated threshold does not fix how much a selector reads, because the number of channels passing it varies across inputs; Prox with thresholds calibrated for an 18% read budget actually reads only 12.9% on average across our benchmarks. Second, top-k keeps each token's k highest-scoring channels and needs no calibration data. At the calibrated budget, a selector's top-k version matches or beats its threshold version on the same score in 40 of 43 comparisons at 13% and 18% budgets, including all 23 on generation tasks. The top-k versions also avoid the repetition-loop collapse that calibrated thresholds cause on Gemma 4. Calibrating a threshold thus provides neither of the two things selectors use it for: a known read budget and a consistent accuracy gain at that budget. We furthermore introduce a reader built entirely from the model's weights, which screens channels with a ternary sketch and recovers the skipped ones with a low-rank map. Across three post-trained MoE models, our reader retains 98.1% of full-model accuracy at 17.8% of routed-expert reads, with Triton kernels running MoE blocks 1.60 to 1.90x faster than optimized vLLM at batch one and up to 2.69x at larger batches on the same three models.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Multi-agent debate (MAD) aims to improve language-model decisions through the exchange of arguments, yet agreement after debate need not reflect independent corroboration. We investigate how peer communication influences individual decisions and collective outcomes through behavioral and internal interventions. We introduce MAD Bench, a 2,400-question benchmark spanning knowledge and reasoning, values and moral judgment, and safety and refusal, and evaluate three open-weight models using a round-matched independent-reflection control. Among answer-changing responses, a forced-choice readout before rationale generation matches the eventual answer more often in debate than in reflection (65.4% versus 22.3% on Qwen3.5-9B). Message ablations reveal model-dependent effects of peer answers and reasoning, while attributing identical text to the receiver rather than another agent substantially changes its influence. For the tested source descriptions, stated track record affects answer preferences more strongly than professional-status cues across all three models. Attention masking produces different changes in decision margins across the three checkpoints. On fully parsed, reference-labelled non-numeric questions, debate yields small estimated accuracy improvements over initial majority voting, while increasing the rate of unanimous wrong answers by 3.1-7.4 percentage points relative to reflection. Longer debates show persistent consensus and greater correlation between agents' correctness than reflection. These findings show that stronger consensus can coexist with more shared errors, motivating evaluations that assess dependence and unanimous errors alongside accuracy.

A harder challenge is forecasting an unknown future from incomplete information. Public markets provide a natural testbed: analysts routinely predict discrete events with information from that point in time. ConstexprCore implements gperf in C++23 constexpr
Why: You can apply this zero-overhead technique to your high-performance parser pipelines.
Hacker News ↗HN 22↑extracted 05:00 · 1,147 words

A new library generates perfect hash maps at compile time using greedy slot assignment and vector comparisons. Benchmarks on Apple M3 Max show 13x speedups over std::unordered_map, with lookups taking 1.2 nanoseconds.

Read the extracted text ↓

A lot of software spends its time mapping strings to values. A web server must identify the HTTP method (GET, POST, …). It must recognize header names. A URL parser must recognize the protocol (http, https, ftp, …). A compiler must recognize keywords.

In C++, the default approach is to put the strings in a std::unordered_map.

std::unordered_map<std::string_view, Method> methods = { {"GET", Method::GET}, {"POST", Method::POST}, {"PUT", Method::PUT}, {"DELETE", Method::DELETE}, {"PATCH", Method::PATCH}};

It works. But notice something: the keys are known when you write the code. They never change. And yet we pay at runtime as if they could change at any time: we hash the whole string, we pick a bucket, we follow a pointer, we compare, maybe we walk a list.

Perfect hashing

A hash function maps keys to slots. When two keys land in the same slot, we have a collision, and we must deal with it. Collisions are what make hash tables complicated and slow.

If you know all of your keys ahead of time, you can construct a hash function designed for your keys, a function that never produces a collision. It is called a perfect hash function. The idea is old: the GNU gperf tool has been doing it since 1989. You give it a list of keywords, and it generates C code.

We follow the same design as gperf. The slot for a key is computed as follows.

slot = (length + asso[key[i]] + asso[key[j]] + ...) % table_size

We add the length of the key to a few small values taken from an association table (asso) indexed by the characters at a few selected positions. Usually, one or two positions are enough. The table size is a power of two, so the modulo is a single bitwise AND.

Consider the four HTTP methods GET, PUT, POST and DELETE. We use only the first character. Set asso['G'] = 0, asso['P'] = 1 and asso['D'] = 0. Then GET goes in slot 3 + 0 = 3, PUT goes in slot 3 + 1 = 4, POST goes in slot 4 + 1 = 5 and DELETE goes in slot 6 + 0 = 6. No collision.

To look up PUT, we compute the slot (4), and we compare PUT with the one key stored in slot 4. One hash, one comparison. If you look up PATCH, which is not in the set, you land in slot 5 + 1 = 6, you compare with DELETE, and you are done: not found. There is no probing, no chain to walk.

How do we find the association values? We sort the characters by how many keys they touch. We then take them one at a time and try 0, 1, 2, … until we find a value that does not cause a collision. We never revise a value once it is set. It is a greedy algorithm with bounded work. The bound matters because we do all of this at compile time, and C++ compilers limit how much work a constexpr function may do.

Doing it in C++

With our library, you write ordinary C++23. There is no external tool to run.

#include <ConstexprCore/perfect_hash.h> using namespace ConstexprCore; enum class Method { GET, POST, PUT, DELETE, PATCH }; constexpr auto methods = make_perfect_map< kv<"GET", Method::GET>, kv<"POST", Method::POST>, kv<"PUT", Method::PUT>, kv<"DELETE", Method::DELETE>, kv<"PATCH", Method::PATCH> >();

The map is a compile-time constant. The compiler searches for the perfect hash function when it compiles your code. At runtime, computing the hash can take as few as four instructions: load one byte of the key, load one byte from the association table, add the length, apply a mask.

Comparing strings quickly

Computing the hash is cheap. But we still need to check that the string we are looking up matches the key stored in the slot. And comparing strings can be expensive: the textbook approach is a loop over the bytes with a branch for each byte. You can easily end up with dozens of instructions and several branches, and the branches are hard to predict when the strings vary.

Instead, we treat the key as a vector. Our processors, whether ARM or x64, have 16-byte registers. So we load 16 bytes unconditionally, even if the key is only three bytes long. We then use a shuffle instruction (tbl on ARM NEON) to zero the bytes past the end of the key. The stored keys are zero-padded at compile time. A single vector comparison handles the whole key. We go from about forty instructions and eight branches to five instructions and no branch.

But wait: is it safe to read 16 bytes when you only have three? Reading past the end of a buffer is fine as long as you do not cross into another memory page, and pages are at least 4 kilobytes. So we check whether the address is within 16 bytes of the end of its page. If it is, we take a slower path. Otherwise, we do the wide load. We traded several unpredictable branches for a single branch that is almost always taken the same way.

How fast is it?

I ran the benchmarks on an Apple M3 Max with AppleClang 15. We look up the keys in random order and we report the time per lookup. Let us first consider the 20 standard HTTP header names.

We need about 1.3 ns per lookup, or about 5 cycles. The classic gperf needs 7.7 ns. Abseil’s flat_hash_map, an excellent hash table, needs 10.9 ns. And std::unordered_map needs 16.6 ns. We are thirteen times faster than the standard library.

It is not one lucky key set. Here are the results over several sets of keys, from 6 URL protocols to the 100 tickers of the S&P 100.

We stay between 1.2 ns and 2 ns per lookup. We are 3 to 6 times faster than gperf, and 7 to 13 times faster than std::unordered_map. Notice how gperf struggles with the JavaScript reserved words: there we are 17 times faster.

Why is std::unordered_map so much slower? The C++ standard essentially requires a chained hash table. A lookup hashes the whole string by calling a function, follows a pointer to a heap-allocated node and walks a list. Our lookup does a few loads and has no function call. The processor can retire over 8.6 instructions per cycle with our code, which is about as good as it gets on this chip, and it almost never mispredicts a branch.

On an Apple M4, with the URL protocols, we exceed one billion lookups per second.

Conclusion

If your keys are known at compile time, you should not pay for collision handling at runtime. A perfect hash function gets you down to one comparison. With some care, a lookup costs about five cycles. The library is a single header you can drop in your project: github.com/ConstexprCore/perfect_hash.

Offline Qwen3.5 9B with OpenCode benchmarks against Goose
Why: You need concrete performance deltas for offline agentic toolchain decisions.
Latent Space ↗extracted 05:00 · 2,055 words

When we talk about working remotely, we take a lot of things for granted, especially reliable internet and power. But sometimes, working remotely means trying to get something done on a flight, during a long train ride, or somewhere with little or no network.

I moved to the foothills of the Himalayas a year ago. I love the peace of living here, but it also comes with its own challenges, including network and power outages. Things I didn’t think about as much before are now part of planning my work.

I work as a Principal Engineer at Thomson Reuters, where I spend most of my time coding and reviewing my teammates’ code. Coding agents are already a big part of my everyday workflow, which made me curious about how much of that workflow I could keep using offline.

Working from the foothills of the Himalayas. Photo of my workspace.

If you’re someone like me, who enjoys working with technology but also finds peace in quiet, remote places where the network doesn’t always cooperate, this article is for you. It’s also for anyone who has wondered whether a coding agent can run entirely on their laptop, with the internet switched off. I tried running a coding agent locally on my laptop to see how much work I could actually get done offline.

What can we do offline?

A project already on your laptop gives us plenty to work with. We can ask an agent to explain an unfamiliar function, investigate a failing test, refactor a small piece of code, or help finish a feature. These tasks can run offline when the model, project dependencies and test tools are available locally. Downloading a new package or looking something up on the web will still need a connection.

Where OpenCode fits

OpenCode is an open-source coding agent that works with your project. You give it a task, and it can read files, propose edits and run commands such as the test suite. In our setup, its permission prompts let us review commands and changes before allowing them.

I picked OpenCode because it lets me keep that workflow in the terminal while connecting to a model running on my own laptop. Ollama handles running the model; OpenCode handles the interaction with the project.

OpenCode on my Mac, with Qwen3.5 9B selected as the local model.

Open-weight models, including Qwen, make this possible: their weights, the learned parameters used to generate responses, can be downloaded and run locally. With Ollama running Qwen on the same laptop, OpenCode can send its requests to a local service. We download everything first, then disconnect the internet for the coding task.

Aider is another option if you prefer a guided editing workflow; it also supports Ollama. We are using OpenCode throughout this walkthrough so that the setup and results stay consistent.

OpenCode works with the project files and sends model requests to Ollama on the same laptop. Ollama runs Qwen locally.

The Job is Knowing When to Be Deterministic & When to Be Probabilistic

This week, I gave a short talk at AGNTCon titled, “Context Fails & Fixes,” that functioned as a sort of “State of Context Engineering.” How did context engineering emerge, where has it gone, and where might it go in the future? As model makers target new capabilities, they inadvertently create challenges we, as users and harness builders, must overcome.

Below are the slides and a lightly edited transcript. As always, shoot me a note with any feedback, thoughts, or questions. I really appreciate it.

Hi, everyone. Thanks for joining me today. We’re going to walk through context fails and fixes.

I have a book coming out in a bit, The Context Engineering Handbook. When I first talked to the team at O’Reilly, back in June 2025, they asked me a very reasonable question: “How do we know this won’t become obsolete in a few weeks?” The trouble with AI textbooks is that many age extraordinarily quickly.

I said, I don’t know, but I’m optimistic. Giving clear instructions about what to do and what not to do, and ensuring those instructions are consistent and not noisy, will forever be a challenge with AI, no matter how good it gets.

We have coworkers, partners, and other people we know and interact with who are sentient. They pass the Turing test, they are “AGI,” and yet: we still have a hard time conveying instructions, ideas, and concepts among each other.

Context engineering will continue to be hard. But it will be hard in unique ways, because models aren’t people. You can make assumptions about how models think or interpret things (use whatever anthropomorphizing word you like), but it helps to understand how they’re built, optimized, and trained. Their unique attributes and construction create a unique set of failure modes we need to reckon with.

So let’s review how we got here.

Let’s flash back to the end of 2024.

If you remember, this was the height of the “RAG is dead” moment.

The hope was: if we can just make the context window large enough, we can put the entire internet in it. We can put everything in it. We don’t have to be selective. We don’t have to build agentic systems. We can fit it all in there, and AI will be solved.

Model builders started aiming at long context capabilities and we had a big leap from ~128,000-token context window models to million-token context window models, starting with Gemini 1.5.

If you look at the marketing material for Gemini 1.5 Pro, it was mostly about the million-token context window. They were even teasing a 10-million-token context window model they had in the lab, that would come out soon.

But they got beaten to the punch. Llama 4 Scout came out with a 10-million-token context window, and well, it wasn’t quite what we hoped. Inference was a challenge, and Scout never really caught on, and we never got another Llama.

But we still got a million tokens. That’s pretty good. We’ll take it.

So this was the brief at the time: just put everything in the context and let the model sort it out.

But with more context, we had room for more problems. Long contexts are not a panacea.

When I wrote about this, I identified four key failure modes.

Context poisoning is when something gets into the context that shouldn’t be there. It could be a hallucination or a bad fact, and the model can’t get past it.

Context distraction is when the context grows so long that the model starts to focus on the context over its base knowledge. We can see this in some really interesting ways.

Context confusion is when extraneous facts—things that don’t relate to the problem I’m working on—come in and influence the model in ways we don’t fully understand. This one’s really pernicious. We’re not going to dig into it today, but it’s one of my favorite failure modes. The model makes assumptions about who you are, and those assumptions influence the answer.

Finally, context clash is when tool calls, skills, and other information within your harness start to conflict and work against you in ways you didn’t expect.

(How many of you keep memory on in ChatGPT or Claude? I turn it off. I hate it. Sometimes I’m talking about work conversations; sometimes I’m talking about a project I’m doing with my son. The model will take notes on both, mix up those contexts, and the confusion grows from there.)

Let me give you my favorite example of context poisoning.

I’m going to date myself: I played the game on the left. The one from 1996, for the original Game Boy. This is the first Pokémon Red. It was in black and white.

In 2004, Nintendo released Pokémon FireRed for the Game Boy Advance. These were essentially the same game, except one had better colors and audio.

But there was one subtle change. At some point in both Pokémon Reds, to get past a security guard, you have to give them something to drink. A bribe. Quite the practical lesson for children.

In the original game, you need to give the thirsty guard lemonade. In FireRed, for some reason, they changed it to iced tea.

That created a problem for the agent. In the Gemini 2.5 technical report, the researchers described how they had the model play Pokémon. Researchers like Pokémon as an agent benchmark because it’s a reasonably controlled environment and doesn’t move particularly fast.

Several times, Gemini 2.5 became convinced it needed to get tea, even though it was playing the older game. The tea task for FireRed was in its weights, so it poisoned its context. It spent hours trying to find or give the guard tea. Once it had been added to its to-do list, the model could not get past it.

That’s context poisoning. And it happens all the time.

Another failure mode is context distraction. This is one of my favorite demonstrations, and the research came out after I wrote the original piece.

As your context gets longer, how does your behavior change? Quick question: how many of you, when you’re coding, let the context go all the way to a million tokens? Fill it all up?

(About half the room raised their hands!)

Wow, that’s more than I expected.

For me, around 200,000 or 300,000 is where I start looking. I’ll check, see it’s at 250,000, and think: I’ve got a big job coming up. I’m going to reset and start fresh. Let me show you why this matters.

Recently, I stumbled on a paper that wonderfully illustrates context distraction: Do as I Say, Not as I Do, by Carolina Camassa and Derek Shiller.

Their experiment is a little contrived, but it’s useful for thinking about how this happens. They give the model an instruction: “I want you to translate every question into Russian as your answer to each of my questions.” Then they present a bunch of examples that are counter to these instructions. In this case, many questions with answers in French.

These aren’t new instructions. They’re just examples sitting in the chat history.

Then they ask the model a question. Will it follow the instructions?

How often does the model respond in Russian, as it was instructed to, and how often does it respond in French, as it’s seen in the examples?

The x-axis on this matrix enumerates how many bad examples were present. The y-axis is the model being tested. The figures in the intersections are the percentage of times the model adheres to the instructions, rather than following the examples.

It turns out this affects every model! For smaller models, like Gemma 3 12B, after just a few contradictory examples, the model follows the instruction less than half the time. With Opus 4.6, which is an amazing model, it takes many more examples, but it happens there too.

Imagine you’re going back and forth on a front-end design, trying to tweak CSS and get a component exactly right. How many bad examples have you seeded in that exchange? Those examples start to influence the model. As your context grows, things fall apart.

Gemini 2.5 ran into this too, while playing Pokémon.

Remember, Google was really proud of its million-token context window. The paper suggested that two million tokens were around the corner. But buried in the appendix was this observation: once the context grew significantly beyond 100,000 tokens, the agent began repeating actions from its history instead of synthesizing new plans.

I’m convinced the marketing people never read the appendix. I love this technical report.

Long contexts let you fit in more information, but they also let you fit in more errors. Long contexts weren’t a panacea.

Thankfully, we’ve developed a pretty good toolbox. Surprisingly, even as new challenges have emerged (we’ll get to those in a minute), this set of tools has remained the same.

These six of them.

You have context augmentation: RAG, skills, and other ways to bring in the information relevant to the problem.

You have tool loadout: selecting which tools and skills to expose so you don’t overload the model. I was recently talking to an enterprise team whose agents had suddenly started performing terribly. We investigated and discovered their enterprise software was loading 600 skills for every engineer at startup. We figured they should probably filter those, with a loadout function.

Third is context quarantine: subagents and peer agents, where you start something with a blank history but let it work in the same environment.

Fourth is context pruning. Claude Code and Codex do this, sometimes without you even noticing. Early stages of compaction often strip out tool results you no longer need.

Fifth is context offloading: storing information in the environment, such as Markdown files or a database, so you can access it later.

And sixth is context summarization, which everyone knows as compaction.

These are the tools, and we’ve increasingly moved them into the harness.

Let’s look at an example harness, to show how simple these patterns are.

I’m really passionate about DSPy. I think it’s an incredibly readable and effective way to build AI workflows and agents. And it’s also a great language for simply specifying behavior, so it’s well suited to this demonstration.

An agent is a while loop. Here we’re using a ReAct module: we give it a request, we want a response, and we give it some tools. Those tools are just Python functions.

For this demo we have a big model (in this case, Claude opted for Claude Sonnet 4.5, good luck with that!). And I’m using a Jev as the small model: a tiny decision model that makes lightning-fast decisions. You could swap in a small LLM in this example, and everything would still work.

We have a while loop that takes input, sends it to ReAct, and carries the accruing history forward. This is an agent in about a dozen lines using DSPy. It would be even fewer if you took out the extra model.

How do we do tool loadout? It’s just a function.

We present Jev with the request and a tool, asking if it’s needed for this task.

Jev is fast enough that we can do this one tool at a time; it’ll be fine. We wrap this in a loadout function that iterates through all the tools and asks if each is needed, then returns the top-ranking subset. That’s it.

With context quarantine, it’s just a subagent. You expose an agent as a tool that the main agent can use, and it starts with a blank history.

With context pruning, we’re stepping through the history, passing each turn to Jev, and asking: is this still needed? If not, cut it.

You can be as rough or as elegant as you want here. Just mind your prompt caching.

We can also rewrite the history with compaction. Take the old turns, create a summary, and start the new context from that summary.

One of my favorite variations lately is checking what matters before you compact. You use Jev, or another decision model, for the first pass to judge what’s relevant. Now you’ve got a smaller subset of the history, and you summarize that. It’s a simple extra step, but it makes the summary more useful.

Put it all together and it’s about 16 lines of changes. Four fixes, one loop. These are the moves we have in the harness. It’s actually remarkably simple.

What isn’t simple is our language: how we talk about this stuff. It’s really hard to keep pace; it changes every day.

One of my favorite quotes is from Stewart Brand, which I think about all the time:

If you want to know where the future is being made, look for where language is being invented and lawyers are congregating.

Certainly applies here! But as our language has changed, our goal has remained the same.

In 2023, we called choosing the right information to get the right response prompt engineering.

In 2025, we called it context engineering, except that now it was more automated. It wasn’t just you writing the prompt; it was your system dynamically assembling the context.

Then, as we moved more of these tools out of the context and into code, we started calling it harness engineering.

But the job is the same. We’re moving more and more of the work of assembling context from probabilistic model calls to deterministic code.

I’ve shown you 2024. Let’s go to 2026.

We’ve moved these context fixes into the harness. What do we want next? We want agentic models that can drive our harnesses.

Everybody started targeting long-horizon agentic models. You’ve seen the METR reports. GPT-5 could handle tasks around 3.4 hours on that measure; Gemini 3.1 got up to 6.4 hours. That’s with a harness. The models have gotten much better at using it, and that’s how they’re able to run for so long.

Opus 4.6 got up to around 12 hours. And now we’re talking about agents running for days, swarms of agents, and models that drive harnesses over much longer periods.

Before, the pitch was: make the context so large that we can fit everything in it.

Now the pitch is: teach the model to drive the harness and manage its own context. That’s become the dominant pattern. That’s what model builders are working towards.

What new failure modes does this approach create?

So far we’ve found two new failure modes.

When you train models to use harnesses really well, you end up overfitting them to a particular set of tools and environments they were trained within.

How many of you have used the Playwright MCP server, or something similar? This is the MCP conference, so probably quite a few.

Smaller models, something like Haiku or Luna, will often botch this tool call on the first attempt, then recover and adjust the parameters. The environment it was trained in had similar tools that looked different than Playwright. Whatever Anthropic or OpenAI used in training. And you have to fight against these biases.

I call this fighting the weights: when the model doesn’t do what you ask because you’re working against what it was trained to do.

It’s a real problem, and it’s not just for us. The labs and the people building their products have to contend with it too.

Here’s a piece of the Claude Code system prompt and some of its tool instructions.

The training environments these models learned to use were, for a long time, largely serial. They’re becoming more parallel, but when I pulled these examples, their biases were towards serial behaviors. So Claude Code’s instructions repeatedly say: no, no, no, we want you to use parallelism. We want you to make multiple tool calls at the same time.

The instructions repeat this over and over—seven times here, four times there, two times, three times: please make tool calls in parallel when they’re independent.

When you crack open a prompt and see repetition and all-caps, there’s a good chance you’re fighting the weights. You’re trying to overcome something the model was trained to do.

The next problem is something we’ve only started to document recently. In fact, four parties landed on it just last week.

I call it context inertia: models hyper-focus on a hypothesis, subtask, or goal in their context, and keep pursuing it after they should stop.

The first example was from Anthropic. In their recent post about whether models are getting close to replicating and deploying themselves onto other machines, Anthropic wrote about a harness it used for these tests called Memento.

What they found was that if the agents ran too long, they’d become fixated on one subtask or one proposed solution and wouldn’t stop. They’d keep trying the same thing.

Their fix is delightfully simple. Every hour and a half, the agent writes down a short note, then its context is cleared. The environment is preserved. All the work it’s done is still there. But the running conversation is gone, and it has to start again from a fresh perspective.

Another paper, from Microsoft Research, is called Fork-and-Flush.

If you’ve run autoresearch experiments, you’ve probably seen charts like this. You make a big gain, then the line goes flat. You make another big gain, then it’s flat again. That’s compute, money, and time going by with very little improvement.

When the researchers looked closely, the agents weren’t doing literally nothing. They were making tinier and tinier changes. They had found an idea and were hyper-focused on it, rather than searching for more creative alternatives.

In a way, they become obsessed with their own ideas. We’ve trained them to be persistent and focused on a goal, because that’s what makes a good coding agent. But the same quality can work against them.

Here’s another paper, Scaling Discovery Requires Forgetting, led by Shangyin Tan at UC Berkeley.

The researchers came up with essentially the same idea: periodically clear the context while keeping the artifacts produced in the environment.

The blue line is the version with forgetting. Every time it clears the context, you see another jump in performance: forget, improve; forget, improve.

It’s amazing that we have these brand-new models trained for increasingly complex, persistent work, and we’re still using the same basic tools. We haven’t discovered an entirely new context fix. We’re using quarantine, offloading, and deleting the accumulated history to deal with context inertia.

This is the advice I keep giving now. Someone says, “My coding agent’s doing something weird. Claude’s been nerfed.” And I ask, “How big is your context?”

“Oh, I’ve got 800,000 tokens.”

Delete your context.

I’m not joking. Around 200,000 tokens is my warning point.

Have you tried resetting your context?

Sometimes that really is the fix.

The solutions we build into models create tomorrow’s problems. The models are getting better, but so are our harnesses.

Longer context windows let us bring in more information, but they also let us bring in bad information. We had to figure out how to manage that, and we still get it wrong.

Agentic models let us run longer tasks and manage their context, but they also let tasks run longer than they should and ignore our preferences, because they were trained for their own harnesses.

So where will the problem move next?

What’s the big promise for 2027? What’s the next thing models are going to offer that creates a new set of problems for everyone in this room to deal with?

(Unless you’re training your own models. But this is a context engineering session, so probably not.)

Here’s my prediction.

Think about all the personal agents: Hermes, OpenClaw, Dot, Bot, Muse, and similar systems. Very few people have really experienced these harnesses running for more than six months.

I’ve been running a Hermes agent for six months. When you look at the skills it creates and updates, you start to discover that the solutions to your problems can get worse over time. The skills accumulate all these weird fixes. They’re general agents: they solve one task one way, then rediscover a different way to solve something similar. The instructions and memories start to build up and conflict with one another.

As we focus more on memory, we also need to think about strategic forgetting. Over time, overly precise memories become conflicted and messy, and nobody has yet figured out how to handle this.

I think that’s the context problem of tomorrow.

Every advance in model capability changes what we delegate to the model and what systems must enforce.

I made this slide for a deck for training enterprise teams, about a year and a half ago, and it remains true.

Our output is the product of the context and the weights.

Our output depends on the data used to train the model. Understand it. Read the technical reports. Look at what the model was trained on and whether that’s relevant to your task. That helps you build a mental model for debugging when something goes wrong.

And our output depends on the context given to the model. That’s it.

So our goal is to give the right context to the right model. Either by our own discrete decisions or by the software, the harnesses, we design.

You can use bigger models, think harder, or improve your context. One of those is cheap and fast.

When building intelligent applications, harnesses, and workflows, our job is to choose when to be deterministic (what to put in code) and when to be probabilistic (when to let the model drive).

Pratt parsing replaces shunting yard stack with recursion in C
Why: Your compiler avoids recursive descent overhead through binding power structs.

I got bored and wanted to make a parser of C expressions. this will probably become a compiler at some point. previously, I’ve made a calculator using shunting yard, but with an itch to perhaps make a compiler of some kind, I looked at pratt parsing.

you won’t find much information about pratt parsing in a conventional learning path of computer science. the dragon book, for all it talks about parsing, doesn’t mention it. as far as I’m aware, most programming language courses will probably teach either LR or recursive descent depending on how academic it is (mine taught recursive descent). pratt parsing specifically appears to be relegated mostly to compiler development and PLT circles. the most mainstream mention I know of it is in crafting interpreters; wikipedia also links a few other posts.

I used the crafting interpreters text to get my bearings, but I also wanted to practice reading formal papers more, so I tried using Pratt’s original paper text as much as possible. it did take a bit more time than I’d have liked to wrap my mind around how the recursion fits into the operator precedence, but it did just click all at once, namely once I related it back to the shunting yard algorithm. so now I’m writing the observations down so I don’t forget. maybe it’s useful to you.

shunting yard

the shunting yard algorithm is, comparatively, much simpler than pratt. you have an output stream and an operator stack. operators are defined with their binding powers, where higher binding power means it binds tighter to its arguments (e.g. * has higher binding power in arithmetic than +). in the simplest description of shunting yard, for every token in the input stream:

if it’s a “standalone” token (i.e. identifier/number), just push it to the output

if it’s of higher binding power than the top of the operator stack, push it to the stack

popping all “higher or equal binding power” operators assumes that all operators are left-associative

at the end of the token stream, pop all remaining operators to the output.

a very primitive shunting yard in C, where expressions are converted to postfix notation, could be:

#include <stdio.h> #include <ctype.h> typedef struct { char ops[32]; // limit of 32 operators int idx; // index of last operator } Stk; void push(Stk *s, char op) { s->idx += 1; s->ops[s->idx] = op; } void pop(Stk *s) { s->idx -= 1; } char peek(Stk *s) { return s->ops[s->idx]; } char empty(Stk *s) { return s->idx == -1; } int get_bindpower(char op) { static const int bps[] = { ['+'] = 1, ['-'] = 1, ['*'] = 2, ['/'] = 2 }; return bps[op]; } int main(int argc, char **argv) { char *str = argv[1]; Stk _opstk = (Stk){ .idx = -1 }; Stk *opstk = &_opstk; while (*str != '\0') { if (isdigit(*str)) { // number printf("EMIT '%c'\n", *str); } else { // operator (assumed valid) // get_bindpower(peek(opstk)) is the left binding power // get_bindpower(*str) is the right binding power while (!empty(opstk) && get_bindpower(peek(opstk)) >= get_bindpower(*str)) { printf("EMIT '%c'\n", peek(opstk)); pop(opstk); } push(opstk, *str); } str += 1; } while (!empty(opstk)) { printf("EMIT '%c'\n", peek(opstk)); pop(opstk); } return 0; }

$ ./a.out '1+5*2+6' EMIT '1' EMIT '5' EMIT '2' EMIT '*' EMIT '+' EMIT '6' EMIT '+'

the problem with shunting yard is that it’s not very extensible to new behavior. for example, the algorithm has no concept of “subexpressions” in the recursive sense; adding parentheses means attaching special functionality to the ) token for popping off the stack until reaching the matching (. that’s not hard in its own right, but more complex behavior like unary operators, right-associative expressions, functions with variable arguments, etc. will get messy. this is where the transformation to pratt parsing comes in.

from shunting to pratt

the main transformation that turns the shunting yard algorithm into a pratt parser is: instead of using an explicit stack for operators, using the call stack to enter subexpressions as operators need them. with this, you thread the “left binding power” of infix/postfix operators to those recursive calls, returning when you encounter a binding power of an infix/postfix operator less than the left binding power. unwinding the stack when encountering a lower binding power in shunting yard is the same as returning from the recursive call in a pratt parser.

a cool thing with this is that, unlike recursive descent, you don’t nest calls through every level of precedence for every expression, but rather only nest when entering subexpressions. simultaneously slightly more efficient and cleaner!

unfortunately, there’s no clean “intermediate” step for this. you kind of just have to thrust it all through the wringer and look at the output. here’s the simplest pratt-style parser I can show in c, equivalent to the shunting yard parser before:

#include <stdio.h> #include <ctype.h> #include <stdlib.h> int get_bindpower(char op) { // the end token ('\0') is now defined in the bind power array static const int bps[] = { ['\0'] = 0, ['+'] = 1, ['-'] = 1, ['*'] = 2, ['/'] = 2 }; return bps[op]; } typedef struct { char *str; } Parser; char cur(Parser *p) { return *p->str; } void bump(Parser *p) { p->str += 1; } void expr(Parser *parser, int left_bindpower) { // all expressions *must* start with a "prefix" token // this is what pratt calls "nuds" in his original paper. // in this case, our only prefix tokens are digits if (isdigit(cur(parser))) { printf("EMIT '%c'\n", cur(parser)); } else { fprintf(stderr, "expression doesn't start with a number\n"); abort(); } bump(parser); // at this point, we're at an infix/postfix operator. // this is what pratt calls "leds" in his original paper. // when this check is false, it's kind of like "unwinding" the stack in shunting yard. while (left_bindpower < get_bindpower(cur(parser))) { // assumed to be valid operator (like the shunting yard code) // there's an expression on the right side of binary operators. parse it first. // this is kind of like pushing to the operator stack in shunting yard. int new_left_bindpower = get_bindpower(cur(parser)); char op = cur(parser); bump(parser); expr(parser, new_left_bindpower); printf("EMIT '%c'\n", op); } } int main(int argc, char **argv) { Parser parser = (Parser){ .str = argv[1] }; expr(&parser, 0); return 0; }

$ ./a.out '1+5*2+6' EMIT '1' EMIT '5' EMIT '2' EMIT '*' EMIT '+' EMIT '6' EMIT '+'

on its own, this doesn’t look too exciting. I made the stack implicit. big whuff. but! there are two neat things here:

these are called “nuds” in pratt’s original paper. the other half of expr() is for handling what I call “infix/postfix” operators, or “leds” in pratt’s paper.

we have the power to assign arbitrary code to any individual operator as soon as the operator is seen, just like recursive descent.

these combined allow for simple & straightforward extensibility. here’s all it takes to add parentheses into the mix:

int get_bindpower(char op) { // add '(' to bps static const int bps[] = { ['\0'] = 0, ['('] = 0, ['+'] = 1, ['-'] = 1, ['*'] = 2, ['/'] = 2 }; return bps[op]; } // ... // forward-declare expr() and add paren() subroutine. // this doesn't even need to be a subroutine; you can inline this in the expr() func. // but this is cleaner :) void expr(Parser *parser, int left_bindpower); void paren(Parser *parser) { expr(parser, get_bindpower('(')); if (cur(parser) != ')') { puts("no matching ')'"); abort(); } } // ... // add the if '(' conditional here; rest is same void expr(Parser *parser, int left_bindpower) { if (cur(parser) == '(') { bump(parser); paren(parser); } else if (isdigit(cur(parser))) { printf("EMIT '%c'\n", cur(parser)); } else { puts("expression doesn't start with a valid prefix token"); abort(); } // ... }

$ ./a.out '1+5*(2+6)' EMIT '1' EMIT '5' EMIT '2' EMIT '6' EMIT '+' EMIT '*' EMIT '+'

want to add even more operators? coalesce the get_bindpower() function with choosing the subroutine to run, storing “rules” as a struct of the binding power, prefix subroutine, and postfix subroutine. the core stays simple while all the operators run in their own little functions! I’m not going to paste another blob of code here, but that setup is described in the crafting interpreters chapter. it’s neat!

update: I also made my own structured example in my snippets repo. it’s for mostly the same expression grammar with a few extensions (unary +, -, and a postfix !), showing how it can get extended to a more realistic and maintable form for a codebase.

hope this helps anyone else. happy parsing :)

Agent workload doubles contributor output to full-stack levels
Why: You face context-switching overhead and work inflation from AI assistance
Hacker News ↗HN 649↑extracted 06:50 · 1,597 words

The pitch was that AI would give us our time back. Write less boilerplate, delegate the boring parts, go home earlier.

I am not complaining about the tools. I use agents for all of my engineering work now, and I wrote about how that setup evolved and why I still read the code. I can investigate and ship things that would not have been worth starting two years ago.

But somewhere this year I noticed that I am working more than I ever have, and that it feels heavier than it used to. Not because any single task is harder. Because there are so many more of them, all at once.

This post is me trying to understand that, with some data from my own GitHub history.

The old load and the new load

As a full-stack engineer, a normal week for me meant two or three serious tasks. A feature, a tricky bug, maybe a migration. Each one needed real focus, but I could hold one of them in my head at a time, finish it, and move to the next.

Today my week looks completely different. I have several agents running in parallel: one implementing a change, one investigating a production question, one working through a review, another doing research I will need later. None of them needs me all the time. Every one of them needs me sometimes.

That “sometimes” is the problem.

Each agent eventually comes back with a question, a plan to approve, a diff to read or a result to make sense of. Responding means switching contexts: reloading a different problem, a different repository and a different set of decisions.

Two or three serious tasks a week was tiring in a familiar way. Working with many agents is tiring in a new way. My attention is spread across more problems, and I find moving between them much more mentally draining.

What my GitHub history shows

I pulled my GitHub contribution calendar for every month from January 2019 to today and marked my roles at Bold.org, which I joined in January 2020: Full Stack Engineer until August 2022, Principal Engineer until August 2025, and Vice President of Technology since then. These are GitHub’s contribution counts, including private work, so they mix commits, pull requests, reviews and issues. It is a rough signal of activity, not a measure of value. 2019 is an incomplete baseline: my job before Bold.org used a self-hosted GitLab, so GitHub only shows what I did outside it. But the shape is hard to ignore.

The first part of the curve is what I expected:

My busiest coding years at Bold.org were the early ones. 2020 was my highest year, with 2,711 contributions. 2021 dropped to 1,839 and 2022 to 1,498.

The Principal Engineer years were flat and lower. From August 2022 to July 2025 I averaged 114 contributions a month, against 173 as a Full Stack Engineer. My lowest year was 2023, with 1,189.

The first months as VP of Technology looked the same. From August to December 2025 I averaged 111 a month, and December 2025 was my quietest month since joining Bold.org, with 50.

As a Principal Engineer, more of my work went into architecture, deep dives into concepts, setting up projects and planning, rather than fixing bugs or iterating on features. Becoming VP of Technology continued that shift, with even less feature work expected. At least until AI came along.

Then 2026 happened:

I am back at my Full Stack Engineer pace. By October 27 I am at 2,102 contributions, already more than any full year from 2021 to 2025. Over the same period in 2020 I had 2,371, so 2026 is close to my busiest year but not above it.

August 2026 is the busiest month in the whole timeline. 427 contributions, against a previous high of 319 in April 2020.

My normal day moved back up. The median weekday was 3 or 4 contributions every year from 2022 to 2025. In 2026 it is 7, close to the 8 I had in 2020.

Fewer weekdays without GitHub activity. Through October 27, I had 18 weekdays with zero contributions, compared with 40–46 across each full year from 2022 to 2025. These are days without recorded GitHub activity, not days off work.

Heavy days became routine again. From 2022 to 2025 I had exactly one day per year with 24 or more contributions. In 2026 I already have 16, almost the 17 I had in all of 2020.

Almost all of this is private work. The dashed line in the chart is public contributions only: it was near zero from 2021 to 2025 and came back this year, with 191 so far, but that is still less than a tenth of 2026.

The twist is the role. In 2020 I was a full-stack engineer doing feature work full time, and this is roughly what that looked like on GitHub. Today I lead engineering at Bold.org and do far less feature work than I used to. On paper my graph should look like my Principal Engineer years, or lower. Instead it is back where it was when writing code was my whole job.

What changed is how much work feels worth taking on. When coding is cheap, I can fix bugs I would previously have left alone, improve patterns across the codebase, take on new projects and turn more ideas into something real. Much of this is work I would never have considered before. Agents did not just make my existing workload faster to finish. They made it easy to give myself a much bigger one.

Bursts, then burnout

Looking at the monthly totals, 2026 is not one steady climb. It is a series of bursts.

January and February were the first clear burst, with 277 and 233 contributions. This is when I started working seriously with agents, and when the observability experiment that became our internal company agent took off. Every week revealed something new I could suddenly delegate, and I wanted to try all of it.

March to June generally winds down, from 203 contributions in March to 77 in June. But April was a smaller burst along the way, another month when I had ideas and acted on them. I think improvements in the models were part of that renewed energy. Its monthly total of 187 is below January to March, but it includes a 29-contribution day and 68 public contributions, my highest public total of the year.

August is the biggest burst of all. Coming back from my summer break, the calendar shows a full week of zero contributions in early August. Then every weekday from August 10 to August 27 was between 14 and 43. August ended at 427 contributions, the busiest month in the whole timeline. September stayed high at 301.

Every cycle starts the same way. A new model or a new workflow makes something possible that was not possible last month, and my head fills with ideas. Because implementation is now cheap, there is no natural friction stopping me from pursuing all of them at once. So I do. I start more agents, open more threads and say yes to more experiments.

That works for a few weeks. Then the reviewing and context switching catch up with me, and I pull back to a more stable flow. I stay there until something exciting comes along, or until a holiday recharges me enough that I go straight back into another burst.

I count three of these cycles since January.

At the end of a burst, I feel tired of AI. I do not want to start another agent or think of more ideas. I do my normal work, but I stop chasing the extra projects and possibilities because I do not have the mental bandwidth for them.

The low season is not low anymore

The part that surprised me most is not the peaks. It is how much I am doing between them.

March did not feel like the bursts in January, February or August, but I was still doing substantial work. It had 203 contributions, against my Principal Engineer average of 114 a month. I was still acting on ideas, but that level of activity no longer felt like an exceptional push. Even when I think I am taking it easy, there are still agent results to work through, reviews to finish and small things that are now “just a quick prompt”.

June was genuinely low, with 77 contributions, comparable to my quieter Principal Engineer months. Maybe that was when all the tiredness caught up with me. Not every quiet month is higher than before. But outside the bursts, I still find myself taking on more work than I used to. That is what I mean by the low season not feeling low anymore.

Where that leaves me

AI made room for work I would never have considered before. I like being able to fix more things and turn more ideas into something real. But the capacity to implement those ideas has grown much faster than my capacity to think about them. Eventually I stop wanting to start another agent and go back to just doing my normal work. I have not figured out a balance. For now, I am noticing that being able to do more keeps turning into doing more. And I am tired.

A note on how this article was written: AI helped me pull and analyze my GitHub contribution data, build the chart and draft this article. The experience, decisions and opinions are mine.
