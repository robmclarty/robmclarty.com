Energy-based models claim 385x to 1000x inference speedup on reasoning tasks
Why: You can reduce latency and cost in agentic loops without adding external verification services.

1Duke University2UIUC3Harvard University

TL;DR – We introduce Energy-Based Reasoners (EBRs), reasoning models that learn an energy function over latent reasoning states and formulate looped updates as gradient descent on this energy. Its value scores candidate predictions without requiring an external verifier, while its gradient guides refinement and provides a natural signal for stopping. With only 1.8M parameters, EBRs achieve 99.9% accuracy on Sudoku-Extreme while using 345× less inference compute than the next-best model. On N-Queens and Graph Coloring, EBRs achieve state-of-the-art coverage while maintaining near-100% single-sample accuracy.

Abstract

Looping is a powerful inductive bias for reasoning. By repeatedly iterating a recurrent block, looped models can scale their effective depth and enable multi-step reasoning in latent space. However, existing looped models are not inherently guided by an explicit measure of progress, making it difficult to determine when to stop looping and how to improve upon the current state. To enable this, we introduce Energy-Based Reasoners (EBRs), which learn an energy function over latent reasoning states and formulate looped updates as gradient descent on this energy. The energy scores latent states, while its gradient guides refinement and provides a signal for stopping. To train EBRs effectively, we further introduce exploration-based training to discover additional modes and to better shape the energy landscape over a broader set of states. Across four algorithmic reasoning benchmarks, EBRs match or outperform prior recurrent reasoning models, achieving 99.9% accuracy on Sudoku-Extreme while using 345× less inference compute than the next-best model and maintaining comparable training compute. On all multi-solution benchmarks, EBRs achieve state-of-the-art coverage of distinct valid solutions while maintaining near-100% single-sample accuracy. These results suggest that energy-based models are a promising approach for strong reasoning performance.

Why Energy?

Existing looped reasoners update their latent state without any explicit assessment of it. An EBR learns an energy function that guides refinement, stopping and selection.

Score. The energy rates the compatibility between the reasoning state and the initial board . Lower means better compatibility.

Refine. The gradient turns the score into an improvement direction.

Stop. The state becomes stationary once the gradient is small, so the gradient acts as a stopping criterion.

Select. Among multiple candidate solutions, we pick the one with the lowest energy.

Correct solutions settle at low-energy stationary states. Energy, gradient norm and step size during descent on Sudoku-Extreme. Correct trajectories settle at low energy with small gradients, while wrong ones keep moving.

Test-Time Scaling

Running more descent steps refines each answer further, and running more trajectories gives the energy more candidates to choose the lowest-energy one from.

EBRs reach EqR's best accuracy with 7× fewer iterations (left) and 43× fewer samples (right).

GRAM and TRM from GRAM's paper; EqR from its released checkpoint. Mean ± SD over three seeds.

Adaptive Stopping

Each trajectory stops once its energy gradient falls below a threshold, so easy puzzles finish in a few steps and hard ones keep going.

EBRs give the best single-sample accuracy among reasoning models. Within only 5 passes, they reach 99.9% and 88.3%, ahead of methods that draw up to 128. Stopping each trajectory once its energy gradient falls below a threshold keeps 99.9% accuracy with 10.9× fewer FLOPs than a fixed-depth EBR, allowing us to surpass EqR with 345× fewer FLOPs.

Multiple Solutions

EBR acts like stochastic gradient descent and can capture many different solutions. Coverage captures the share of each input's distinct valid solutions that 20 samples find.

Top-Nsigma sampling prevents accuracy collapse in high-temperature agentic pipelines
Why: It gives you a concrete engineering fix for reliable long-horizon tasks in your high-temperature pipelines.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 285 words

Evaluating 29 open-weight models up to 122B parameters reveals that variable-pool samplers drop from 100% to 26% of their temperature-1 accuracy when context grows. Top-Nsigma maintains stable candidate pools, enabling coding agents to resolve up to 62 issues at T=2 compared to zero for top-p.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Open-weight language models are widely observed to get worse the longer they generate. Often, attempts to fix this involve more training. At high temperature, The sampler, i.e. the rule that picks the next token out of the model's probabilities, decides whether long and agentic generation survives and/or thrives. We test 29 open-weight models from 1B to 122B parameters on ten established benchmarks plus needle retrieval out to one million tokens, covering SWE-bench Verified coding agents, long chain-of-thought reasoning, long-form writing, long structured output and long-context reading. Samplers that keep their pool of candidate tokens small and steady as temperature rises don't lose accuracy. Samplers that let in low-probability tokens fall apart. At temperature 2, coding agents running top-p, top-k or Top-H resolve 0 of 100 GitHub issues, while the same agents running top-nsigma resolve 46 to 62 and win every issue the two samplers disagree on, on three Qwen3.5 models. Over all benchmarks top-nsigma keeps a median 100% of its temperature-1 score where top-p keeps 26%. Counting how many tokens each sampler lets through explains why. In one Llama-3.1-8B story, going from temperature 1 to 2 grows top-p's pool from 4 tokens to 51,845 while top-nsigma stays at 3. Across 133 combinations of sampler setting and temperature, the typical pool size predicts judged quality (Spearman correlation of -0.77), and when we pick the pool at temperature 1 and only apply temperature afterwards, Top-H, min-p and p-less stop collapsing. At the default temperature of 1 most samplers perform about the same, We release all code and data to the public.

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Animated 3D blockouts support previsualization and provide structural conditions for video generation, but manually reconstructing them from reference videos is costly. This limits both their use by creators and the production of paired training data. We introduce MAVEBench, a benchmark of 50 film shots and 416 keyframes for evaluating camera motion, spatial layout, and character blocking. We propose MAVE, a video-to-blockout framework combining Metric observation, Agentic modeling, and evidence-based VErification. It first recovers cameras, human motion, and geometric evidence in a shared metric coordinate system. An agent then constructs an editable environment under constraints from these observations, while programmatic checks report inconsistencies to guide refinement. The outputs are editable Blender scenes and frame-aligned blockout videos. On MAVEBench, MAVE improves overall scores by 27.5-41.1 points across three agents over their corresponding vanilla baselines. With DeepSeek-1017, it achieves a score of 73.2 at an average API cost of only USD 0.054 per shot, outperforming the strongest vanilla baseline while reducing total reconstruction time by 53%. We also construct MAVEData-3K, a dataset of 3,000 blockout-video pairs for model training, and will release it publicly. Creators can change viewpoints and character paths, then render the edited scenes to condition video generation. Code is available at this https URL.

CompoundingKernel Generates GPU Kernels on B200 with 4.4x Speedup
Why: You get a concrete agentic architecture for low-level tool use with real performance deltas.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 244 words

The system achieves rank one on SOL-ExecBench using a lineage-based search process. It operates from zero by relying on PyTorch definitions and external verification rather than reference kernels.

Read the extracted text ↓

Authors:Qian Qu, Hongyang Wang, Wenbo Chen, Yichuan Li, Jingguo Liu, Xian Li, Xuan Wang, Ruochen Wen, Leo Fan, Yiming Yan, Zheshen Wang, Benoit Dumoulin

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:We present CompoundingKernel, a self-improving agent system that writes GPU kernels by search. As of 2026-11-11, its generated kernels rank #1 on the public L1, L2 and Quantization boards of SOL-ExecBench (NVIDIA B200). They have held #1 on L1 since 2026-10-27, albeit a short loss on 2026-11-07. All 235 problems have a site-verified correct kernel; on the 209 public-board problems they are 4.40x faster than the benchmark's reference baseline. The search starts from zero: agents get each problem's PyTorch definition but no reference kernels or official scores. A per-problem archive outside the model holds every correct kernel and its lineage. An LLM controller reads it, picks a parent or a new design, and gives each parallel child agent one direction. Checks outside the model decide correctness. At equal wall time, it beat a coded UCB style tree on 35 of 38 problems by making 2.4x as many correct attempts. Over a thousand agents ran at once, and one warm restart fix raised their evaluation rate 3.6x. We find that the rate of reliable measurements, not the number of agents, limits how far such a search scales. Our code and kernels are available on URL.

Power side-channels on NVIDIA H200 identify AI workloads with 97 percent accuracy
Why: You can detect training versus inference via external power measurements, challenging your infrastructure security model.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 322 words

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:AI compute verification is one of the first tangible and tractable points for international policy aimed at AI governance. Determining whether frontier labs, or any operator, comply with established agreements requires the regulating authority, at the very least, to discern how their compute is used. The elementary building block of AI compute is the GPU, and any activity it executes leaves a physical trace. We show that an external observer can identify the class of the workload running on a single NVIDIA H200 from its power draw. Unlike on-chip NVML telemetry, which can be spoofed or replayed, this physical channel can be observed without the operator's cooperation. We recorded 930 five-second traces at 10 MHz with a Rogowski probe, covering seventeen open-weight LLMs and twenty-five non-AI workloads. We separate training from inference and from non-AI computation with an accuracy of 97% and a macro-averaged F1 score of 0.955, evaluated on models unseen during training. We find that AI workload spectral content predominantly lies below $\sim 20$ kHz and training appears to be particularly recognizable through the optimizer update. The GPU operator is then treated as adversarial and able to reshape the training program. Four evasion strategies are tested to disguise training as inference, producing 680 additional adversarial traces. A detector hardened against evasion strategies, with the tested strategy held out, catches training $\geq 99$% of the time for three of the four strategies. The fourth, diluted low-rank adaptation (LoRA), is detected $48-88$% of the time with a hardened classifier, rising to $\geq 98$% with an additional rescue rule. While these attacks are not intended to provide a comprehensive evaluation against adversarial behaviour, they offer initial insights beyond genuine activities and a dataset for developing and testing stronger evasion mechanisms.

Quad9 Exits France Over Dynamic DNS Blocking Mandates
Why: DNS filtering creates infrastructure gaps for your critical systems.
Hacker News ↗HN 94↑extracted 05:50 · 2,916 words

On Tuesday, November 24, 2026, Quad9 will stop answering DNS queries from France. Quad9 is the Swiss non-profit behind the free 9.9.9.9 resolver, which blocks malware and phishing domains. It is not leaving because of costs or lack of users. It is leaving because a Paris court fined it for refusing to block a list of pirate sports-streaming domains on behalf of beIN Sports. With around 20 similar cases still pending in France, leaving the country is the only option Quad9 can afford.

Cloudflare, which runs the competing 1.1.1.1 resolver, called the departure "a canary in the coal mine", and the post reached the front page of Hacker News. In that thread, Quad9's CTO John Todd asked readers to write "a long-form, well-reasoned statement of why France is taking the wrong path", with plenty of sources, and to send it to policymakers. This is our attempt. Every claim below links to a primary document, a court ruling, or contemporaneous reporting.

What Quad9 is, and why it matters​

A DNS resolver turns a name like example.com into an IP address your computer can connect to. Most people use their ISP's resolver without knowing it. A public resolver is one anyone can point their device or network at instead, such as Google's 8.8.8.8, Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1, Cisco's OpenDNS, or Quad9's 9.9.9.9.

Quad9 has a few properties that make it unusual:

It is a non-profit. It was founded in 2017 by the Global Cyber Alliance, IBM, and Packet Clearing House, and moved its legal seat to Zürich in 2021, hosted by SWITCH, specifically so that its privacy promises would be backed by Swiss law. As John Todd put it at the time: "Privacy policies which are not backed by law are empty promises."

Security filtering is its main purpose. Quad9 refuses to resolve domains that threat-intelligence feeds flag as malware, phishing, or botnet infrastructure. Its about page claims 670 million+ blocks a day across 230+ resolver clusters in over 110 countries.

It keeps no user records. Quad9 does not know who its users are, and that is the point.

Organisations have come to depend on it. Mullvad shut down its own public encrypted DNS service on November 2, 2026 and moved Mullvad Browser users with default settings to Quad9, funding Quad9 instead. (Mullvad Browser users in France will now be migrated to a resolver that disappears three weeks later.) Quad9's announcement lists dozens of French networks it sees traffic from, including the interministerial digital directorate DINUM, France Travail, CEA, Institut Pasteur, RENATER, EDF, SNCF, AP-HM hospitals in Marseille, France Télévisions, Thales, and Airbus's Stormshield. Quad9 can't tell any of them it is leaving. As Cloudflare points out, when a resolver disappears, users don't see an error message. "Their Internet will simply stop working."

How France got here​

The law: Article L.333-10 of the Code du sport​

The legal basis for all of this is Article L.333-10 of the French sports code. It was created by Law 2021-1382 of October 25, 2021, the same law that merged HADOPI and the CSA into the new regulator Arcom. L.333-10 lets a sports rightsholder go to the president of the Paris judicial court and obtain "dynamic" blocking orders, meaning orders that cover sites already identified plus new mirror domains notified later through Arcom, against "toute personne susceptible de contribuer à y remédier" (any person able to help stop the infringement).

That last phrase is what puts DNS resolvers in scope. In July 2026, Parliament passed Law 2026-725 on professional sport, which amended L.333-10 and added an automated real-time blocking system run by Arcom. TorrentFreak reported that under the new system "ARCOM's agents no longer approve each block in advance." Quad9 called it "recklessly dangerous."

The cases: from ISPs to everyone​

The timeline shows how the circle of targets has widened:

The ruling itself​

The 21 October judgment is short and worth reading. A few details matter:

The €580,000 figure was beIN's request, not the penalty. beIN asked for €10,000 per day per domain still reachable; across 58 domains that is €580,000 a day. The judge set €1,000 a day in total, running until the end of the WTA season on November 14.

The judge used Quad9's malware filtering against it. Quad9 argued it could not implement geolocated blocking. The court (para. 16) replied that Quad9 "appears to contradict itself" because it already "applies automated filtering strictly limited to cybersecurity to neutralise malicious domains on a global scale." The fact that a resolver can drop a name for everyone was treated as proof it can drop a name for France.

Refusing to try was enough. Paragraph 18: "Bound by an obligation of means, the Quad9 foundation states that it implements none in order to execute the decision. This circumstance alone justifies the imposition of a penalty."

€1,000 a day for three weeks is survivable, about €24,000. Twenty more cases like it, each running for a full football season with ever-growing domain lists and Arcom's new automated system feeding them, is not. Quad9 says: "Quad9 cannot survive the accumulation of penalties yet to be assessed in 20 other pending cases."

Why Quad9 can't just comply​

Quad9 is often asked why it doesn't just block the domains. It has three options, and none of them work.

1. Block globally. This is what Quad9 did for Canal+ in December 2024 and what DNS4EU does today. It means a French court decides what a user in Brazil, Kenya or Germany can resolve, for the commercial benefit of one company. Quad9's announcement asks how France would react if other nations imposed their laws on French users through DNS. The answer is obvious, and the reverse should be too. Nothing limits this to sports either: once global blocking is accepted, every court in every country can add names to everyone's resolver.

2. Geo-block France. Quad9 has already tried this, in Germany. In the Sony Music v. Quad9 case, Quad9 used geoIP blocking for German users while the case continued. Sony's lawyers used a VPN to reach the content "from Germany" anyway, the court agreed this was non-compliance, and Quad9 was fined for contempt. GeoIP is a probabilistic guess about where an IP address is, not a legal fact about where a person is. A resolver that keeps no user records has no reliable way to define a "French user." As Quad9 told TorrentFreak: "There is no legally useful way for us to define a French user." Even the French order is geographically complicated: heise notes it covers the overseas departments and Saint-Pierre-et-Miquelon but excludes New Caledonia, Wallis and Futuna, and French Polynesia.

3. Pay and fight. Quad9 won in Germany. The Dresden Higher Regional Court held in December 2023 that DNS resolvers get the same liability privilege as access providers, and the German civil rights group GFF said "the judgement puts the onus on rights holders." But winning took "several years, six figures of cost to Quad9, and massive wasted time," and the opposition, in Quad9's words, "has more lawyers than we have money and time." As Quad9 wrote in 2025, compliance costs that are routine overhead for Google are, "for small, mission-driven nonprofits like Quad9, … an existential threat."

That leaves leaving.

Why DNS blocking doesn't work​

The argument against resolver blocking isn't only about Quad9's survival. Even on its own terms, it does not do what the rightsholders want.

It doesn't remove anything. The DNS only holds pointers. Quad9's Dresden write-up puts it this way: "The DNS does not contain content - it is a system designed for delivery only of pointers." The pirate server stays online and keeps serving everyone who reaches it some other way. The Internet Society's 2025 guide for legal professionals says the same thing.

It takes seconds to bypass. Quad9 notes there are thousands of other open resolvers. Users can also run their own, or use encrypted DNS over HTTPS (RFC 8484), which looks like ordinary web traffic. Arcom's own December 2025 study for the National Assembly says 20% of French internet users already use an alternative DNS resolver and 29% use a VPN. Quad9 compares DNS blocking to "holding a piece of newspaper in front of a stadium full of people to block the view of a concert."

It is blunt. DNS works at the level of whole domains. A resolver can't block one infringing stream on a shared host without blocking everything else on that name. The IAB's RFC 7754, the IETF's standard reference on blocking and filtering, says: "From a technical perspective, there are no perfect or even good solutions -- there is only least bad." ICANN's security committee revisited the question in SAC127, "DNS Blocking Revisited" (2025), warning implementers to minimise overblocking and avoid affecting users outside their jurisdiction. France's own registry operator, AFNIC, made the core point years ago in its scientific council's report on the consequences of DNS filtering: "Le DNS n'a pas été conçu pour filtrer les contenus." (The DNS was not designed to filter content.)

It is opaque. When a resolver is forced to lie, the user sees a site that doesn't load. They have no way to tell whether the site is down, their network is broken, or a court ordered the block. There is a standard for being honest about this: RFC 8914 Extended DNS Errors defines code 16, "Censored", for answers withheld "due to an external requirement imposed by an entity other than the operator." But nothing in the French orders requires resolvers to use it.

Blocking at scale causes real collateral damage. France is not the only test case. Italy's Piracy Shield system blocked Google Drive for much of the country in October 2024. A RIPE Labs study of Piracy Shield found more than 500 unrelated sites blocked and concluded that "IP-level blocking is an indiscriminate tool with consequences that outweigh its benefits." In Spain, LaLiga's IP blocking of shared Cloudflare addresses knocked innocent sites offline every match day, leading Cloudflare's CEO Matthew Prince to call the approach "bonkers". The same RIPE study found most European piracy is hosted in Europe, which is where enforcement belongs: at the hosting provider.

Measured by its own goals, the French system has a mixed record. Arcom reports 12,606 sports domains blocked since 2022, 6,496 of them in 2025 alone (up 71%), while piracy fell 4% in 2025 and 34% since 2021. The number of blocks is growing much faster than piracy is shrinking, and Arcom's figures don't separate the effect of resolver blocking from ISP blocking. Every one of those domains has to be found, blocked, and then found again when it reappears under a new name. OONI's 2025 measurement of French ISP blocking found that of 542 domains tested, 291 were blocked by at least one ISP but only 132 by all four. The European Commission's own 2025 assessment of its live-events piracy recommendation concluded the effects "have been limited and have not allowed to significantly curb the volume of online piracy of live events."

What France loses​

Driving Quad9 out of France doesn't hurt pirates at all. The people who lose are the ones who chose a resolver for security.

French users lose a malware filter. Quad9 exists to stop people resolving phishing and malware domains. Users who move to an unfiltered resolver, or to their ISP's resolver, lose that protection. Users who move to an unknown free resolver may be trading it for one that sells their browsing data. As Cloudflare puts it, "Penalizing transparent services only pushes users toward less accountable corners of the web."

Critical infrastructure may break silently. Hospitals, utilities and local authorities on Quad9's list will lose DNS if their IT teams miss the announcement. Embedded devices with 9.9.9.9 hard-coded may be hard to fix.

The choice of resolvers shrinks. OpenDNS left France in 2024, and Quad9 leaves now. An Internet Society report cited by TorrentFreak warned that small and non-profit resolvers are the most likely to be pushed out, leaving the field to the largest companies, which can afford to fight or to build per-country infrastructure. Cloudflare can satisfy the court by blocking at its CDN; Quad9 has only a resolver.

France's own digital sovereignty goals suffer. France promotes privacy-respecting European infrastructure, yet its courts are driving out a Swiss privacy-focused non-profit while US giants remain.

It's not just France​

Quad9 is the clearest casualty so far, but the same process is underway across Europe.

Belgium: DAZN obtained an order against Cloudflare, Google and Cisco in March 2025, and OpenDNS left Belgium in April 2025 before returning when the order against Cisco was suspended. In August 2026, the Brussels business court upheld the core of the order, with fines of €100,000 per match day capped at €20 million per company. Cisco says its public DNS "does not allow for the implementation of selective, geolocated and dynamic blocking as required by the order." Cloudflare, Google and Cisco have appealed ahead of the January 2027 deadline.

Italy: AGCOM fined Cloudflare €14.2 million in January 2026, 1% of its global revenue, for not blocking via 1.1.1.1. Prince threatened to pull servers and free services from Italy: "Play stupid games, win stupid prizes."

Spain: A Barcelona court upheld LaLiga's right to block Cloudflare IP addresses.

Germany is the counter-example. Courts there rejected resolver blocking for both Quad9 and Cloudflare.

The EU level: A study commissioned by the European Parliament's legal affairs committee proposes a binding regulation that would require blocking within 30 minutes across DNS resolvers, VPNs and CDNs. John Todd's warning on Hacker News is that "the EU and EC are looking at this issue right now and I expect will look to France as a case study of how to do things for the rest of the region."

That is why France matters beyond France. If its model becomes the EU template, every public resolver in Europe will face the choice Quad9 faced: lie to all users worldwide, build unreliable geolocation infrastructure, or leave.

The counter-arguments​

The Hacker News discussion wasn't one-sided, and the counter-arguments deserve answers.

"Hosting providers are unreachable, so courts move up the chain." One commenter argued that pirate hosts are bulletproof and ignore courts, so going after the intermediaries is the only lever left. Another asked the Quad9 CTO directly where the problem should be attacked. This is the strongest point. But the RIPE data shows most of the hosting is in Europe and within reach of European courts, and the commenter's logic leads to blocking at the last link in the chain that cannot fight back. That is not the same as the link that can actually stop the infringement.

"Friction works. Most people won't bother to switch." Some argued that DNS blocking is good because it nudges the majority while staying easy to bypass for those who know how. The Paris Court of Appeal used essentially this reasoning. But Arcom's numbers say a fifth of French users already use alternative DNS, the friction is gone the moment one person changes a router setting for a household, and the cost is paid by resolvers that never had anything to do with piracy.

"Cloudflare is a hypocrite." Several commenters pointed out that Cloudflare's CDN fronts many pirate sites. That may be true, and it is an argument about Cloudflare's CDN, which French courts can already reach and have. It says nothing about whether a resolver should lie.

"The yellow pages would be held responsible." One commenter asked whether we'd accept a phone directory that kept listing a drug dealer's new address. The analogy fails because a resolver is not a curated directory. It's closer to the operator who connects you to whatever number you dial. Quad9 has "no association with these domain names, nor with the operators of the websites." Making the operator liable for every number dialled is how you end up with no operator.

What you can do​

If you use Quad9 in France, check whether you do at on.quad9.net, and switch resolvers before November 24. Check routers, VPN configs, DHCP settings, Pi-hole upstreams, and any devices with 9.9.9.9 or 149.112.112.112 hard-coded. Whatever you switch to, read its privacy policy first.

If you're in France or the EU, do what John Todd asked: write something long-form, ideally in French, make it indexable, and send it to your representatives. Quad9 specifically suggests telling Arcom that its mandate is to balance rightsholders' interests against citizens' digital rights, and that "balance cannot be claimed when the privacy and security of digital users are the collateral damage."

If you work on infrastructure, push for transparency wherever blocking does happen. RFC 8914's "Censored" error code exists so users can tell a court-ordered block from a broken network.

If you can afford it, Quad9 accepts donations for its legal defence. Its appeal against the May order is still pending at the Paris Court of Appeal.

The canary​

Quad9 put it this way: "Once the tools of censorship are built, they are rarely used sparingly or justly." The French system started with football. It now covers tennis, rugby, MotoGP and Formula 1, has expanded from ISPs to resolvers, VPNs, CDNs and search engines, and runs through an automated system in which no human approves each block. Each step was reasonable on its own. Together, they have pushed a non-profit security service out of the country while the piracy continues.

The sports-rights industry has a real problem. DNS resolvers are the wrong place to solve it, and France is about to find out what the internet looks like when the trustworthy resolvers leave first.

News Analysis – Artificial Intelligence Beat

Harvard researchers find AI coding agents shifted the bottleneck to code review

ComplexDiscovery OÜ Staff

Engineers at firms that adopted AI coding agents added about 30 percent more code, but finished work did not measurably rise and getting code through review took 49 percent longer, a Harvard working paper found.

In the study’s data, the extra coding produced no detectable rise in finished work, and code review became the bottleneck. Fiona Chen and James Stratton, both Ph.D. candidates at Harvard University, measured that pattern across 725,938 workers at 718 companies. Two legal AI announcements this month, Harvey’s motion-to-dismiss workflow and a court-filing redaction agent Cooley is developing with Google Cloud, place a lawyer at a comparable review step, and neither reports a measure of it.

What the Harvard data measured

How much weight that pattern can bear depends on where the numbers came from. Chen and Stratton built the study on data from Jellyfish, a company that sells an analytics platform tracking engineering activity, and joined it to LinkedIn employment records collected by Revelio Labs. The sample runs from January 2021 to March 2026 and covers Jellyfish client firms that consented to research use of their data. The paper’s tables list 68.3 million Jira issues, 200.9 million commits, 49.1 million pull requests and 21.3 million calendar events, though only 213 of the 718 firms supplied calendar data.

The design is a staggered difference-in-differences comparison, which tracks month by month how outcomes changed at adopting firms against firms that had not yet adopted. A comparison like that is only as good as the reasons adoption dates differed, and here the authors point to a process legal and security teams will recognize, because they sit inside it. The paper says timing was shaped mainly by when firms learned about the tools and by how long procurement took, meaning sourcing, legal and security approvals and pilot programs. One interviewee’s firm approved a tool in about a day; another interviewee put the average approval time at six to 12 months.

The authors call those procurement delays plausibly unrelated to how fast a firm was growing, which they argue supports comparing early and late adopters. They acknowledge one known imbalance, that larger firms adopted earlier, and they control for firm size. If some hidden difference remains, the authors say, it would more likely make AI look more productive than it is, not less. A bias in that direction would make the flat output result, if anything, generous to AI.

The current version of the paper is dated Aug. 4, 2026, and the first was dated Jan. 7. Chen circulated it as her job-market paper, and nothing in the version reviewed for this article indicates journal acceptance or completed peer review. Her website lists coverage links to The Wall Street Journal and The Economist, and legal trade press picked it up this month, including an Oct. 12 eDiscovery Today post by Doug Austin that relayed an Ars Technica report.

Drafts went up and finished issues did not

With that provenance in view, the coding results are large. Lines of code added rose by 1,494 per worker-month against a baseline of 5,017, about 30 percent. Commits rose 20 percent, and pull requests, the bundles of code submitted for review, rose 23 percent. The authors note that these estimates capture the added effect of agents at firms that already had AI coding assistants, not the effect of AI from a standing start.

Output is where the gain stops. The paper counts finished work as Jira issues resolved, and an issue counts as complete when its code is deployed to production. Agents moved that measure by 0.12 issues per worker-month on a 3.67 baseline, with a standard error of 0.17. That result is statistically indistinguishable from zero, and the 95 percent confidence interval rules out an increase above about 12 percent of the baseline, well short of the 30 percent rise in code written.

The authors tested the obvious escape route. If engineers had shifted to larger tasks, a flat issue count would understate real output. So the researchers labeled a 0.5 percent sample of issues by size with GPT-4o and extended those labels to every issue with a text-embedding model. They found no shift in average issue size, and pull requests did not grow larger either, which the authors use to set aside the simplest explanation for what happened in review.

Where the shortfall shows up: the review queue

The paper locates the shortfall in code review. The time from pull request submission to merge rose 3.45 days on a 7.03-day baseline, a 49 percent increase. The share of pull requests receiving a formal change request increased by about 12 percentage points from a baseline of 13 percent, nearly doubling, and review comments per pull request rose 35 percent. The share of workers doing any code review rose 14 percent from a base of about 29 percent, while the share writing code showed no measurable change.

AI review tools did not remove the bottleneck. By March 2026, nearly 80 percent of the firms had used one, yet AI generated only 23.3 percent of review comments, and only 10.8 percent of pull requests received even one AI comment. The authors write that the bottleneck persists after firms adopt such tools. The paper’s interviews show what that looks like on a team: a junior software engineer described a project where only one senior engineer could approve changes and could review the interviewee’s code only once a week. “After some outages that were caused by AI-generated commits, there were also new rules requiring senior engineer approval for AI-generated code, which created even more review bottlenecks,” the engineer said, in a quote the authors say they lightly edited for clarity.

That account fits the model the authors use to explain their numbers, which has two channels. In the first, faster drafting sends more code to a fixed pool of reviewers, a problem that moving labor into review can largely relieve. In the second, the rise in change requests and comments, which faster drafting alone does not explain, reads through the model as a sign of lower-quality drafts. That problem, the authors say, would require better technology. Anyone buying an agent should care which channel dominates, because the two call for different spending.

Employment did not move either

Nor did the coding gains show up as job cuts. The estimates rule out a decline in overall employment larger than 2.9 percent. A noisier estimate rules out a decline in engineering employment larger than 13.8 percent. The authors caution that these are short-run effects measured in partial equilibrium, which could mask job losses at competing firms, and that the assistant estimate for engineering employment shows upward pre-trends that make it hard to read causally.

Legal AI tools are designed around a similar checkpoint

Those software results arrive as legal AI tools adopt a related design, and the Harvey and Cooley announcements both follow it: automate a first pass, then keep a lawyer at the checkpoint. Harvey’s workflow, co-developed with LexisNexis and announced Oct. 7, is the drafting case. Harvey says the multi-step process of reading the complaint, researching and drafting a motion to dismiss “commonly takes 40 to 50+ hours of work” and that its tool produces the research and a first draft “in a matter of hours.” The announcement describes “human checkpoints at the moments where a lawyer’s judgment matters most,” but it does not state how long lawyers spend at those checkpoints or how long it takes to reach a motion ready to file.

Cooley’s Oct. 8 announcement applies the same posture to a different task, since its agent recommends redactions rather than drafting arguments. Cooley says the agent is still being developed and that attorneys make all redaction determinations before a filing goes to the court, which puts the human checkpoint at the last step before submission. As ComplexDiscovery reported Oct. 10, the announcement carries no error rate.

For a motion filed in federal court, the checkpoint is a legal duty rather than a design choice. Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 11(b), an attorney presenting a motion certifies several things about it to the best of the attorney’s knowledge, information and belief, “formed after an inquiry reasonable under the circumstances.” Its legal contentions must be “warranted by existing law or by a nonfrivolous argument for extending, modifying, or reversing existing law or for establishing new law.” Its factual contentions must have evidentiary support or, if specifically so identified, be likely to have it after a reasonable opportunity for further investigation or discovery. A first draft can arrive in hours, but that inquiry stays with the attorney, and the software evidence raises the possibility that review could absorb some of the drafting savings.

The paper’s method carries a second lesson, this one for information governance teams. The researchers identified agent use partly from traces inside the firms’ own systems, including bot accounts tied to AI tools and signatures such as “Co-authored-by” lines in commit messages and pull request descriptions. AI authorship can leave its own metadata trail in collaboration systems. That trail is a record that a retention schedule and a legal hold may need to reach.

None of this means legal work will behave like software, and the limits of the comparison matter. Document review is not code review, a production is not a pull request, and Chen and Stratton studied no legal work. A separate National Bureau of Economic Research working paper, revised in September 2026, reports a similar falloff using data on over 500,000 GitHub developers. Its authors, Mert Demirer, Leon Musolff and Liyuan Yang, found a 240 percent cumulative rise in commits from autonomous agents shrinking to 30 percent for actual releases. That paper carries its own disclosure: Demirer and Musolff each previously held a postdoctoral research position at Microsoft, which owns GitHub, and each now works as a paid research consultant for the company. Two software studies point to a pattern in software, not a finding about law firms.

Document review offers a partial precedent

Discovery practitioners have argued a version of this question before, and Austin’s post draws that comparison. He wrote that discovery has seen a similar debate over generative AI review, with some practitioners saying manual validation offsets the gains and others, including him, saying statistical sampling of the kind used in predictive coding is enough. For coding agents, though, he wrote that the extra review time may be warranted, since code that is 90 percent correct can still fail to run.

His comparison suggests a useful split between two kinds of legal output. Population-level work such as first-pass relevance review can be validated by sampling. A single filing is different, because its citations, factual assertions and sensitive data generally need direct examination when one fabricated citation or one missed Social Security number is the failure. Buyers can turn that split into a test. Ask a vendor which kind of output its agent produces, then ask for the downstream measure rather than the drafting one: hours from upload to an approved work product, the share of drafts a reviewing attorney sent back and reviewer time per matter. Budget review capacity before scaling drafting volume, and log what reviewers change, because that log shows whether the bottleneck is volume or quality.

Read the paper’s limits as closely as its findings

Those questions apply to the paper’s own sourcing, too. The data partner has a commercial interest in the subject, and the relationship runs deeper than a data license. Jellyfish describes itself as an “AI Impact Platform,” and the authors have published on its blog: a Sept. 25, 2025, post by Chen and Stratton described itself as “part of our ongoing work with Jellyfish.” The paper thanks the Jellyfish team “for their partnership” and states that Jellyfish reviewed the manuscript only to check for proprietary disclosure, not for scientific content, and that the conclusions are the authors’. Its funders were the Stripe Economics of AI Fellowship, a Harvard Kennedy School program on wealth distribution and the Schmidt Sciences AI at Work Program.

Jellyfish has also promoted an earlier version of the work. A Jan. 28 post on its blog by Nicholas Arcolano called it “a preliminary version of a new paper from our collaborators at Harvard Economics, Fiona Chen and James Stratton,” based on 100,000 software engineers across 500 companies. That post, and a March 17 Jellyfish press release after it, reported that AI was making coding faster and that “code quality doesn’t seem to be suffering.” The August version, built on a larger sample running through March 2026, reads the near-doubling in change requests through its model as a possible sign of lower draft quality. The two descriptions rest on different versions and samples, so they need not conflict, but a reader relying on Jellyfish’s summary would miss the quality question the current paper raises.

The paper also carries small internal inconsistencies that do not touch the headline agent estimates. Its introduction rounds the sample to about 700 firms and 700,000 workers, and its main text describes 17 interviews where an appendix describes 21. Other limits are structural rather than clerical: the underlying data are proprietary, and use of AI tools through individual licenses goes unrecorded.

None of those caveats overturns the central result, though the March 2026 data cutoff means a revision could move the figures. What the paper offers is a standard the two legal announcements discussed here do not meet: named authors, a disclosed method, a confidence interval and a measured checkpoint. If an agent cuts 40 hours of research and drafting to a few, how many of those hours reappear on the desk of the lawyer who signs the motion?

Self-host maps with PMTiles for $5 monthly on Bunny.net
Why: You reduce infrastructure costs by avoiding commercial API fees via range requests.
Hacker News ↗HN 464↑extracted 05:50 · 804 words

I came across Protomaps in research for my latest project, Mapboard.1 For most non-commercial projects, commercial APIs become prohibitively expensive if you ever go viral (Mapbox, for instance, charges $5 per 1k map loads once you’ve exhausted the free tier).

PMTiles, part of the Protomaps ecosystem, is a tile format that ships in a single file, accessible via Range requests, and is thus extremely portable and easy to deploy. For CDNs that support caching Range requests of large files, it requires no effort besides down- and uploading it to storage. For others, like Cloudflare, you can utilize an edge function to translate between the traditional x/y/z URL path and Range requests.

Getting some Maps

If all you want is the world map, getting it is as simple as going to the build page and downloading the latest daily build.

However, more fun can be had with the CLI. For example, you can extract data for only a bounding box or specific zoom levels, to get a significantly smaller file.

Deploying to Bunny.net

One great CDN for deploying this is Bunny.net (affiliate link). They support HTTP Range requests, don’t collect personal data by default,2 are headquartered in the EU, and offer low-cost access to a global network of CDN and edge storage servers.

Pricing varies based on region and replication factor of the edge storage. But generally you pay $0.01/GB ($0.005/GB on the smaller Volume network) for bandwidth to Europe and North America, and $0.01/GB/month (no replication) to $0.055/GB/month (maximum replication) for storage. A high degree of replication mostly matters for latency in case of a cache miss (as the data is further replicated when it’s cached at the CDN PoPs).

In practical terms, storing the 139GB world map with replication to most continents (5x) will run you about $5/month in storage fees. Loading a map of Europe on a laptop and zooming in on a city causes about 5MB of data to be transmitted, which would be $0.00005 — further navigations and reloads will be less expensive as more of the map gets cached locally. At $0.05/1k map loads that could be 100x cheaper than Mapbox. However, the actual bandwidth of course varies (less for people that bounce immediately, possibly tens of MB for heavy users). For Mapbox you only pay a flat fee per visit.

Uploading the file

It’s a good idea to create a storage zone with S3 compatibility enabled, as uploading via the HTTP API timed out after 15 minutes for me.

Then, with rclone installed, you can download and stream the file to the Bunny storage zone. Preferably in tmux or from a temporary VPS, if your connection isn’t great.

rclone config create bunnycdn s3 \ provider=Other \ access_key_id="<zone-name>" \ secret_access_key="<storage-key>" \ endpoint="https://<primary-region>-s3.storage.bunnycdn.com" rclone copyurl "<build-url>" "bunnycdn:<zone-name>/<filename>.pmtiles" \ --s3-chunk-size 256M \ --progress

Configuring the pull zone

Theoretically, at this point you just need to create a pull zone, select the storage zone as an origin, and you’re done. Practically, it would be sensible to change some settings.

Allowed CORS origins can be configured under Security > General > Allowed referrers, so that other websites cannot embed your tiles.

You’ll also want to verify under Caching that “Optimize for large object delivery” is enabled, as this makes it possible to cache Range requests.

You might also want to dissuade people from downloading the whole file and leaving you with a large CDN bill. The most practical way I’ve found for this is limiting the download speed to 10kB/s after 10MB transmitted in a single request (using the “Download speed limits” and “Limit after” settings under “Network limits”). Regular usage should never reach this amount. You may further want to introduce a requests/second limit (do note that legitimate usage can also quickly reach high numbers here) or a monthly bandwidth limit for the pull zone.

Embedding the Map

With the tiles deployed, the only thing left to do is to embed them in your website via MapLibre GL, for example. To do this, you’ll need to install the pmtiles npm package and add the protocol and a style to MapLibre as detailed in the official installation guide.

Congratulations, you now host your own cost-effective and highly available map of the world!
