ARC-AGI-2 costs drop when evolutionary search recovers verifiable code accuracy
Why: Your pipelines inherit this verifiability cost. Evolving outputs recovers accuracy while enabling early stopping.

Post-generation evolutionary search improves scores without extra model cost. This approach enables early termination against training examples, reducing computational budget by up to 49%.

Read the extracted text ↓

Abstract:Generative models produce many candidate solutions but provide no intrinsic signal by which to rank them. One mitigation is to demand a \emph{verifiable artifact}: a program that can be executed against the available evidence. A common concern is that requiring executable verification incurs costs in both solution quality and computational budget. We measure these two costs on the $120$-task ARC-AGI-2 evaluation set. Demanding a verifiable artifact from an LLM reduced the score from $93$ to $68$ of $120$ tasks ($77\%$ to $57\%$). Of those $25$ tasks, $21$ were reached by no program-producing configuration we ran, at any budget, by sampling or by search; yet for $27$ of the $28$ tasks that only the answer-only arm solved, some program arm held a program that reproduced every demonstration and still failed the test. The bottleneck is obtaining an explanation that \emph{generalizes}, not finding one. A short evolutionary search over two already-sampled programs, at no additional model cost, beats best-of-$2$ sampled code by $9.2 \pm 1.4$ tasks, and over a single sampled program beats $1$-shot sampling by $9.9 \pm 1.3$. Moreover, because a program can be checked against the training examples, any program-producing protocol can terminate early once a task is explained: under the same stopping rule, best-of-$n$ sampling declines up to $38\%$ of its budget and the evolved arm up to $49\%$, whereas an answer-only baseline cannot terminate early. Applying the same methodology to decoder synthesis for quantum error correction yields interpretable decoders evaluated against a certified panel of published decoders on held-out exam shots.

FileMark Anchoring Cuts Editing Costs by Nearly Half
Why: Your agent pipelines require lower latency and higher accuracy for long-horizon tasks.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 230 words

Experiments on large R files demonstrate that code-anchored feedback via FileMark reduces output tokens by 50.5% and hosted costs by 46.3%. The study confirms these gains in cost and correctness stem from the anchored format rather than prompt wording.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Tokens drive the cost and latency of generative AI (GAI) code editing, and correctness decides whether an edit is useful. We study how the format of a reviewer's feedback, a human-AI collaboration (HAIC) channel, impacts both. In a paired experiment on 36 synthetic base-R tasks with seven models, the same requested changes were delivered as a holistic list (control) or as the export of FileMark (treatment). That code-review extension anchors each comment to the code it concerns and quotes it. For the typical task, total tokens changed by -4.2% to +14.7% across models, while output tokens fell by 14.7% (Claude Opus) and 50.5\% (Claude Sonnet). Since an output token costs five times as much as an input token, the hosted cost of an edit on files over 100 lines fell by 20.0% (Opus) and 46.3% (Sonnet). The treatment raised the correct-run share of four of five local models by 24% to 46%. A decomposition experiment places these gains in the format, not the wording, and mostly in the quoted code. Misplaced anchors cut correctness sharply. Code-anchored feedback thus lowers hosted editing cost on large R files and makes weaker models more correct, provided the anchors are accurate.

SoK reveals open-weight LLMs now match frontier models in vulnerability research autonomy
Why: Your agentic workflows face faster, cheaper autonomous threats without improved defense timelines.

A Systematization of Knowledge paper introduces an autonomy scale for LLM agents, showing open-weight models have caught up to restricted frontier models from 2026. The authors correlate this capability spike with a doubling of disclosed critical vulnerabilities, noting defensive response times remain unchanged.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:LLM-based agents are becoming increasingly capable at discovering, validating and patching vulnerabilities in real software, and vendors and researchers report that this is already changing how vulnerabilities are found and fixed. These claims, however, are scattered across papers, vendor reports and disclosures and are rarely comparable. We introduce a framework that rates these capabilities on an autonomy scale modeled on the levels of driving automation, and qualifies each claim by its correctness, its availability and the strength of its evidence. We use this framework to systematize the published evidence on LLM capability in vulnerability research. We find that agents discovered, validated, and patched vulnerabilities in real-world software without human intervention before the restricted frontier models of 2026, and that open-weight models subsequently attained comparable autonomy for discovery and validation. We then measure the consequences of evolving LLM security capabilities in a decade of public vulnerability records, advisories and exploitation data. In 2026 the number of disclosed vulnerabilities more than doubled, with an increasing share rated critical or high, and severe bugs that had survived a decade or more in widely used code were reported several times more often than before. Yet the time between the stages of a vulnerability's life, from report to fix and from disclosure to attack, has not shifted noticeably so far. We grade the mitigations proposed in the literature with an explicit rubric and draw guidance for each group of stakeholders from the best-supported ones.

REVISE reduces model calls by 56 percent in agent workflows
Why: You need durable state and efficiency for long-horizon agentic tasks.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 277 words

The arXiv preprint introduces a validity-guided runtime for online revisions in structured agent workflows. It intersects deltas with dependencies to resume partially executed DAGs instead of discarding progress or restarting. Evaluated on coding-agent traces, the system reduces revision-to-correct-completion tokens by 13.26 percent.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Agent revisions expose a fundamental correctness--efficiency trade-off during concurrent execution. Discarding ongoing work preserves latest-version correctness but wastes progress that may remain valid, whereas reusing prior work preserves efficiency but risks propagating stale state into outputs and tool effects. Existing recovery strategies resolve this trade-off in an imbalanced way with coarse-grained policies: they either favor efficiency by allowing potentially stale work to continue, or favor correctness by restarting the workflow or recomputing a linear suffix from the earliest conflict, thereby discarding unaffected progress. We present \textsc{Revise}, a validity-guided runtime for fine-grained recovery in structured agent workflows. When a revision arrives, \textsc{Revise} first intersects its delta with recorded data and control dependencies and propagates the resulting impact through the partially executed DAG to identify affected work. It then stops invalid work, preserves validity-established progress beyond the earliest conflict, and recomputes only the affected region. Incomplete provenance conservatively expands recovery, while reused results are revalidated before commit. Analysis of real coding-agent traces show online recovery opportunities: 118 sessions retain observable work before a queued later message is delivered; across 167 overlapping assistant responses, enqueue-to-completion overlap reaches 56.55~s at p95. Across 300 challenging revision/commit executions, \textsc{Revise} matches a latest-version oracle with no stale outputs or effects. On unmodified LangGraph and LLMCompiler applications using Qwen3-14B, it reduces model calls by 40.6--56.0\% relative to full restart and by 31.3--43.6\% relative to suffix recomputation. Under serving pressure, it further reduces revision-to-correct-completion tokens by 13.26\% and improves SLO goodput by 3.07--5.43\%.

E.1. Release 19 #

Release date: 2026-11-19

E.1.1. Overview #

PostgreSQL 19 contains many new features and enhancements, including:

New REPACK command that reclaims disk space and reorganizes table contents, which can improve query performance. REPACK (CONCURRENTLY) allows repacking without blocking reads and writes to the table.

pg_plan_advice extension for controlling query planner decisions and pg_stash_advice extension that applies plan advice automatically based on the query.

Better performance in many areas, including automatic scaling of the number of I/O worker processes, quicker foreign-key checks, and further planning and execution optimizations.

Logical replication can now be enabled without a server restart when wal_level is set to replica.

Logical replication of sequence values.

Support for “read-your-writes” consistency on standbys via the new WAIT command.

Parallel autovacuum of table indexes and autovacuum prioritization through a new scoring system.

The above items and other new features of PostgreSQL 19 are explained in more detail in the sections below.

E.1.2. Migration to Version 19 #

A dump/restore using pg_dumpall or use of pg_upgrade or logical replication is required for those wishing to migrate data from any previous release. See Section 18.6 for general information on migrating to new major releases.

Version 19 contains a number of changes that may affect compatibility with previous releases. Observe the following incompatibilities:

Add server variable password_expiration_warning_threshold to warn about password expiration (Gilles Darold, Nathan Bossart) §

The default warning period is seven days.

Issue a warning after successful MD5 password authentication (Nathan Bossart) §

The warning can be disabled via server variable md5_password_warnings. MD5 passwords were marked as deprecated in PostgreSQL 18.

Remove RADIUS support (Thomas Munro) §

PostgreSQL only supported RADIUS over UDP, which is unfixably insecure.

Force standard_conforming_strings to always be on in the database server (Tom Lane) §

Dumps created using pre-PostgreSQL 19 versions of pg_dump or pg_dumpall, and using standard_conforming_strings = off, will not properly load into PostgreSQL 19 and later servers. Users should create dumps using PostgreSQL 19 or later versions of these applications, or use standard_conforming_strings = on.

Client applications still support operations with servers having standard_conforming_strings = off, for compatibility with old servers. The server variable escape_string_warning has been removed as unnecessary.

Disallow carriage returns and line feeds in database, role, and tablespace names (Mahendra Singh Thalor) §

pg_upgrade will also disallow upgrading of clusters that use such names. This was changed to avoid security problems.

Change the default index opclasses for inet and cidr data types from btree_gist to GiST (Tom Lane) § §

The btree_gist inet/cidr opclasses are broken because they can exclude rows that should be returned. pg_upgrade will disallow upgrading of clusters with btree_gist inet/cidr indexes.

Disallow system columns from being used in COPY FROM ... WHERE (Tom Lane) §

The values of such columns were not well-defined.

Change a json_array() call which returns no rows to return an empty JSON array (Richard Guo) §

This previously returned NULL.

Cause transactions to pass their READ ONLY and DEFERRABLE status to postgres_fdw sessions (Etsuro Fujita) §

This means READ ONLY transactions can no longer modify rows processed by postgres_fdw sessions.

Change default of max_locks_per_transaction from 64 to 128 (Heikki Linnakangas) §

Lock size allocation has changed, so effectively settings must now be doubled to match their capacity in previous releases.

Remove server variable io_workers (Thomas Munro) §

This has been replaced by four new variables; see Section E.1.3.1.2.

Change JIT to be disabled by default (Jelte Fennema-Nio) §

Previously JIT was enabled by default, and activated based on optimizer costs, but this costing has been determined to be unreliable. This change requires sites that are doing many large analytical queries to manually enable JIT.

Change parallel vacuum leaders and workers to share the cost-based vacuum delay limit (Bharath Rupireddy) §

Previously each worker process slept based only on the cost it incurred itself, so a parallel vacuum could consume several times the budget set by vacuum_cost_limit. With this change, VACUUM with parallel workers and cost-based delay enabled might run slower than in previous releases.

Rename column sync_error_count to sync_table_error_count in system view pg_stat_subscription_stats (Vignesh C) §

This is necessary since sequence errors are now tracked separately.

Rename wait event type BUFFERPIN to BUFFER and rename wait event BufferPin to BufferCleanup (Andres Freund) §

Remove wait event BufferContent and add more precise wait events BufferShared, BufferShareExclusive, and BufferExclusive (Andres Freund) §

Change index access method handlers to use a static IndexAmRoutines structure, rather than dynamically allocated ones (Matthias van de Meent) §

Remove optimizer hook get_relation_info_hook and add better-placed hook build_simple_rel_hook (Robert Haas) §

Change psql's \crosstabview command to honor the \pset null setting (Chao Li) §

Change full text search and pg_trgm to use the database's locale provider for character classification when parsing text (Jeff Davis) §

Previously, characters were classified using libc, regardless of the locale provider. In databases with a locale provider other than libc (e.g., ICU, builtin) and where the locale configured through that locale provider behaves differently from the LC_CTYPE setting configured for the database, particularly a LC_CTYPE of C, the output of some full text search and pg_trgm functions could change. When upgrading such databases using pg_upgrade, it is recommended to reindex all indexes which use full text search and pg_trgm after the upgrade, and to recompute any stored tsvector values.

Change how fuzzystrmatch functions handle non-ASCII characters (Peter Eisentraut, Jeff Davis) § §

dmetaphone() and dmetaphone_alt() now upper-case their input using the database's default collation rather than always using libc, and soundex(), metaphone(), and difference() now treat only ASCII letters as letters. When upgrading with pg_upgrade, it is recommended to reindex any indexes that use these functions after the upgrade.

Remove MULE_INTERNAL encoding (Thomas Munro) §

This encoding was complex and rarely used. Databases using it will need to be dumped and restored with a different encoding.

Monumental Labs deploys autonomous CNC marble carving with sim-to-real toolpaths
Why: You get concrete sim-to-real orchestration and durable state patterns for robotic workflows
Hacker News ↗HN 125↑extracted 05:50 · 1,268 words

We taught KUKA KR240 robots how to carve marble sculptures.

This month, my team and I at Monumental Labs announced the first end-to-end autonomous CNC stone carving system of its kind. Sebastian Marino, James Darby, and I presented it at Embodied AI NYC, hosted by NY Robotics and ZeroSpace Labs in Brooklyn. This post is a written version of our talk: how our system turns a 3D model and a block of marble into a finished sculpture, with no human in the loop.

Sim-to-real: the planned toolpaths in simulation alongside the robot carving them in stone.

From simulation to stone: a finishing toolpath on the Stanford bunny (left) and the marble bunny the robot carved from it (right).

Why stone, and why now

Civic buildings like Penn Station and Grand Central were once expected to be monumental. Stone carving never disappeared, but rising costs pushed it out of ordinary construction. At Monumental Labs we use machine learning, computational geometry, and optimization to cut that cost: de-extinction, not displacement.

Our goal is zero humans in the loop during the manufacturing process: a fully automated, end-to-end pipeline built for “lights-out” manufacturing. Aside from working towards lights-out factories, our mission is to continue to work with artists and designers to decide on the final desired sculpture, and hand carvers to finalize the surface quality of many of our pieces.

Monumental Labs HQ in Greenpoint, Brooklyn.

Roughing vs. finishing

Carving happens in two broad phases. Roughing is about material removal rate: getting as much stone off the block as quickly and safely as possible. Finishing is about surface quality: tracing the final form closely enough that what comes off the robot matches the design. The two phases call for different tools, different strategies, and different algorithms.

A magic trick: autonomous roughing paths

The running example in the talk was a magic trick: start with a block, carve a top hat, and pull a rabbit out of it. The rabbit is the Stanford bunny, a classic test model in computer graphics. We carved the top hat first partly for fun, and partly as a reminder of the magic that happens when robots and art come together.

Block

Top hat

Rabbit

The trick in simulation: block, top hat, rabbit.

Voxels

Everything starts with a voxel representation of the stone. Voxels let us accept arbitrary mesh inputs and store them in a sparse data structure, which matters because a dense grid scales cubically with resolution. From the voxels we compute a signed distance field, along with normals and gradients. That gives us a natural basis for constructive solid geometry, which is exactly what subtractive manufacturing is: the stone minus everything the tool has removed.

The stock and target as a sparse voxel grid, and a close-up of the grid at the surface.

Bulk material removal

The first job is primary bulk material removal: clearing the large volume of stone between the raw block and the rough shape of the part.

Bulk roughing passes for the top hat, and the result in marble.

What is a toolpath?

A toolpath is the route the cutting tool follows through the material, eventually written out as G-code that the machine executes point by point. A roughing toolpath has its own anatomy: isoplanar passes that step down through the stone one level at a time, with each pass planned around how much of the tool is engaged in the material.

A roughing toolpath around the top hat, from the front and side.

Then the trick continues, and the same machinery starts working the bunny out of the hat.

Fine roughing and off-axis roughing

As we get closer to the final surface, we switch to smaller tools and finer passes. Some geometry can’t be reached by cutting straight down, so off-axis roughing cuts along tilted planes to clear material under ears, chins, and other overhangs.

Fine roughing: closer to the surface, with smaller tools.

Off-axis roughing along tilted planes.

At the end of roughing, the trick is complete in real stone: block, top hat, rabbit.

How can a robot finish a sculpture with no human in the loop?

Finishing is where the problem gets hard. The system takes two inputs: the simulated output of roughing, and the target design model. Its job is to close the gap between them.

Finishing inputs: the simulated roughing output (left) and the target design model (right).

Layering

We use voxel grid dilation and mesh intersection cuts to solve for optimal finishing layers, taking the remaining stone down in a sequence of offset surfaces until the last layer is the design itself.

Layer 1, layer 2, and the final layer.

Automated point-cloud segmentation

Each layer is then automatically segmented into regions that can be machined together, so every region gets its own toolpath and its own tool orientation.

Generating the toolpath: 3D iso-geodesic distances

Within a segment, we generate the toolpath from geodesic distances. Euclidean distance is a straight line, which on a curved part would pass through the stone. Geodesic distance is measured along the surface. The result is a set of curvature-informed isolines on the bunny’s surface, evenly spaced in a way that is optimal for toolpathing. We pair this geometry with machine learning to plan toolpaths that adapt to each sculpture.

Euclidean distance cuts through the stone; geodesic distance follows the surface (left). Curvature-informed iso-geodesic lines on the bunny (right).

Optimal toolpath connections

The isolines on their own are separate curves. We solve for a continuous, smooth, and optimally connected toolpath per segment, so the tool stays in the material and moves predictably instead of lifting and repositioning between passes. Machine learning helps inform how these connections are made.

Tool orientations and kinematics

Every segment also needs a tool orientation the robot can actually reach. We solve for collision-free tool orientations by segment, using access directions and tool tilt along the toolpath.

From there, we solve for safe, smooth, and collision-free joint angles for every point on the toolpath. In our proprietary toolpathing app below called Hilbert, we monitor joint angle health and visualize the kinematics limits of our various policies.

Kinematics in Hilbert: the robot following the finishing toolpath in simulation.

Why we carved the Stanford bunny

Since Greg Turk and Marc Levoy scanned a ceramic rabbit at Stanford in 1994, the Stanford Bunny has been graphics’ shared test model for three decades of research in reconstruction, mesh processing, and rendering. Our work would not have been possible without the computational geometry that grew up around it: signed distance fields, volumetric shape representations, and geodesic distances on surfaces, often tested on this very bunny. After milling ours in marble, we came across two old SIGGRAPH proceedings covers: the bunny rendered as stone in 2000, and a marble bust in 2001. We like to think this work makes us a small part of both graphics and robotics history, and we can’t wait to keep advancing the field.

Solid 2.0 Integrates Server Functions And Async Primitives
Why: You gain durable state management without manual race condition logic.
Hacker News ↗HN 378↑extracted 04:00 · 1,656 words

5 years is a long time to work on anything. It took 5 years from my first commit of Solid to 1.0, and it's been another 5 since then.

A lot changes in 5 years. Solid 1.0 made a bet that fine-grained reactivity, signals, was the right foundation for building user interfaces. Back then it was an unusual position. Today signals are everywhere. In Angular, in Svelte's runes, in Vue Vapor, and in a proposal before TC39 to put them in the language itself. It even pushed the conversation into React's compiler.

More recently that conversation has shifted to AI, arguably an even larger change in how we build. But Solid has always been about building the right things; the performant things. So for us the mission hasn't really changed all that much. The things that we identified as standout about our approach still stand out now. But instead of living in the shadow of more popular solutions Solid 2.0 carves out a path that others can follow. Deliberately this time.

Today that solution reaches stable.

Async Graph

If fine-grained reactivity's greatest strength is knowing the shape of our application, that knowledge shouldn't end with the process, or the machine the code is running on. Real applications are distributed. Real applications are born from data that lives on a server. The coordination of these pieces should be intentional and by design.

This starts with bringing async into the graph. A computation can return a Promise or an async iterator, and everything downstream just understands:

function Profile(props) {

const user = createMemo(() => fetchUser(props.id));

return (

<Loading fallback={<Skeleton />}>

<h1 class={{ stale: isPending(user) }}>{user().name}</h1>

</Loading>

);

}

This allows authoring by affordance while automating the handling of race conditions. The introduction of async doesn't cause an app refactor. Speculative state is automatically rolled back without manually written processes ensuring the server remains the authority on truth.

SolidStart introduce the world to server functions in early 2022, and now they have found their way home to Solid's core, better than ever. Not only do they support highly capable RPC calls over Request/Response but persistent connections over Server Sent Events. And consuming it remains as simple as the code below.

import { live } from "@solidjs/web/server-functions";

const stockPrice = live(async function* (symbol: string) {

"use server";

for await (const tick of subscribe(symbol)) yield tick.price;

});

// in your component

const price = createMemo(() => stockPrice())

This is only the beginning of what can be done with Server Functions and we will be announcing some new features (like reactive Server Components) in coming weeks that leverage this capability.

Built to Be Written by Agents

"But, we aren't writing the code anymore!" We hear you. A system built on constraints by construction ends up with less code to maintain and less edge cases. What used to be considered a DX shortcoming of reactive frameworks are now their greatest strength. And Solid 2.0 goes all in.

Every framework now says it "works great with AI", and usually that means models have seen a lot of it. That's a statement about training data, not about the framework. The question that matters is what happens when the agent gets it wrong.

In most frameworks a reactive mistake is silent. The app runs, it's just wrong, and the framework can't tell you, because it can't see it either. Solid can. A fine-grained graph knows exactly what was read, where, and what will update. So Solid 2.0's development builds report mistakes as they happen, with a stable code, the location in your source, and the fix:

[STRICT_READ_UNTRACKED] Reactive value read directly in an effect callback will not update. Move it into a tracking scope (JSX, a memo, or an effect's compute function).

in <App> › <Item> › effect

at http://localhost:5173/src/Item.tsx:12:19

[STRICT_READ_UNTRACKED] what it means and how to fix it: https://docs.solidjs.com/reference/solid-js/diagnostics/strict-read-untracked

[STRICT_READ_UNTRACKED] agent repair guide: node_modules/solid-js/skills/reactivity-diagnostics/SKILL.md#strict_read_untracked

These handle everything from strict errors to helpful heuristics. Thanks to async in the graph we can detect waterfalls, poorly constructed effect loops, poorly scaling graph shapes like wide graph fanouts and fan-ins. Today along with linting rules we can provide the best in class direction for agents at compile and at runtime.

This area is still evolving as we learn more about how agents build applications with Solid. The hard part, defining the primitives and semantics, is done. We've integrated with new dev tooling, and Chrome's Performance Tracks. And this capability goes beyond development as this observability powers things like our updated Sentry plugin so you can get the same information on your production builds.

We've also been looking at alternative syntax. Solid 2.0 adds experimental support for ReactiveScript and TSRX, both aiming to use compilation to further improve agent authoring.

One Framework for All

I've always been a big believer that React's super power is its composability. That it allowed the ecosystem to build on its humble pieces. However, we've learned a lot since 2013. At that point composable state, async patterns, and server rendering lacked the primitives to build upon and the ecosystem came up with their own solutions. This was fine for a while but tied React's hands and fractured their ecosystem. Something only a framework as popular as React could survive.

Newer frameworks have taken more on themselves and tend to only have one metaframework, or one router. We respect React's original positioning but have updated it for 2026. It isn't about restricting the solution but giving developers the primitives to build upon in the way React originally did. Server functions are example of this. Supporting any router, and cache solution for Single-Flight Mutation is another.

We retired our metaframework SolidStart, since every primitive needed to build a Solid Metaframework is now provided by the core or by framework agnostic Vite plugins. We believe that building an opinionated layer like a metaframework shouldn't have you needing to re-implement everything again. We've taken care of the hard parts so you can focus on what you care about. And these days a lot of that work is going to be taken on by your agent. This means less to maintain but no loss in capability.

And this makes it easier for the ecosystem. Solid 2.0 only requires one Clerk or Sentry plugin since we provide the primitives for Request/Response (built on web standards) and the hooks for observability that work across any library. We've already seen success with TanStack Query and Router being as capable as anything we've built first party, bringing in Tanner's impeccable attention to detail for the APIs people love to use.

Less is More

Somehow even though Solid 2.0 brings more capability to Solid, we've collapsed the surface area at almost every point. There are less primitives needed to be learnt, less ceremony around handling async. createResource, batch, startTransition, on, produce, createComputed, createMutable are all gone, because we no longer need them.

The equivalent code written for Solid 2.0 is shorter and clearer on intent. It informs our ability observe the runtime, provide better compiler errors, and removes the need for as many escape hatches (like createEffect) that can get us in trouble. This is something we believe important in an age of AI. Safe by construction rather than by exhaustion. Reduce complexity by deriving(reducing) state rather than putting it in a reducer.

// async cascading dropdown

function App() {

const states = createMemo(() => getStates("US"));

const [selectedState, setState] = createSignal(() => states()[0]);

const cities = createMemo(() => getCities(selectedState()));

const [selectedCity, setCity] = createSignal(() => cities()[0]);

return (

<Loading fallback={<p>Loading...</p>}>

<select onChange={(e) => setState(e.target.value)}>

<For each={states()}>{(state) => <option>{state}</option>}</For>

</select>{' '}

<select disabled={isPending(cities)} onChange={(e) => setCity(e.target.value)}>

<For each={cities()}>{(city) => <option>{city}</option>}</For>

</select>

<p>

Selection: {selectedCity()}, {selectedState()}

</p>

</Loading>

);

}

When the framework carries the guarantees, all loading state, race conditions, and cancellation logic disappears and what you are left with is the UI.

The Road Here

The first discussions of what a Solid 2.0 would look like started at the moment 1.0 was released. We saught feedback from the community on new reactivity ideas. The real effort started in late 2024. We announced the road to 2.0 in February 2025. Over a year of experiments followed, rewriting the reactive core from scratch until the async model held up to everything we threw at it. The beta landed in March, and the Release Candidate in August, with the API frozen.

Then 23 release candidates in 14 weeks. In that time alone, 971 pull requests were merged and 753 issues closed. We set one bar for stable. A full week with no regression in ordinary app code, and no crash or hang anywhere. It took a while to get there. By the end, the bug reports coming in were from people(agents) deliberately hunting edge cases in the deepest corners of the scheduler, and finding them.

So many people have touched the project over the past 5 years, from working on the initial reactivity prototypes, to testing the latest RC builds on their production apps. I've tried to thank as many of you as I can and attached hundreds of handles in the RC announcement. There are so many. There are also the many great organizations that have sponsored our development over the years: Sentry, Netlify, Google Chrome, JetBrains, Anomaly, Cursor, Code Rabbit, Anthropic, Tembo and so many more.

But mostly I want to thank everyone who has believed in the project. We've accomplished great things and there is much more to come. That isn't possible without all of you. Personally, the past 2 and half years have been some of the most difficult of my life, but knowing what we are building that impacts so many people leaves me with a sense of fulfillment. Solid has always been bigger than its core team, and 2.0 is the clearest proof of that yet.
