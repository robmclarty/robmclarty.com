Abstract:We introduce MANGO Arena, an open-source testbed in which four LLM agents each run one company in a serial supply chain adapted from MANGO, a classroom factory-management game. The environment enforces hard rules (a truck limit, a no-credit cash constraint and production capacity), logs every blocked attempt to break them, and gives agents a public message board on which side payments are explicitly forbidden. This lets one environment measure three things at once: an economic outcome (the bullwhip effect), rule adherence under pressure, and proposals to collude. In a pilot with three small reasoning models, two scripted reference policies, two conditions (with and without a supplier fire) and three demand seeds, all LLM agents amplified demand variability as it moved upstream, by 7 to 25 times at the Retailer and 13 to 179 times at the Supplier, often more than a textbook order-up-to policy. They did so largely by ordering in batches (zero orders in up to 60% of decisions). Real rule violations were rare (at most 7 of 288 decisions per model) and no agent proposed a money side deal. The pilot's main lesson concerns evaluation method. API failures (rate limits and runaway hidden reasoning) entered the metrics silently as "no order" decisions. And when we reran games with the same seeds and no failed calls, upstream bullwhip changed by up to 17 times between runs. With three seeds, conclusions about how a disruption affects a given model did not survive a rerun. We report these failures in full and propose reporting practices for multi-agent evaluations of this kind. Code, prompts and every logged decision are public.

Abstract:GPUs are expensive, yet latency-critical large language model (LLM) inference leaves part of them idle. Colocation reclaims this capacity by running best-effort work, such as fine-tuning, alongside serving. Many leading LLMs now use a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture, which adds a new source of idle time. Under expert parallelism, MoE experts are spread across GPUs, and because the router sends uneven numbers of tokens to each GPU, lightly loaded GPUs wait for the most loaded one. Even after applying load balancing solutions, such bubbles still take 8--13% of prefill time. We present BubbleTea, the first colocation system that leverages expert-parallel bubbles for fine-tuning. Unlike existing colocation systems, which make one scheduling decision for all GPUs per forward pass, BubbleTea predicts each GPU's bubble at every layer from the router's token assignment, and fills it with short chunks of fine-tuning work that reuse the weights already loaded for serving. We also introduce compiler passes that automatically derive these chunks from the served LLM as-is, without porting each model architecture to a separate training engine. Our evaluation on three MoE models, served on four A100 GPUs, shows that fine-tuning throughput with BubbleTea is 1.9--6.2$\times$ faster than with LLMStation, a state-of-the-art colocation system. BubbleTea keeps p90 inference latency within 10% of serving alone in 50 of 63 workloads, compared to 44 for LLMStation. On bursty production traces, BubbleTea trains 3.3$\times$ faster than LLMStation and 14$\times$ faster than a stock trainer colocated under CUDA MPS, with p90 inference latency staying within 7% of serving alone.

Abstract:Agents that do science must choose informative experiments and reason from their results, but most discovery benchmarks cannot separate that skill from recall, unstated scoring rules or shortcut policies. We introduce Openhagen Worlds, a suite of 27 procedurally generated environments (some with finite truth spaces), from classic puzzles of experimental reasoning to simulator-grounded physics and chemistry, in which an agent runs experiments under a budget and commits an answer that a deterministic verifier checks against a hidden truth. Every observation states the complete scoring rule, and each of the 178 difficulty levels is calibrated: it is admitted only if, on 64 development instances, a scripted oracle succeeds on at least 0.75, the strongest plausible non-deductive policy on at most 0.25 and each gated shortcut on at most 0.35. The 92 core levels span all 27 families and are sized to train small models; the 86 frontier rungs extend the 15 discovery worlds with harder questions and with campaigns of three, five and eight or more committed stages on one hidden system. On the same 84 withheld frontier instances, one per family and rung, DeepSeek V4.1 Flash solves 32 at its default reasoning effort with 16,384 tokens per call and 57 at its maximum effort with up to 393,216 tokens per call (oracle: 79); the headroom that remains is largest in eight-stage campaigns. On-policy expert iteration gives a 9B model modest gains: its core-tier success on trained families rises from 0.090 to 0.148, partly through better action format, with no detectable loss on GPQA Diamond. A hosted service plays the core tier's withheld instances.

Abstract:World models operate in a world they cannot fully observe. When a hidden disturbance, such as a current pushing a boat, changes the outcome of an action, a counterfactual question follows: what would have happened under a different action, subject to the same disturbance? Its answer separates bad decisions from bad luck. We prove that world models trained on factual transitions cannot answer it on their own, whatever their data or capacity. For many physical systems, the observed outcome and a simple property of the dynamics suffice: natura non facit saltus, nature makes no leaps. When actions and disturbances act as separate forces, their interaction is second-order in time, so the counterfactual outcome is, to first order, the observed outcome plus the difference between the world model's predictions under the alternative and factual actions. This yields a counterfactual world model with no counterfactual data, extra parameters, or retraining. For transitions of duration $h$, we prove an $O(h^2)$ bound on the counterfactual error relative to the disturbance variance, establish a matching minimax lower bound from unpaired factual data, and characterize failures at discontinuities, coordinate folds, and disturbances that scale the command. We validate these predictions from pixels with JEPA world models on frozen DINOv2 and masked autoencoder (MAE) encoders. On four rendered simulators, our approach improves counterfactual $R^2$ from $38$--$46\%$ to $86$--$96\%$ with the same world models; on a fifth, an underwater vehicle, it reaches $90$--$94\%$ with sixteen times more factual data. Four further frozen encoders, DINOv3, SigLIP~2, CLIP and EVA-02, give $81$--$95\%$ on the same simulators. In hindsight planning, it finds the action that would have reached the goal in $35$--$69\%$ of events, against $14$--$23\%$ for an oracle planner that knows the simulator but not the outcome.

15M web servers on one VM using user-space Rust
Why: You need this for extreme density optimization in low-level systems engineering.
Hacker News ↗HN 212↑extracted 04:00 · 2,809 words

A developer bypassed the Linux kernel network stack to run 15 million concurrent connections on a single virtual machine. By replacing TCP/IP handling with smoltcp and leveraging DPDK/AF_XDP, idle memory usage dropped to approximately 620 bytes per instance.

Read the extracted text ↓

Put a web server online and everyone starts trying to hack it. Why? A basic rule of economics. Short supply, big demand - there aren’t enough web servers for everyone. Let’s run a web server for every human on the planet, all 8 billion of them. I couldn’t get funding, so we’re bootstrapping. The goal is to run 15 million web servers on one VM with 10GB of RAM and 1 CPU core. If we succeed, I’ll find some RAM from old microwaves around the house and we’ll scale it up so everyone can have their own web server. Demand will drop to zero and cybersecurity will be solved once and for all.

Web server

Instead of vibe coding it all, let’s use an ancient technique called learning. First of all, what is a web server? There are a few essential syscalls.

listen - syscall, asks kernel to listen for connections on a socket

accept - syscall, accepts a connection on a socket

recv - receives data from a socket

send - sends data

close - closes

What is a syscall? A function that is executed by the OS kernel. Whatever fancy language or library you use for HTTP, it all comes down to those syscalls (well, yes, but actually no). Let’s use C to make it all explicit. We start listening, then read from the socket in a loop and always reply with OK.

int main(void) { // We use port 0 which makes OS return any available port int server = socket(AF_INET, SOCK_STREAM, 0); if (listen(server, 128) < 0) return 1; const char reply[] = "HTTP/1.0 200 OK\r\nContent-Length: 2\r\n\r\nOK"; for (int client; (client = accept(server, NULL, NULL)) >= 0;) { if (recv(client, NULL, 4096, MSG_TRUNC) > 0) send(client, reply, sizeof reply - 1, MSG_NOSIGNAL); close(client); } }

It couldn’t be simpler: listen on a socket, accept a connection, read the data, send a reply, close the client connection, and repeat. It gives us around 65k RPS. Now that we’ve learned everything, let’s finish the article and call it a day:

$ for i in $(seq 1 15_000_000); do echo $i; ./server &; done ... ... 28232 program exited with error 1

Hm, why did listen fail? Ah, we’ve run out of ports! We’re using port=0, so the kernel picks an available port from ip_local_port_range.

$ sysctl net.ipv4.ip_local_port_range net.ipv4.ip_local_port_range = 32768 60999

Why are so many ports skipped? Historically, port 0 was created for outbound connections. When you call connect('example.com', 80), Linux connects to the remote server (the sending part) and then allocates a random port on the host from ip_local_port_range to receive data (the receiving part). There are a few reasons the default range is small.

Port 0 is special. You cannot listen on it (actually, you can - https://acid.vegas/blog/the-port-that-does-not-exist/).

Ports 1-1024 require root.

Ports 1024-32768 are skipped to keep dynamically allocated ports from conflicting with existing apps. You don’t want to call connect('example.com') and end up allocating port 3306 to receive incoming data. mysql cannot start because you have example.com open in your Firefox would make for a confusing UX.

Ports 60999 - 65535 are excluded for historical reasons dating back to IP Masquerading.

Nothing prevents us from overriding the range and using 1-65535, but that’s still a bit short of 15 million. A UDP server and a TCP server can use the same port, so we can double that to 128k. We could run a proxy in front to forward requests to the right process (virtual hosting, yay), but these are all ancient techniques - in the AI era, whenever you have a software problem, you just throw money at the hardware. Let’s simply connect 15M/32k=450 new network cards to the server.

The 65k limit comes from the TCP standard. A port is a u16, so even if we hack the kernel with find '*.c' -exec sed -i 's/u16/u32/' \; to support bigger port numbers, normal clients still wouldn’t be able to connect. Each listening server is identified by a (protocol, IP, port) tuple. That explains how we can use the same port for UDP and TCP. It also means we can use a bunch of different IPs for our web servers. If we connect 450 network cards to the server, we’ll get 450 unique IP addresses and be able to listen on 15 million unique addresses!

UPD. I’ve heard from readers that the network card is the ONLY hardware component that didn’t get 100 times more expensive in the last year. Can’t we avoid ruining that too and solve this in software?

Let’s use a beautiful technology called AnyIP, which lets us listen on a whole subnet. Many VPS and dedicated server providers routinely give you a whole TCPv6 subnet. We can simply run sudo ip -6 route add local 2001:db8:1234:5678::/64 dev lo to get 18 quintillion addresses (plus or minus one) to listen on. You can do the same for IPv4 - acquiring a subnet with 15M addresses would cost around $250 million, so adjust the command for your environment. We’ll change our server to pick the nth IP from our range:

// Read IP index from CLI, shift base IP by it int i = read_cli_param(argv[1]); const uint32_t base_ip = 0x0a400001; // ex. 10.64.0.1 addr.sin_addr.s_addr = htonl(first_ip + i); // shift, ex. 10.64.0.2

And now we can finally finish the article.

$ for i in $(seq 1 15_000_000); do echo $i; ./server $i &; done ... ... Out of memory

Hm, one server consumes 1.5MB of RAM, so running that many would take… 20TB of RAM, which is a few bytes more than our 10GB budget.

Runtime

By pure coincidence (what are the odds?), I explained in Running 10 million Codex CLIs on one server - LFI processes why running millions of processes creates too much overhead. The answer is to run them all in one process inside a custom runtime, much as V8 can run millions of guests in a single process. In Userland Linux scheduler in Rust, I showed how to build a specialised userland scheduler and switch between tasks with the absolute minimum amount of RAM per task.

TLDR: We’re using LFI for sandboxing. It gives us memory isolation and replaces all syscalls with calls to our host handler, which lets us implement I/O however we like. To schedule multiple guests, we load them all into our main process’s memory and make the CPU constantly switch between guests - we become our own micro OS.

Since we need to save every byte and CPU cycle we can, we’re doomed to build another specialised runtime. Let’s get started. How much is 10GB divided by 15M, exactly?

Ten Great Britains divided by 15 million gives around half of kilo britain. Not much. If we run all our guests in our runtime, our custom scheduler needs to switch between them. What’s the absolute minimum state we need to preserve for each guest? We can ignore server initialisation and assume switches only happen within the main loop. Let’s take another look at the loop.

for (int client; (client = accept(server, NULL, NULL)) >= 0;) { if (recv(client, NULL, 4096, MSG_TRUNC) > 0) send(client, reply, sizeof reply - 1, MSG_NOSIGNAL); close(client); }

This code has only two state variables, server and client. Both are simple file descriptors. If our scheduler had to suspend a guest at any syscall point in this loop, we’d need to save the following state:

struct Context { pc: u64, // Instruction address to return to fd_server: i32, // server file descriptor fd_client: i32, // client file descriptor }

We only need 16 bytes per guest, so to run 15M guests in our runtime and be able to suspend them and switch between them, we would need 220MB of RAM. We’re playing it safe here - we don’t need the full u64 or i32, and with bit packing, we could probably get down to 8 bytes per guest. What else do we need?

$ codex "How much TCP connection takes?" Idle connection takes zero, while active connection consumes around 5kb

Here’s what we get if we assume that only 1M of connections are active at any given moment.

14M idle connections - 0 bytes

Scheduling state for 15M guests - 220MB

1M active connections at 5kb each = 5GB

Only half of our RAM budget, yay! I don’t often exceed my own expectations. Let’s validate it:

int main(void) { // Run 14M of idle connections - just listen and do nothing // SHOULD CONSUMER ZERO MEMORY! for (int i = 0; i < 14_000_000; i++) { int server = socket(AF_INET, SOCK_STREAM, 0); listen(server, 1) } }

$ ./run_15m_idle_servers Out Of Memory $ dmesg | tail -n 1 'SHOULD CONSUMER ZERO MEMORY' - what are you, stupid?

Looks like the kernel still has some leftover comments from Linus. I’m not stupid, but I have to admit I was below average here. While an LLM is a significant boost for learning, it’s too easy to accept its answers without thinking critically and feel ashamed later on. But hey, embarrassment is a cost of entry - let’s dive into networking in the kernel.

Networking in the kernel

Remember when I said that our simple server uses only 5 syscalls? I missed one that was right in front of me. Let me highlight it:

int main(void) { for (int i = 0; i < 10_000_000; ++i) { socket(AF_INET, SOCK_STREAM, 0); } }

That code will OOM in a 10GB VM. The socket syscall allocates around 3300 bytes, whether the socket is active, listening, or doing nothing. Linux was clearly written at a time when RAM was big and cheap, sigh. Let’s ask AI where all the memory goes:

Potato-potato, TLDR, the kernel uses that memory for many things. I was hoping to see some buffer: [u8; 3300], but it doesn’t look like we can optimise much without rewriting half the kernel. It’s Sunday and I have few tokens left, so we’ll have to think instead - how can we bypass the kernel?

What are our options? Engineering wisdom tells us that if we can’t solve a task, we’re working at the wrong level of abstraction. Hm… level, level, layer. Wait a minute! We started with HTTP and went level down to TCP/IP. That reminds me of something! Mom! Mom! Where are all my university books? I NEED THEM NOW!

The OSI model is… um… something I studied at university. Yeah, that’s a good definition. We started with our app and HTTP at the application layer, then went down to TCP/IP at the transport layer. There are three more layers below that. Can we go lower to get full control and solve our RAM problem? Oh yes, that’s exactly what operating at the network and data link layers lets us do. What about going to the lowest?

Clear.

Each layer wraps the data from the layer above it.

TCP/IP is stateful, but everything below is stateless. None of the lower layers know or care about the payload, so nothing prevents us from sending new IP packets or even Ethernet frames with our own handcrafted binary data. We just need to reimplement the TCP and IP stack ourselves. Are there any examples? Embedded folks have solved this millions of times. The most extreme case is Adam Dunkels’s Miniweb - it combines a custom TCP/IP implementation and an HTTP server that together need only 28 bytes (B-Y-T-E-S) to work.

There are plenty of less extreme, more mature implementations, though:

gVisor essentially reimplements the whole OS, including the network stack

Seastar is a whole runtime with its own network stack from the amazing team behind ScyllaDB

lwIP is a popular C implementation of TCP/IP

F-Stack uses FreeBSD’s TCP/IP stack

Go, C++, and C are nice languages (well, kinda), but they’re nowhere near as nice as our beloved Rust. So we’ll take smoltcp, which is purely amazing:

>smoltcp is a standalone, event-driven TCP/IP stack that is designed for >bare-metal, real-time systems. smoltcp does not need heap allocation at all

So, cargo add smoltcp and call it a day? Not so fast. smoltcp is essentially a smart parser and can’t do I/O. How does it communicate with your network card? There are 3 ways:

TUN is a virtual network interface that the kernel provides. It operates at the IP layer

TAP is another kernel feature that operates one layer down, at the Ethernet layer

DPDK lets you communicate directly with the network card using Ethernet frames, completely bypassing the kernel

smoltcp is kind enough to provide the first two options out of the box, and they’re easy to use.

fn main() -> std::io::Result<()> { let (mut device, config) = match std::env::args().nth(1).as_deref() { Some("tun") => ( TunTapInterface::new("smoltun", Medium::Ip)?, Config::new(HardwareAddress::Ip), ), Some("tap") => ( TunTapInterface::new("smoltap", Medium::Ethernet)?, Config::new(EthernetAddress([0x02, 0, 0, 0, 0, 2]).into()), ), _ => panic!("pass tun or tap"), }; let mut iface = Interface::new(config, &mut device, Instant::now()); iface.update_ip_addrs(|addrs| { addrs.push(IpCidr::new(IpAddress::v4(192, 0, 2, 2), 24)).unwrap(); }); let mut sockets = SocketSet::new(Vec::new()); // Main network loop loop { let now = Instant::now(); iface.poll(now, &mut device, &mut sockets); wait(device.as_raw_fd(), iface.poll_delay(now, &sockets))?; } }

But what about the third option, DPDK? When you care about latency A LOT, every syscall is a bloody wound. The syscall itself might take 500ns, and copying data from the kernel to userspace might take another 1000ns, depending on the buffer size. That sounds tiny, but for perspective: it’s common for high-frequency trading firms to saturate 400Gbs network interfaces. That means processing up to 600 million packets per second, or roughly 1.7ns per packet.

DPDK lets your program communicate directly with the hardware network card using Ethernet frames, bypassing the entire kernel network stack. You read from and write to a memory-mapped buffer without syscalls: when you want to send data - you simply write it to the buffer and you’re done; when you want to read what you’ve received - it’s already there.

There’s another, less obvious advantage - what happens when a new packet arrives at your network device? An interrupt fires, the CPU core stops executing your app, switches to the kernel, processes the interrupt, and then goes back to your app. Now imagine A LOT of incoming packets, so many that your app gets interrupted all the time. It has a beautiful name, livelock, and I guess debugging it in production is its own level of hell. DPDK lets you avoid all that, but there’s a cost.

DPDK works by running a busy loop that takes up an entire CPU core. It constantly moves data between memory and the network device in both directions, kinda like that:

loop { send_from_app_memory_to_network(); receive_from_network_to_app_memory(); }

There’s another, less obvious nuance. DPDK requires exclusive access to the network device because it uses a driver that operates at the hardware level - two drivers can’t work with the same device at the same time. Once you enable the DPDK driver for your network interface… it disappears from the OS. Yep, nothing in ifconfig, if you enable it over SSH, you’ll lose SSH access too. I can imagine a lot of funny stories coming out of that.

Actually, there’s a fourth option now - Linux now has an AF_XDP subsystem that does something similar. It lets you bypass the kernel network stack and uses the same idea of memory-mapped buffers to get zero-copy I/O in your app. The kernel still owns the device and interrupts still happen as usual. When a new packet arrives, the kernel runs a simple eBPF filter that decides whether to send it directly to the userspace buffer, bypassing the network stack, or process it normally. That means SSH still works. AF_XDP supports an alternative mode, SO_PREFER_BUSY_POLL, which is even closer to DPDK: you get a busy loop with no interrupts at the cost of a CPU core. DPDK still beats it because it bypasses the kernel completely, but AF_XDP is very close (and simply easier to manage).

To support DPDK and AF_XDP in smoltcp, we only need to implement the Device trait with 3 methods if we want full control. Alternatively, we can use the existing crates rust-dpdk-net for DPDK and xsk-rs for AF_XDP to help us.

Now the final flow is:

The runtime loads 15M web server guests into its memory

The runtime scheduler starts executing guests one by one

Guest syscalls are intercepted by the runtime and replaced by the smoltcp network stack and its adapters

All 15M guests start listening, each on its own address

TLDR; We built Blobasaur, a sharded, distributed, lightweight blob storage server on top of SQLite to solve our high-throughput blob read/write usecase. JSON blobs varying from a few KBs to MBs are now written and read thousands of times per second on commodity hardware while maintaining single-digit millisecond latency.

On Kite, our trading platform with ~20 million customers, users are able to customize their investment and trading screens heavily. They save multi-chart layouts, financial indicator templates, vector drawings, and various kinds of arbitrary UI preferences. In addition, we offer a Terminal mode that allows users to design their own workspaces by dragging, dropping, and customizing widgets. Under the hood, our backend services store these user preferences and metadata as opaque blobs (mostly JSON) ranging from a few kilobytes to several megabytes.

Many years ago, we stored such blobs in CouchDB, which turned out to be ill-suited for the kind of throughput we were starting to see. We then moved to SSDB (ssdb), a C++ disk-backed store that speaks the Redis protocol. This made it very easy to integrate as well. SSDB solved our immediate performance problems and ran reliably in production for several years.

Over time, two problems forced us to look for a replacement:

SSDB became unmaintained, which was an obvious operational risk. It is written in C++, which is not a part of our stack or expertise, so forking and maintaining was also not viable.

Scaling and size limits. SSDB is not a distributed system and can only run on a single node. This imposes significant future scaling challenges, including at one point forcing us to cap blob sizes at 1 MB because latency degrades on larger payloads.

As we added features like the Terminal mode and onboarded more internal services, we needed a robust solution. Our criteria were:

A key-value blob store with a simple API.

Designed for multi-megabyte blob read/write ops and high throughput. We sustain loads of thousands to tens of thousands of requests per second reading and writing arbitrary blobs. These are unpredictable and bursty thanks to the volatility in stock markets.

Horizontally scalable, distributed.

Disk-space efficiency / compression.

Easily maintainable and highly economical.

So we investigated FOSS databases optimized for high-throughput blob storage and looked at the following:

ScyllaDB: We already run ScyllaDB for high-throughput workloads, but in our tests, it turned out to be suboptimal for large blobs. Significantly lower throughput and higher resource usage. We specifically want multi-megabyte blobs. Tombstones from frequent overwrites and TTL expiries require careful tuning in ScyllaDB.

PostgreSQL: We already have considerable experience with Postgres in our stack and experimented with storing blobs as BYTEA columns, but it turned out to be severely suboptimal again for the use case in terms of performance and disk-space efficiency.

YugabyteDB: Has blob storage as one of the options, but is not primarily designed or optimized for it. Also, a large system with too many features.

RocksDB: Had the right primitives, but again, not architected for high throughput multi-MB blob writes.

Ceph: Severe operational complexity and resource requirements. We didn’t even get to trying it out.

SeaweedFS: This was the closest candidate. However, there were a few blockers. Our use case is overwrite-heavy (a user changing their workspace) over the same key, and SeaweedFS keeps appending before compaction, which can cause disk usage to grow uncontrollably. Blobs have their own IDs, which then have to be mapped against user keys in a separate DB, adding another layer. It doesn’t support TTL per blob. Also, reasonably complex setup with many parts.

S3 and MinIO: Object storage has high per-request latency for small blobs, high request costs under write-heavy workloads (writes are 50% of our traffic), and coarse lifecycle TTLs instead of exact key expiry. Not to mention, S3 would incur significant costs given thousands of ops per second and tens of millions of ops per day.

Raw filesystem (one file per blob): Putting blobs directly on disk meant implementing atomic writes and write batching, managing large numbers of files and directories, good old atomic read/write disk performance and inode headaches, and maintaining a separate metadata index for TTLs. Not to mention, orchestrating this over AWS storage volumes.

Around this time, we were toying with SQLite for another project and were marveling at its 35% Faster Than The Filesystem claim. We also happen to have a query cache system that creates millions of tables every day in a Postgres instance, which pushes the database to its limits quite successfully. Putting two and two together, we thought, why not build a system on top of SQLite to store millions of blobs and see what happens?

So we ended up building Blobasaur, an open source sharded blob server written in Rust that speaks the Redis protocol and uses SQLite as its storage engine. It is a single binary application that is high performance, and super simple to deploy and maintain. It is in production at Zerodha and it has successfully replaced SSDB.

What we needed

We had several Go and Python services already talking to SSDB via the Redis protocol, with more waiting to onboard. We wanted the new solution to meet these requirements:

Redis protocol compatibility (if possible at all): Every existing client already used standard Redis libraries (GET, SET, HGET, HSET, DEL, EXPIRE). If we could get a system that could be a drop-in replacement, that would allow us to leave existing services untouched and reduce risk significantly.

Low latency across payload sizes: It needed to serve both 2 KB settings blobs and 5 MB chart layouts with single-digit millisecond latency.

Built-in compression: Almost all our payloads are repetitive JSON, which compresses well.

Simple CRUD with TTLs: We didn’t need complex data structures, server-side scripting, or secondary queries. We just needed key-value and hash CRUD with key expiry.

Multi-node partitioning: We needed to split data across multiple machines so a single node wasn’t a bottleneck.

Relaxed durability is acceptable: This store holds non-critical UI and workspace preferences, and not critical financial data. If a sudden power loss drops recent writes, a user might lose a recent chart layout tweak, which is annoying, but it never affects trading. This was a good performance trade-off for our use case.

Why SQLite (and Rust)

We had a few options for the storage. The obvious one is the raw filesystem, with one file per blob. But with that, we’d need to invent everything ourselves:

Atomic writes and batching.

TTL cleanup, which needs secondary indexes or walking the directories.

Dealing with fd limits.

Managing metadata separately from the raw data.

SQLite pretty much gave us everything that the raw filesystem lacked. For small blobs, SQLite is up to 35% faster than raw filesystem I/O. While raw files start to beat SQLite above ~100 KiB, we compress blobs before writing them. And thankfully, 99% of our compressed values sit well below 100 KiB.

Using SQLite also meant inheriting a battle-tested storage engine with ACID transactions, crash recovery, and standard tooling. Any shard on disk can be inspected with the standard sqlite3 CLI or queried with plain SQL for analytics, etc.

While most of our backend stack is written in Go, we wrote Blobasaur in Rust. Go’s standard SQLite driver requires CGo, which complicates static builds, pins OS threads during C calls under high concurrency, and adds garbage-collection pressure when allocating and compressing multi-megabyte buffers. With Rust (sqlx, rusqlite, and tokio), we call SQLite’s C API directly with predictable memory usage and ship a single static binary.

Architecture

Splitting data into shards

In WAL (Write-Ahead Logging) mode, a single SQLite database allows concurrent readers, but still permits only one writer at a time. Since writes make up roughly 50% of our production traffic, a single SQLite file per node would bottleneck quickly.

To get write concurrency on a single machine, Blobasaur splits data across multiple SQLite database files on disk, called shards:

Consistent hashing: Each key maps to one shard via multi-probe consistent hashing.

Per-shard writer with group commit: Each shard has a dedicated writer task fed by a bounded queue. Instead of committing one key at a time, the writer drains a batch of pending writes from the queue and executes them inside a single BEGIN IMMEDIATE ... COMMIT transaction. This amortizes transaction and WAL overhead across concurrent clients.

Concurrent readers: Reads go directly to a per-shard pool of read-only SQLite connections and can run concurrently with the writer.

How much does sharding help?

We benchmarked 1 shard against 16 shards using a load generator opening 64 concurrent connections. Each request used a 64 KiB JSON blob payload (close to our production average), which zstd compresses roughly 9x.

All runs used 100,000 keys, synchronous writes, and batch_size = 50, with the data directory on tmpfs to isolate SQLite locking and CPU overhead from disk variance. Both setups received the same total resources: 1,600 MB of SQLite page cache and 64 total read connections.

With 16 shards, write throughput increases 3.3x and median write latency drops from 8.2 ms to 2.1 ms. Read throughput stays nearly identical because SQLite WAL mode already serves concurrent readers without contention.

At 25,324 HSET operations per second, each carrying a 64 KiB payload, Blobasaur processes roughly 1.66 GB/s of uncompressed JSON through zstd. Both the server and load generator ran on the same 16-core laptop, sharing its CPU resources.

Schema

Our services use both plain keys (SET/GET) and Redis hashes (HSET/HGET) to group blobs by service or feature (for example, HSET prefs userID <json>).

Inside each shard, plain keys live in a blobs table:

CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS blobs ( key TEXT PRIMARY KEY, data BLOB, created_at INTEGER NOT NULL, updated_at INTEGER NOT NULL, expires_at INTEGER, version INTEGER NOT NULL DEFAULT 0 ); CREATE INDEX IF NOT EXISTS idx_expires_at ON blobs(expires_at) WHERE expires_at IS NOT NULL;

SET writes to blobs. HSET writes to a table for its namespace, blobs_<namespace>, with the same columns. For example, HSET prefs user42 ... stores user42 in blobs_prefs.

Expiry is stored in expires_at on the same row. Read queries filter out expired rows at read time (expires_at IS NULL OR expires_at > ?), while a background worker reaps expired rows asynchronously. Because the index on expires_at is a partial index (WHERE expires_at IS NOT NULL), rows without a TTL take up no space in the expiry index.

Compression

Blobasaur supports zstd, lz4, gzip, and brotli compression before writing to SQLite. We run zstd in production.

With SQLite’s default 4 KiB pages, shrinking a 63.6 KiB payload to 5.5 KiB reduces its storage footprint from roughly 16 pages to roughly 2, before accounting for row and index overhead. Fewer pages holding blob data means less pressure on the page cache and less data to write through the WAL and into the database.

Durability

Because we don’t require strict durability for our specific use case of UI preferences, shards run in WAL mode with PRAGMA synchronous = NORMAL. SQLite does not fsync on every transaction commit. An OS crash or power loss can lose recent committed transactions that have not yet been synced to durable storage, while preserving database consistency.

For workloads that want even lower write latency, Blobasaur also supports an optional async_write mode that acknowledges writes as soon as they enter the shard’s memory queue, before the SQLite batch commits.

Scaling across machines

To scale beyond a single server, Blobasaur implements the Redis Cluster protocol, making it compatible with standard cluster-aware Redis clients. Like Redis Cluster, the keyspace is divided into 16384 hash slots using CRC16. Each Blobasaur node owns a configured range of slots, and nodes discover each other via gossip. If a client sends a key to the wrong node, the node responds with a MOVED redirect pointing to the owner node.

This gives Blobasaur two-level routing:

Cluster level: CRC16(key) % 16384 selects the machine.

Node level: Multi-probe consistent hashing selects the SQLite shard on that machine.

There are some limitations right now. At present, slot assignment is static in config. Live slot rebalancing between running nodes and automatic failover are not implemented yet.

Changing the number of shards

We chose multi-probe consistent hashing so resharding a node only has to move minimal data, without needing virtual nodes. With naive hash % num_shards, increasing from 4 to 5 shards moves ~80% of keys. With consistent hashing, only ~20% of keys move (the share belonging to the new shard).

Resharding is currently an offline operation via the built-in CLI:

Stop the server.

Run blobasaur shard migrate 4 5 --verify.

Update num_shards in config.toml and start the server.

The migration tool moves keys whose target shard changed. With --verify, it checks that keys are in the expected shards and reports post-migration row counts.

Moving off SSDB

We rolled out Blobasaur in a phased deployment strategy:

New workloads first: When we launched Kite Terminal mode, we pointed its workspace storage directly at Blobasaur.

Dual-write and backfill: For existing workloads like chart preferences, we updated the services to dual-write to both SSDB and Blobasaur while running a background migration for historical keys. Reads went to Blobasaur first and fell back to SSDB on a cache miss or error.

Cutover: On the client side, the only code change was switching from a single-node Redis client to a Redis Cluster client with the three Blobasaur node IPs.

After running dual-writes for a month with zero discrepancies, we turned off SSDB.

What we learned along the way

Deleting rows doesn’t shrink SQLite files

Early on, we noticed disk usage only grew, even after millions of keys expired or were deleted. By default, SQLite keeps freed pages on an internal freelist for future writes rather than returning space to the OS.

To fix this, we enabled PRAGMA auto_vacuum = INCREMENTAL across every shard and added a blobasaur shard vacuum subcommand to reclaim unused freelist pages within a set allocation (128 MB default). Since shrinking the actual database file in WAL mode requires a checkpoint, Blobasaur runs PRAGMA wal_checkpoint(TRUNCATE) right after executing an incremental vacuum.

Async writes have to be visible to every read

When async_write is enabled, a write returns OK while still sitting in the shard’s memory queue waiting for the next batch commit. To preserve read-your-writes consistency, queued writes are also placed in an in-memory pending map that reads check before querying SQLite.

During testing, we hit edge cases where certain read and existence-check paths bypassed this pending map or didn’t account for deletes. For example, a DEL followed immediately by a GET could return the old value from SQLite because the queued DEL hadn’t committed yet. We fixed this by storing explicit tombstones in the pending map and routing every read (GET, HGET, EXISTS, TTL) through it first.

In production, our p99 write latency normally sat below 5 ms, but we occasionally saw spikes to 30 to 50 ms.

We traced the spikes to the background TTL cleanup task. Initially, the cleanup worker opened its own write connection and ran a single large DELETE statement for all expired rows. That large DELETE contended with the shard’s main writer for SQLite’s single write lock, stalling normal client batches.

We fixed this in two ways: routing cleanup batches through the shard’s existing writer connection so they take turns cleanly with client writes, and bounding each pass with cleanup_chunk_size (1,000 rows by default). Since expired rows are already filtered out at read time, spreading physical deletion across smaller chunks eliminated the latency spikes without affecting correctness.

In production

Our current Blobasaur deployment runs as a three-node (4 vCPU, 8 GB RAM) cluster. Over a full trading session (09:00 to 15:30), p99 latency across all commands stays around 2 ms on every node, and p50 sits between 250 and 550 µs.

Broken down by command, HSET p99 sits around 2 ms, HGET p99 around 1.4 ms, and HDEL p99 mostly under 1 ms.

Gemini 4 Autonomously Proves Weil Conjectures via /teamwork
Why: You observe context partitioning and falsifier patterns for long-horizon agent verification.
Hacker News ↗HN 214↑extracted 04:00 · 2,966 words

Over the past several weeks, teams across Google and academic partner institutions have tackled open problems in math, science, and complex systems engineering that had remained open for years. Using Gemini 4 Argon, Gemini 4 Carbon, Gemini 4 Hydrogen and multi-agent workflows such as Antigravity’s /teamwork (building on various Gemini models), these collaborations have produced new innovations across information theory, formal verifications, molecular structure discoveries, and kernel optimization, along with several production infrastructure gains at Google. We are making these agentic tools available in Antigravity’s /teamwork (alongside /boost and other agents) so that researchers and developers can apply them to their own work.

Frontier research and systems engineering require navigating vast literature, building custom computational pipelines, testing against physical measurements, and verifying every step. Progress comes from an interactive discovery loop where researchers frame questions and define the theoretical or physical constraints, while long-horizon agents write custom solvers, explore large search spaces, test candidate designs, and check long chains of logic.

Scaling long-horizon reasoning

Two limits of current language models motivate /teamwork's design. First, model generation can be unpredictable, leaving a gap between a single attempt and finding the correct answer. The system bridges this gap by pairing parallel exploration with dedicated falsifiers (e.g., the Lean 4 proof checker, unmodified software test suites, execution simulators, web search, or hardware counters) to automatically verify which candidate solutions work.

Second, AI models often lose track of details over multi-day tasks because attention disperses across growing context windows. To prevent this, /teamwork partitions context by construction: each specialized agent receives only the exact files and tools it needs for a specific step. Coordinators hand off tasks to fresh successors, while critics and auditors challenge and verify results from clean contexts without the original producer's reasoning.

Teamwork expresses these workflows as declarative self-improving natural-language patterns and dynamically routes each user prompt to the pattern best suited to the task. These patterns range from distributed coding and iterative problem-solving (packaged standalone as /boost, a sequential solve-and-verify loop refined from prior trajectories) to specialized research workflows like long proof and document review, all of which are available in Antigravity today. This allows teams to design custom specifications from past run transcripts routing across different harness patterns and dynamically improving agentic harness. While many of these features are available in Antigravity, we will provide more updates in the coming weeks providing all the features to our external users on Antigravity. Following our previous posts on Antigravity /teamwork, we provide new results to open challenges empowered by new variants of /teamwork and Gemini 4 models.

Mathematics and theoretical computer science

Across mathematics and theoretical computer science, researchers paired with Gemini 4 and /teamwork harnesses to collaborate on large-scale proofs and generate complete algorithms and proofs directly from open problem statements. We present several examples.

Proving and verifying the Courtade–Kumar conjecture

Researchers from Google, CUHK, USC, and CMU used /teamwork's Long Proof pattern (implementing Stellar Colosseum in Antigravity) to prove information theory's 2013 Courtade–Kumar conjecture in full generality. The result confirms that when a random bit string passes through a noisy channel, no compression of the input to a single bit preserves more information than a "dictator function", which just copies a single input bit. Gemini first found counterexamples to a generalized conjecture from the CUHK team's earlier paper. The CUHK team then proposed a new candidate function that reduced the conjecture to a 14-variable optimization problem, along with a key analytical lower bound. Gemini 4 Argon and earlier Gemini models then partitioned the parameter space and developed most of the new analytical bounds and interval-arithmetic certificates. They also extended the proof from balanced to unbalanced functions without human input. The team formalized the entire proof in Lean 4, producing 50 million lines of code.

Resolving open questions in optimization, statistics, quantum computing, and algorithms

Researchers also posed open problems directly to Gemini 4 and to multi-agent harnesses such as Stellar Colosseum and /teamwork, receiving end-to-end algorithms and proofs (often with Lean 4 formalizations) that the authors verified, simplified, and edited for publication. In adaptive data analysis, a multi-agent harness running on Gemini 4 Argon independently proved and Lean-verified near-optimal sample bounds for statistical queries chosen adaptively from earlier answers, closing a gap open since 2014. These bounds yield the first nearly space-optimal algorithm for data streams with both insertions and deletions that stays accurate against adaptive adversaries. In quantum computing, a new construction settled a 2014 open question by finding the first n-bit Boolean functions needing strictly more than n/2 quantum queries at constant error, while pinning down the exact query overhead across symmetric functions.

Several results break long standing barriers in processing and compressing large matrices and graphs. For fifty years until STOC 2024, combinatorial algorithms for Boolean matrix multiplication and triangle detection (avoiding the algebraic cancellations of fast matrix multiplication) saved only logarithmic factors over cubic time. A new deterministic algorithm resolves STOC 2024's main open question, achieving its target speedup over cubic time. In high-dimensional geometry, a new lower bound resolves an open problem on sparse networks, proving that approximating all pairwise distances among n points within any constant factor can require polynomially more than n edges, even with extra Steiner points and arbitrarily many hops. In randomized linear algebra, stacked sparse sketches resolve a 2025 open question by breaking the classical sparsity barrier for compressing a matrix to linear dimension without collapsing any direction of an r-dimensional subspace, needing only √log r nonzeros per row (quadratically fewer than the log r required to preserve all lengths) and nearly matching a new lower bound. For weighted graphs under dynamic edge-weight insertions and deletions, Stellar Colosseum resolved an open question left by FOCS 2022, developing the first single-pass streaming algorithm for cut sparsification that achieves near-optimal memory, update time, and reconstruction time simultaneously.

Our results also span optimization, arithmetic, data structures, and statistics. In optimization, resolving an open question on online inverse linear optimization (inferring an expert's hidden objective from decisions over time), the approach gave a deterministic algorithm achieving optimal regret while cutting runtime from exponential in the dimension to polynomial time. For n-bit integer multiplication, multi-agent campaigns simplified the theoretical Turing-machine bound below n log n, while a separate effort gave a new O(n log n) algorithm on a single two-dimensional grid whose C implementation matches the GNU Multiple Precision Arithmetic Library (GMP) bit-for-bit up to a billion bits. In data structures, another result made significant progress on Tarjan's 1985 Deque Conjecture (whether self-adjusting splay trees handle insertions and deletions at both ends in constant amortized time), proving a new upper bound on the per-operation overhead that simultaneously beats every finitely iterated inverse-Ackermann function, verified in Lean 4. Finally, in high-dimensional statistics, the approach proved that approximate leave-one-out cross-validation, which avoids refitting the model from scratch, remains accurate for non-smooth sparse penalties like elastic net.

Scaling formal verification and manuscript review

Checking a computer-assisted manuscript by hand places a heavy burden on peer reviewers, and subtle flaws can hide inside long calculations. Formalizing mathematics in Lean 4 turns a proof into code that a computer kernel checks line by line. Once human mathematicians confirm that the formal theorem statement matches the original problem, the proof only compiles if every logical step holds.

Many of the deepest open problems in mathematics govern how physical waves propagate, how dynamical systems evolve, and whether polynomial equations have integer solutions. Working directly from informal proofs, our multi-agent framework, LEAP, developed with colleagues in Google Cloud AI and paired with Argon, has made progress in these core areas.

Notably, /teamwork-LEAP fully autonomously produced an end-to-end, machine-checked formalization of the Weil conjectures, the landmark results on counting solutions of polynomial equations over finite fields that culminated in Pierre Deligne's 1974 proof. This formalization spans about 1.8 million lines of Lean, produced across 28,800 subagent invocations. The system also synthesized over 919,000 lines of Lean to produce the first formalization of a recent proof of Hilbert's tenth problem over general rings of integers. It also independently reproduces an end-to-end formalization of a recent proof of finite-time blowup for the 3D Navier–Stokes equations with smooth forcing, part of one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems and open for about 90 years.

Beyond these, it also generated the first formalization of the Birkhoff conjecture, a dynamical-systems problem open since 1927. Moreover, it formalized for the first time the 3D Bochner–Riesz conjecture, a central problem in harmonic analysis open for more than 50 years, finding and fixing a gap in a recently released informal proof along the way. Additional machine-checked breakthroughs include resolving the C¹ regularity of infinity-harmonic functions in three and higher dimensions (an open question for about 20 years), formalizing Killip and Vişan's theorem that the KdV water-wave equation is well-posed in H⁻¹ and verifying a UCLA group's informal proofs of the 40-year-old Jamison Caterpillar Conjecture, the Ağırseven-Ollis conjecture, and an improved upper bound on Erdős Problem #1063.

Finally, we provided Deep Review for authors of another CS conference: ahead of STOC 2027, authors of 578 submissions (over a third of all submissions) opted in to receive Deep Review feedback on their drafts. In an ongoing author survey (N = 70), 90% of respondents rated the feedback mostly or almost all factual and 90% said they would use the tool again. Over 30% said Deep Review caught substantive proof bugs or mathematically significant typos, and 14% said it found technical gaps that took more than an hour to fix. Surveyed authors shared that “Tools of this kind can help produce cleaner and more precise research, faster” and that DeepReview provides peace of mind on scientific discovery”. We provide users two weekly calls to Deep Review for free. Deep Review is also implemented as /teamwork's document review pattern and available in Antigravity. Read more about DeepReview in our recent blog post.

Molecular chemistry discovery

Beyond formal proofs, pairing specialized generator agents with independent verifier agents enables discovery workflows in experimental laboratory science.

Determining a molecule’s structure from experimental nuclear magnetic resonance data is one of chemistry’s hardest puzzles. For example, in HIV protease inhibitors, one misplaced bond separates an inactive look-alike from a drug. By organizing 30 specialized agents into a self-correcting loop with /teamwork and a physics-informed spectral simulator, we achieved 95% top-1 exact match and 100% top-3 accuracy on complex benchmark molecules, nearly doubling single-agent performance. Beyond teaching Gemini to reason over structure, this lays the groundwork for ongoing works on 3D protein and complex structures dynamics.

Beyond this example, science problems including applications in nuclear fusion and quantum algorithms reported in the Gemini 4 blog post benefited from /teamwork and the Argon model.

Generating a causal inference benchmark

We also applied a similar multi-round auditing workflow to build empirical benchmarks. Starting from five human-specified acceptance criteria like data anonymization, 29 agents running on Argon over 11.5 hours turned 13 public tables into a 50-task causal inference benchmark. In these tasks, models must determine whether a causal effect can be identified and estimate it against a known answer. After initial checks, separate reviewer and auditor agents caught shortcuts, flagged wording hints, and vetoed hallucinated scripts, turning each into a permanent test. Automating this heavily manual task generation supplies the hard problems needed to help Gemini answer counterfactual "what-if" questions.

Coding and systems engineering

When paired with objective evaluators such as network benchmarks, game engines, unmodified test suites, and hardware timers, /teamwork campaigns can execute multi-day software and hardware engineering projects.

Distributed systems

Using /teamwork and Argon, we built FarArray, a low-latency kernel-bypass Partitioned Global Address Space based on the SNAP microkernel. Applications built on FarArray include lock-free Connected Components, which scale to 1-trillion-edge graphs with 60x to 90x throughput gains and 40x to 180x speedups at 6x lower RAM over TCP key-value stores. Similarly, InstantEmbedding (Personalized PageRank) reduced peak DRAM by up to 14x and runtime by 11x to 27x over accelerated MapReduce implementations. The team also used /teamwork and Argon to create custom TPU kernels for Graph Neural Networks, delivering up to a 185x High Bandwidth Memory reduction and 8x to 15x speedups in forward and backward passes. Further details are available in our recent Antigravity blog post.

Autonomous hill climbing

To test autonomous optimization over long horizons, hill-climbing teams on Argon trained a 9x9 Go engine from scratch starting from a basic AlphaZero implementation. Human researchers set the chip allocation and evaluation setup, while the harness ran autonomous campaigns for faster self-play (achieving a 2,300x speedup) and faster Elo readings, promoting champions strictly via statistical tests on locked records. In under eight days, the engine gained roughly 2,600 Elo, reaching 1,885 Elo, placing it between KataGo at 16 and 32 visits per move.

Memory-safe Rust rewrites, /boost, and TPU kernel optimization

Large code migrations fail when agents stub out dependencies or alter tests to make a broken port pass. A new /teamwork rewrite pattern, soon to be available in Antigravity, solved this by scheduling each module only after its dependencies compile, linking every leaf module against the original, unmodified test suite. Building on the initial 25% port of the Zircon microkernel, a two-week Argon campaign rewrote the remaining C++ code, adding about 800,000 lines of safe Rust that pass all kernel unit tests and boot on arm64 hardware. /teamwork also rewrote libgav1, Google's AV1 video decoder, from C++ into around 170k lines Rust, achieving 100% bit-exact parity across a broad conformance test suite. Initially, the port was around 4x slower than the original C++ code. However, after giving /teamwork access to profiling tools and a mandate to maximize speed on sample video clips, the Rust port became 20-30% faster than the original C++ and hand-optimized assembly code on both x86_64 and ARM.

For day-to-day coding tasks, the Antigravity /boost command packages /teamwork's iterative coding pattern. A long-running /teamwork campaign analyzed past run trajectories and found that tightly coupled code is best handled through sequential implementation and revision stages rather than parallel splits, improving /boost on coding tasks. This is an example of a harness optimizing another harness for token efficiency.

For machine learning systems, full hardware evaluations of custom JAX and Pallas TPU kernels take 7 to 32 minutes, limiting how many candidates prior optimization agents can explore. Using /teamwork's DuoDiscover pattern on Argon, four parallel agents identified performance bottlenecks and constructed fast, 23-second proxy evaluators called yardsticks. These yardsticks produce candidate orderings that are continuously validated against periodic full hardware runs using Kendall's rank correlation. DuoDiscover achieves a 4.09x geometric-mean speedup over reference implementations across 17 JAXBench kernels and a 2.99x geometric-mean speedup across 7 hand-optimized vLLM TPU-Inference Pallas kernels for frontier open-weight models.

Google infrastructure applications

We have also applied /teamwork and /boost to many production infrastructure and hardware modeling problems across Google. Examples include:

Fleet-wide memory optimization: Building on the initial Argon memory efforts, we analyzed fleet-wide metrics, as well as internal memory saving past successes, and created reusable /teamwork workflows that have identified memory saving opportunities, surpassing our initial savings estimates of 1 PiB. We also deployed the human-in-the-loop Memory Efficiency Monitoring Agent, achieving additional memory savings, using techniques such as struct padding elimination, scalar narrowing, cliff jumping, outlining, and tagged pointers.

Borg agentic scheduling: Built on /teamwork and /boost, Borg’s agentic workflow for scheduling optimization shipped 2 successful changes, resulting in a 24% reduction in TPU workload preemption on some hardware types, while preserving workload SLOs and cluster utilization.

External scientists and developers can use Gemini 4 models with /teamwork and /boost in Antigravity and Deep Review today. We have also opened applications giving 10,000 academic researchers six months of free access to our highest usage tier. Upcoming updates will bring our formal proof pattern and additional specialized workflows into Antigravity. These features will be accompanied by deep-dive tutorials on the Antigravity blog in the coming weeks, ensuring that developers and researchers have the resources needed to generate, inspect, and iteratively refine custom multi-agent teams. We are excited to make our tools (in addition to our models) available for everyone.
