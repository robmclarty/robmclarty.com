A new 30B parameter model leverages execution-grounded task synthesis and direct preference optimization to outperform larger baselines on tau-bench, BFCL v4, and MCP-Atlas. The work introduces a scalable dataset generation pipeline coupling verified trajectories with auditable states.

Abstract:Reliable agentic tool use requires training data that pair diverse multi-step workflows with executable evidence that the requested outcomes were achieved. Scaling either trajectory coverage or verification alone is insufficient as new environments introduce distinct states, rules, and dependencies that must also be reflected in the success criteria. To address this challenge, we introduce Davinci-Tool, a 30B tool-use model, and Davinci-ToolData, an execution-grounded dataset designed to scale environment coverage and outcome verification together. Davinci-ToolData preserves restorable states, execution traces, outcomes, and verifiers as a unified substrate for supervision. Two complementary pipelines populate this substrate: Execution-Grounded Task Synthesis (EGTS) derives verified demonstrations from independently authored systems, and Executable Environment Synthesis (EES) synthesizes auditable environments, state-grounded tasks, and executable verifiers across single and coordinated domains. These pipelines yield 30,395 verified trajectories spanning 2,260 invoked tool names. We first train on these demonstrations with supervised fine-tuning, then reuse EES states and verifiers to compare alternative actions and construct outcome-grounded preferences for Direct Preference Optimization. Davinci-Tool improves Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct from 52.4 to 88.2 on $\tau^2$-bench, from 30.4 to 62.8 on BFCL v4 multi-turn, and from 16.9 to 56.2 on MCP-Atlas, outperforming Qwen3-Coder-480B-A35B-Instruct on all three benchmarks with only 1/16 of its total parameters. Abstract:In this study, we address the problem of language-guided robotic manipulation, where a robot is required to manipulate a wide range of objects based on visual observations and natural language instructions. This task is essential for service robots that operate in human environments, and requires safety, efficiency, and task-level generality. Although Vision-Language-Action models (VLAs) have demonstrated strong performance for this task, their deployment in resource-constrained environments remains challenging because of the computational cost of standard transformer backbones. To overcome this limitation, we propose AnolisVLA, a lightweight VLA that uses a deep state space model to process multimodal sequences efficiently. The model uses lightweight, fast sequential state modeling to process visual and textual inputs. This design enables efficient trajectory generation. We evaluated the proposed method in simulation and on two real robot platforms. Compared with SmolVLA at a comparable parameter scale, AnolisVLA achieved higher task success rates, faster inference, and comparable GPU memory usage. In mobile manipulation, it outperformed the larger, 3-billion-parameter $\pi_{0.5}$ model by 21 percentage points in task success rate while achieving approximately three times faster inference.

CWE-bench shifts agentic security evaluation to defense
Why: Verify your repair pipelines against real-world defensive reliability constraints.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Cybersecurity evaluations of AI agents have focused largely on offense, yet a working exploit does not show that an agent can make software safe. An attacker needs to exploit only one vulnerability; a defender must find and fix all of them. We introduce CWE-bench, a private benchmark of 120 held-out repository audit-and-repair tasks spanning 73 CWE types, eight languages, and all OWASP Top 10 2025 categories. Agents are given a repository to audit and a description of its threat model, but are not told the weakness, where it occurs, or how many vulnerable sites there are. Each patch must block the exploit while preserving original functionality, as checked by a programmatic verifier. We validated the verifier against a panel of agentic judges, manually reviewing disagreements during development and refining the checks. The best agents reach 68% pass@1 and 81% pass@4. We further conduct analyses showing that (i) agents with the same pass@1 differ widely in retry gains and cost, and (ii) no single agent is best across languages or vulnerability categories, and difficulty varies far more by vulnerability category than by language. As the offensive capabilities of agents improve, CWE-bench provides a broad and rigorous measure of whether their defensive capabilities keep pace.

Double-Rollout RL cuts training steps for Qwen and Nemotron

Abstract:Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) implemented with Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) has driven substantial progress in training reasoning language models. However, verifiable outcome rewards provide an imperfect measure of reasoning quality: a correct final answer may not be supported by the reasoning trace, whereas an incorrect rollout may contain a nearly correct reasoning trajectory. We present empirical evidence for this mismatch and hypothesize that the resulting reward noise contributes to instability in reinforcement learning for both reasoning accuracy and token efficiency. To address this credit assignment challenge, this paper proposes double-rollout reinforcement learning. For each sampled rollout, we truncate its reasoning trace, sample multiple continuations from the truncated prefix, and replace the original reward with the mean continuation reward, yielding a more robust estimate of reasoning quality. This double-rollout algorithm incurs only a moderate increase in compute cost and yet reaches substantially higher accuracies across math and coding tasks and across Qwen and Nemotron models than baseline algorithms with fewer RL steps. For example, using only DAPO data it trains Nemotron-3-Nano-30B-A3B to reach 95.4% on AIME 2026, versus 91.5% by GRPO and substantially above state of the art for this model size, within 30 RL steps. When combined with length-based rewards, the same algorithm further improves the accuracy-efficiency Pareto frontier over existing efficiency optimizations. These findings suggest that the double-rollout reward estimation is an effective and cost-efficient way to improve both the accuracy and token efficiency of reasoning RL.

DishwasherBench V0.1 releases reproducible simulation for agentic spatial reasoning.
Why: You test agent tool use and packing efficiency in a verified physical environment.
Hacker News ↗HN 86↑extracted 06:50 · 4,371 words

DishwasherBench is a dishwasher-loading game on a physical simulation of one real machine, built to benchmark language models and playable by people in a browser. The game inside is called Spotless, and the packages are named @spotless/*.

A player loads a 3D model of a Bosch SMV4HAX40G with rigid-body physics. A wash on its Eco 50 programme is then simulated (jets, droplets, run-off, soil and detergent chemistry), and the player gets points for every item that comes out clean. On every level after the first, not everything fits, so the player has to choose what to load and how.

This is an early release, version 0.1.0. The simulation is calibrated on one test wash of the real machine. A score shows whether a player packed well and put cups, glasses and pots in mouth down. It does not yet show whether plates and cutlery were stacked so that they wash clean. The section What a score can be trusted for says so in full.

What is solid today: loading is deterministic rigid-body physics, every episode is a record that anyone can verify by replaying it and by washing its load again, and the same game is played by people in a browser and by models through MCP. What is rough is how the wash scores plates and cutlery.

Built and maintained by Composo. Many documents under docs/ are working records, kept as they were written; docs/README.md explains how to read them.

What works today

The machine. One Bosch SMV4HAX40G as data and geometry, from its owner's photographs and tape readings (packages/machine): both baskets with their tine rows, shelves and rests, the spray arms and the Eco 50 programme.

Loading. A catalogue of items and soils, deterministic rigid-body loading (Jolt), and a session layer with the actions and queries of the game (place, remove, set_rack, list_slots, check_load and the rest).

The wash. A simulation at two tiers. The official tier gives the scored result; the fast tier is a coarser preview. A cold official wash of the largest level takes 4.9 minutes on the reference machine, a MacBook Pro (Apple M3 Pro, 18 GB), best of three, and 5 to 8.5 minutes with another job running beside it, against a budget of 30.

A course of seven levels, numbered in order of difficulty (docs/benchmark/levels.md), each with saved reference loads of three scripted players (careful, careless, random). Six pass the benchmark's gates on the reference loads; the tutorial does not (its careless load loses too little).

Three ways to play. The page (apps/web), an MCP server for agents (packages/mcp), and a harness that runs scripted players and models and writes verified episode records (packages/bench).

Checks. 48 loading phenomena from the literature as executable pairs of washes (docs/research/standards.md section 9), and a calibration tool that scores the simulation against a record of a real wash, fits parameters and ranks candidate test washes (the stored ranking is of an earlier parameter set and has not been run again).

Two models have played, through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint: six episodes on three levels at the official tier (Results so far). No person has played for a baseline, and the path for Anthropic's API has not been run against the live API.

What a score can be trusted for

The full account, with every number and where it comes from, is docs/validation/README.md. In short, the simulation has been checked against four things:

One test wash on the real machine. Four egg-yolk plates on Eco 50. A prediction written before the result got the order of the plates and the form of the residue right, and was ten to thirty times too optimistic on the amount left on the two oven-dried plates. The calibration that followed moved one number: the share of the long soak during which the pump runs, from 0.5 to 0.8 (anywhere from 0.59 to 1). The record that the prediction came first is in the private development history, which is not published, so that order cannot be checked from this repository.

Published class means of a sister machine (Bosch SMV6ECX51E, eco programme, reference detergent): cutlery within 0.1 of a score point, crockery 0.2 low, glass 0.1 to 0.5 low. This is a fit, not a prediction: the soil and chemistry defaults were set so that these numbers come out.

48 loading phenomena with evidence in the literature: 17 pass, 21 fail in size or sign, 1 is a faulty scenario and 9 need mechanisms that are not built.

Its own book-keeping. Water is conserved exactly, and the same input gives the same result on one machine. The fast tier agrees with the official tier on 89 % of the verdicts of a standard 13-place load and on 97 % on the levels.

A score today shows whether a player packed well and put cups, glasses and pots in mouth down. It does not yet show whether plates and cutlery were stacked so that they wash clean.

About three quarters of the gap between a careful and a careless reference load is hollow pieces put in the wrong way up. The soils in those pieces (smoothie, tomato soup, ketchup, guacamole) rest on values from the literature that no test wash has checked.

Not modelled at all: drying, spots and film on glass, items moved by the jets, and soil settling on clean pieces. Only Eco 50 is calibrated and scored.

Requirements

DishwasherBench has been run on one machine: a MacBook Pro (Apple M3 Pro, 18 GB) under macOS 27. Every figure in this repository was measured on that machine. Linux and Windows are untested. Nothing is known to rule them out, and a report of what happens there is welcome (docs/ROADMAP.md).

Node 20.19 or newer in the 20 line (.nvmrc pins 20.19.0). An older Node is refused at install. Node 22 has not been tried.

pnpm 9 (package.json names 9.4.0; corepack enable picks it up).

A GPU that Dawn can open. The wash traces water on the GPU through WebGPU from Node (the webgpu package, version 0.6.2, which carries prebuilt Dawn binaries). On the reference machine that is Metal. This version did not load on macOS 14.7 on the same hardware.

Google Chrome or Chromium, for the pictures an agent is sent and for the scripts that drive the page. The harness looks for it in the usual install locations; set SPOTLESS_CHROME to the path of the program to choose another. Playing by hand needs only a desktop browser, and scripted players need none (--no-render).

About 680 MB for the clone (a download of about 290 MB; the files themselves are about 385 MB, mostly photographs, screenshots and episode records) and about 420 MB for node_modules.

1 to 2 GB of memory for an official wash of a level (2.4 GB was seen on the standard 13-place load), and free ports 8787 (game server) and 8788 (MCP server).

Results are repeatable bit for bit on one machine. Across machines, operating-system builds and versions of the webgpu package they may differ in the last bits, which is why every result names the stack it was computed on (docs/research/spike-gpu-compute-macos27-recheck.md).

Quick start

git clone https://github.com/composo-ai/DishwasherBench.git cd DishwasherBench corepack enable # once: makes pnpm 9.4.0 available pnpm install --frozen-lockfile # about 15 seconds the first time

There is no build step for the packages: they are consumed as TypeScript source and run with tsx. Only the page is built, by the play command below.

The shortest thing that shows a result is to wash a saved load from the command line:

pnpm --filter @spotless/wash-runner wash-load tutorial-careful --tier fast # under a minute pnpm --filter @spotless/wash-runner wash-load tutorial-careful --tier official # about 5 minutes the first time

It prints the points and, for each item, the verdict, the score and what is left on it. The saved loads are in packages/loader/loads/ (<level>-<player>.json). The first wash of a load works out where the water goes and keeps that in .spotless/exposure-cache, so a second wash of the same load takes seconds (pass --no-cache to switch this off).

To check that everything runs on your machine:

pnpm typecheck # the root and every package; a few seconds pnpm test # every package; 8 to 17 minutes on the reference machine

How long pnpm test takes depends on what else the machine is doing. One test is skipped by design. The tests run real washes on the GPU.

A wash refuses to run when the parameter set is stale. The set the game washes with (anchored-v7.owner-capsule, in packages/calibration/anchors/) records the state of the simulation source and of the machine data it was made for. After a change to either, the wash stops and lists what differs. CONTRIBUTING.md explains the rule and how to make the set current again.

Play in the browser

pnpm --filter @spotless/web play

This builds the page and starts the game server. Open http://127.0.0.1:8787 (add --port <n> for another port). The page plays music: mute the tab, or use the Sound button, if you do not want it.

If the port is taken the server stops with serve: port 8787 is in use; give --port <n>. The server answers only the page it serves itself: a request that carries the Origin of another site gets 403 unless the server was started with --allow-origin <scheme://host[:port]> (may be repeated), and a request whose Host header names another host than the addresses the server listens on (127.0.0.1, localhost and [::1] by default) gets 403 as well (--allow-host <name> adds a name). Requests without an Origin header (the harness, the MCP server, curl) are not affected, and the client under the Vite development server needs no flag.

When you start the wash you are offered two kinds:

Quick preview: the fast tier. About a minute; coarser, and a few verdicts may differ from the full wash. It is not the scored result.

Full wash: the official tier. Several minutes; this is the scored result. After a preview, the report offers the full wash on the same load.

The game is scored on Eco 50 with one capsule of detergent (Finish Powerball Ultimate, 12.9 g). The levels are listed 1 to 7 with a difficulty figure, which is worked out from what is on the counter and is not a measured score.

Placing by pointing works for plates in a free row, bowls and cutlery. Two things do not work by pointing yet: a plate pointed at the gap next to a plate already placed often perches on its neighbour, and cups and glasses cannot be leant on the side rests of the upper basket. For both, pick the piece up and press "Suggest spots" (it costs one query), then click a numbered mark.

After a quick preview of a careless load of the first level: a bowl left mouth up holds 432 ml of water and is not clean, and the report says that a fork held the lower spray arm still. The banner says what a preview is: not the scored result. All of this is the simulation's output for a deliberately bad load, not a test of the real machine or detergent.

Looking closely at one item after the wash: the residue is painted where it was left, with its area and the water the item held.

Run a scripted player

A scripted player needs no model and costs nothing. This plays the first level with the careful reference player at the official tier, then verifies the record by replaying its actions and by washing the load a second time (about five minutes each):

pnpm --filter @spotless/bench bench run --levels tutorial --policies careful \ --no-render --out "$PWD/.spotless/bench/scripted" pnpm --filter @spotless/bench bench verify \ "$PWD"/.spotless/bench/scripted/tutorial__careful__*/record.json --rewash

Give --out as an absolute path; a relative one resolves inside packages/bench.

The scripted players are empty, random, careless and careful. Add --tier fast for a preview in about a minute; a fast wash is labelled as a preview in the record and in the results table, and is never a score.

Each run writes under --out: record.json, a results table (results.md, results.json), the data of the game server it started (server-data/) and, without --no-render, the pictures the tools returned.

The scripted careful player's load of the largest level (62 pieces on the counter, 49 loaded), from above and from the front and side. Both models that have played beat it.

Run a model

Everything about the harness, what each kind of player sees and what is recorded is in docs/benchmark/README.md.

A model run costs money. Copy .env.example to .env (which git ignores) and fill in the address and the key of your endpoint. The command prints the names of the variables it set, never a value, and neither the key nor the address is written to a record.

A model behind an OpenAI-compatible endpoint (OpenAI, Azure, or any server with the Responses or chat completions API):

pnpm --filter @spotless/bench bench run --levels tutorial --policies openai:gpt-6.1-sol \ --max-input-tokens 2000000 --out "$PWD/.spotless/bench/sol-tutorial"

The policy names the model or, on Azure, the deployment: openai:<model or deployment>.

With no address set, no request is made: the episode ends at once with no result, and its record says no address of the API (set SPOTLESS_OPENAI_BASE_URL, or give --base-url). The command then exits with status 1, and pnpm reports the failed script.

--max-input-tokens ends an episode before its requests add up to more. As measured with two models: the first level takes 0.4 to 0.65 million input tokens, a level of 37 pieces 2.5 to 2.8 million, and the largest level (62 pieces) 3.0 to 3.2 million, with 95 % or more of it read from the provider's cache. Use limits of 2,000,000, 12,000,000 and 20,000,000.

The harness records tokens. It records a cost only when prices are given in .env.

Without --tier fast the wash is at the official tier, which is the score.

Each run writes the record, the transcript, the pictures and a results table under --out, and verifies the episode by replaying its action log.

A model through Anthropic's API, with the key in ANTHROPIC_API_KEY (put the model's id in place of <model id>):

pnpm --filter @spotless/bench bench run --levels tutorial --policies anthropic:<model id> \ --max-input-tokens 2000000 --out "$PWD/.spotless/bench/anthropic-tutorial"

This path has not been run against the live API. It has unit tests against a local stand-in and nothing else behind it. A report of a first run is welcome. With no key the episode fails at once (Could not resolve authentication method). The SDK also accepts a stored login profile if you have one, so the absence of a key in .env does not guarantee that nothing is spent.

Connect an MCP client

Any agent that speaks MCP can play. An MCP server is bound to one episode, so the game server must be running in benchmark mode and the episode must exist first.

# 1. The game server (the token: 8 characters or more, of your choosing) pnpm --filter @spotless/server exec tsx src/cli.ts serve --admin-token <token> --wash-tier fast \ --data "$PWD/.spotless" # 2. An episode; prints an address for the page, and the episode's id pnpm --filter @spotless/server exec tsx src/cli.ts link --admin-token <token> \ --level tutorial --player my-agent --agent # 3. For a client that speaks streamable HTTP: serves http://127.0.0.1:8788/mcp pnpm --filter @spotless/mcp exec tsx src/cli.ts --server http://127.0.0.1:8787 \ --session <episode id> --http --port 8788

--data keeps the server's files, the episode records among them (.spotless/episodes/), in the directory the page uses; without it serve writes to packages/server/.spotless. The address that link prints shows the episode in the page, and works only once the page has been built (pnpm --filter @spotless/web build, which play also does); the MCP calls work either way. If the game server is on another port (serve --port <n>), give link its address with --server http://127.0.0.1:<n>. For a desktop client that starts the MCP server itself over stdio, the configuration is in docs/benchmark/README.md section 3.

Over HTTP the MCP server is for MCP clients on this machine: it answers 403 to any request that carries an Origin header and to a request whose Host header does not name an address it listens on (127.0.0.1, localhost or [::1] by default). For a client that reaches it under another name, for example from a container, start it with --allow-host <name>; the game server takes the same flag.

The agent gets thirteen tools (place, remove, reset, set_rack, start_cycle, get_level, get_item, get_state, render, preview, list_slots, check_load, get_report). To try one call by hand:

pnpm --filter @spotless/mcp exec tsx scripts/call.ts --url http://127.0.0.1:8788/mcp get_state '{}'

Both servers listen on 127.0.0.1 and are meant for use on your own machine (SECURITY.md).

How it works

Loading. Every item and every part of the machine is a rigid compound of exact shapes generated from a parametric description; the picture, the collision shape and the surfaces the wash sees all come from that one description. Items are placed with rigid-body physics (Jolt) at a fixed step, with the player's inputs rounded to 1 mm and 1 degree, so that a person and a model act on the same grid and the same load always settles the same way. Every action is logged with a hash of the scene after it, and replaying the log must reproduce every hash. See docs/design/platform.md.

The wash. Jets are traced on the GPU from the nozzle positions measured on the machine and the turning of the arms, onto the exact shapes of the load. Splash and drips travel as droplets; water that lands runs down surfaces as a film, pools where a surface holds it and drains where it does not. A soil model per kind of soil then removes soil with time, temperature and the chemistry of the wash water (detergent, enzymes, bleach), over the whole programme. Where the water goes is cached apart from the soil and the chemistry, so a wash with other soil parameters replays in seconds to about a minute. The official tier works on blocks of 2 mm, the fast tier on 4 mm. See docs/design/scope.md for what is built and what is left out, and docs/design/sim-core.md for the physics as designed.

The scoring. After the wash an inspector looks at every item. An item is clean when it carries at most 4 specks and 4 mm² of visible soil, counting what dirty water left where it stood on the item. Each item is worth a number of points stated on the level card; a clean item earns them, an item that is not clean or was left on the counter earns nothing. An episode is one load and one wash. The report also gives a graded score from 0 to 5 for each item and what is left where, which do not change the points. See docs/benchmark/README.md and docs/benchmark/levels.md.

The calibration. Every physical constant of the wash is in a parameter registry with a default, a range and a source. A wash record describes a real test wash: the machine and the programme, each item with its soil and where it stood, and what was seen afterwards. The calibration tool runs the simulation on a record, scores it against what was seen, fits chosen parameters and says which parameters the data can and cannot identify. One such wash exists so far. See docs/calibration/wash-record.md, docs/calibration/fitting.md and, for the set the game washes with, docs/calibration/anchoring-v7.md.

Limitations and roadmap

The open problems, each written as something a contributor could pick up, are tracked as issues; docs/ROADMAP.md is the map of them. The largest:

Stacking makes no difference for plates and cutlery. Plates fallen over, crowded cutlery and a stalled arm cost little or nothing.

Faces lying within a millimetre or two of another body come out too clean. A mechanism for such gaps was built and is not in this repository, because it broke the cutlery control and its deciding parameter has no measurement behind it.

Fat comes clean with no detergent, and starch always comes clean.

One machine, one programme, one test wash. The soils the levels rest on have never been tested in a real wash.

No human baseline, no repeated episodes, and no run through Anthropic's API.

One platform. Linux and Windows are untested.

LLMs require human judgment comparable to compilers
Why: You need skill to direct AI output effectively.
Hacker News ↗HN 733↑extracted 05:00 · 598 words

In his October 11, 2026 article, "'AI' is Not a Tool," Rich Hickey argues that LLMs are automation rather than tools because they cannot be wielded. I agree that outsourcing work can cost us opportunities to learn. But his conclusion does not follow. Even by his definition, an LLM can be something a human wields. And using it well takes skill, just as using other tools does.

Picking up a saw does not guarantee a straight cut. Holding a chisel does not make you a sculptor. Tools can amplify poor judgment as readily as good judgment. Their usefulness depends on the operator's ability to direct them, interpret feedback, recognize mistakes, and make corrections. Those requirements also apply to LLMs.

One of my colleagues used to say, "AI is a mirror." I find that insightful: it reflects who you are, including the curiosity, judgment, and habits you bring to it. The questions you ask and the standards you apply to its answers matter.

An LLM's unpredictability is a meaningful limitation. But predictability, reliability, simplicity, and bounded function are qualities by which we evaluate tools, not prerequisites for membership in the category. A tool does not stop being a tool because it is difficult to control or easy to misuse.

Hickey's distinction between tools and automation also creates a false division. Automation can be a feature of a tool. A compiler performs substantial work without the programmer continuously directing each operation. A spreadsheet recalculates results without someone manually carrying out every calculation. We still wield these systems by specifying inputs, examining results, and deciding what to do next. Why should generating text automatically disqualify an instrument from being a tool when translating code or calculating numbers automatically does not?

The claim that LLM use lacks intention, feedback, direction, and expertise is especially difficult to defend. A programmer can ask an LLM to explain unfamiliar code, challenge that explanation, inspect the source, test a proposed change, reject it, and refine the request. That is a feedback loop directed by a human pursuing a purpose. Doing it well requires knowing what to ask, what evidence matters, and when the answer is wrong. Expertise can make the instrument more useful.

Hickey's concern about reading is valid for a particular use: accepting a summary in place of reading a paper can sacrifice much of what reading teaches. But someone can also read the paper and use an LLM to question their interpretation, explore an objection, or identify something to examine more closely. The possibility of avoiding intellectual work does not establish that every use avoids it. The relevant question is what the person actually does.

What disappoints me the most is the reliance on the AI industry's anthropomorphic vocabulary: "go rogue," "escape containment," "conspire." Repeating those expressions risks accepting the framing that makes these systems sound like independent beings. Software can produce unexpected outputs, fail to follow instructions, or cause harm when given access to consequential actions. Those are real engineering problems. Calling them "conspiracy" adds a suggestion of human motives without establishing any.

I would not claim that an LLM always does exactly what a human tells it to do. It plainly can fail. But failure to obey perfectly does not make it cease to be a tool, any more than successful use proves that its operator has surrendered their judgment.

The useful distinction is between wielding an instrument with skill and delegating work without adequate understanding or oversight. That distinction applies to AI, and it gives us something more practical than declaring that an entire class of software cannot be a tool.
