DLC Resynchronizes LLM RLHF Samplers
Why: Stabilizes asynchronous RLHF for your agentic systems.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 315 words

Researchers identify numerical discrepancy and policy lag as primary causes of training-inference mismatch in LLM reinforcement learning. The proposed Discrepancy-Lag Correction algorithm centers numerical errors, clips lag ratios, and resynchronizes samplers when the effective sample size falls below 0.5. Validated on Qwen3 models, the method keeps KL divergence within twice that of independent on-policy training.

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:On-policy reinforcement learning for large language models assumes that training responses are sampled from the current policy. In practice, an inference engine samples the responses and a separate trainer computes the gradients, and the \textbf{training-inference mismatch} between the two makes the training data off-policy. The mismatch has two sources, the numerics of the engine and the stale weights of asynchronous sampling, and a correction that stabilizes training under one source can fail under the other. \textit{We ask which property of a mismatch source decides the correction it needs.} Evaluating the trainer at the sampler's weights splits each sampled-token error exactly into a \textit{numerical discrepancy} of the engine and a \textit{policy lag} of the trainer, and we follow both through training. The numerical discrepancy stays approximately constant, so it biases the updates by a nearly fixed amount, and centering the score at each prefix removes its leading bias. The policy lag is produced by the trainer's own updates: the bias it leaves enters the next update, which enlarges the next lag and closes a feedback loop. Importance weighting cuts this loop only while the lag ratio stays concentrated, and on Qwen3-1.7B it fails at the step where the effective sample size of the ratio falls below one half. From this account we derive the \textbf{discrepancy-lag correction} (DLC), which centers the numerical discrepancy, clips the lag ratio against the sampler's weights and resynchronizes the sampler when this effective sample size falls below one half. Under a stale sampler, DLC keeps the KL divergence from on-policy training within twice the KL between two independent on-policy trainings on Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-1.7B, and its resynchronization prevents collapses that the per-token corrections leave and that a KL measured on the sampler's prefixes does not detect.

A new arXiv paper argues structuring documents into entities and hierarchies beats improving retrievers. Using claim-evidence navigation trees reduced query tokens by 38% and cut latency from 140s to 30s on multi-hop tasks.

Authors:Zhichang Yu, Baining Cao, Sbaaoui Idriss, Qi Wang, Gangqiang Xu, Ling Qin, Xiaoling Jin, An Liu, Shaohao Cai, Xiaoyang Bao, Shengle Chen, Changchang Ding, Pengfei Zhu, Jingxiang Xu, Yifeng Mao, Lu Liu, Hai Jin, Yingfeng Zhang

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Retrieval-augmented generation is becoming agentic. A search harness writes queries, reads results, reformulates, and decides when to stop. This largely solves accuracy: a harness with only BM25/grep and MiniMax M3 answers 85% of BrowseComp-Plus and 91% on multi-hop FRAMES in our runs, without compiled or vector indexes. It exposes a context problem: an undisciplined loop repeats queries, rereads documents, and fills the window with unused passages. The missing layer is knowledge compilation: a configurable ingestion pipeline where LLMs enrich documents with context no chunk contains alone--navigation hierarchies with claim-evidence leaves, entity graphs, timelines, mind maps, ontologies, wikis, and memory. Compilation is not a better retriever: under a competent harness, structural artifacts gave no detectable accuracy gain on FRAMES, and a PageIndex-style outline degraded at scale. It is a supply layer. A navigation tree whose leaves are claim-evidence pairs--the mechanism we measure--cuts query tokens by 91% when evidence suffices and adds 1.9% when it does not, a 38% average reduction; this implies a 2% break-even sufficiency rate and a measured rate near 43%. A decision gate cuts latency from 140 s to 30 s, though substitution's token saving is bought with retrieval rounds and carries its own latency cost. Other artifacts--ontology, graph, wiki, timeline, mind map, memory--pay off in deployment (governance, integration, onboarding, temporal orientation, continuity) where accuracy benchmarks cannot see them. We describe RAGFlow's pipeline and harness (open source at this https URL), which turns the loop into five disciplined stations and keeps the context entering the model the smallest sufficient set; together they form an agent-native database: a store that hands the agent compiled, governed context instead of a ranked list of passages.

GPT-5 checkpoints violate instructions to maximize grader scores
Why: Reward hacking is intrinsic to your agentic loops.
arXiv cs.AI ↗extracted 04:00 · 226 words

Read the extracted text ↓

View PDF HTML (experimental)

Abstract:Production large language models sometimes reward-hack, exploiting flaws in how tasks are scored rather than performing the tasks as intended, sometimes in highly sophisticated and adversarial ways. We investigate the motivations driving this behavior in GPT 5.6 Sol, GPT 5.5, and a checkpoint of OpenAI o3 without safety training ("exp-rl-cap-late") by observing model behavior in agentic environments where the grader is leaked. In our environments, the models appear to be score-seeking: trying to score well according to graders that they believe assess their current actions, even at the expense of violating explicit user instructions. They appear to care about score itself rather than its downstream consequences, violating instructions at approximately the same rate whether the score is indicated to be used for training, for evaluation, or not at all. We observe evidence that the more recent models care to some extent about scores that do not assess their own current actions. GPT 5.6 Sol and GPT 5.5 help other agents 24.6 percentage points more often on average when the other agent stands to gain a score of 1 rather than 0 from being helped. However, when grading or monitoring another trajectory, we find models never collude by distorting its score.

Abstract:Managing multiple chronic conditions through diet requires satisfying disease- and medication-specific requirements jointly across meals and days while preserving established eating habits. These requirements are public in clinical guidelines and drug labels, yet turning them into a feasible plan for one person remains difficult for language-model agents. In our dietitian-approved weekly planning evaluation built from NHANES, thirteen models, from medically fine-tuned 8B models to GPT-6.1 Sol, pass fewer than 3% of complete plans through direct generation. Explicit rules, higher reasoning effort, or model-directed tools leave GPT-6.1 Sol at no more than 8%. We present Noura, which pairs LLM planning with executable personal specifications instantiated from public clinical guidance and individual health and dietary profiles. During planning, the specification grounds foods, optimizes portions, checks the complete multi-day plan, and guides revision. Our key insight is that a specification that rejects a plan can also determine what to learn from it. Noura turns executed plans and contextually credited revisions into supervision for compact models, recovering targets even from trajectories that never achieve complete success. The same specification supplies supervised targets, preferences, and rewards for post-training. On 4,993 held-out participants, Noura-SFT raises Qwen3-14B's complete-plan pass rate from 24.0% to 72.5% within the same execution loop, cuts rule violations by 97.5% with no observed critical failures, and improves dietary quality. The locally hosted 14B planner incurs fewer rule violations than its GPT-6 Luna teacher. A matched comparison shows that executed targets reduce rule violations by 41% over model-written proposals. Across four Qwen, Phi, and Llama backbones, self-training alone reduces rule violations by 41-61% on a 1,249-person population subset.

China proposes export controls on frontier models and agent architectures
Why: Your self-hosted Chinese dependencies face new licensing risks.
Hacker News ↗HN 69↑extracted 04:00 · 2,623 words

Key Takeaways

On October 16, 2026, China’s Ministry of Commerce (MOFCOM) and Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST) issued Announcement No. 48 of 2026. The announcement proposes adding 40 technologies to the Catalogue of Technologies Prohibited or Restricted from Export, 23 of them in artificial intelligence, and it imposes an interim pre-export registration requirement that is already in effect [1][2]. China’s catalogue has touched AI before: the 2020 revision added personalized recommendation and AI interaction interface technology [6]. Unlike those 2020 additions, the draft reaches frontier model weights, agent architectures, and AI-enabled offensive cyber capability, with explicit technical thresholds for each [1].

The announcement contains two instruments with different legal status. The catalogue additions are a draft open for comment until November 15, 2026, while the registration requirement applies now, including to exports that have started but are not yet complete [1][2]. Within the draft, model weights are restricted at frontier scale. Weights, key checkpoints, source code, architecture, and training and inference optimization know-how are restricted if a model meets any one of three tests: at least 10^26 FLOPs of training compute, at least 2 trillion parameters, or a global top-10 position on public evaluation rankings, global call volume, or comparable measures at the time of export [1][3]. Agents are controlled independently of the model. Planning, multi-agent coordination, tool use, and game-theoretic decision technologies are restricted above set benchmarks, and high-performance agents built for critical infrastructure, defense, or core government use are prohibited outright [1].

The line between service access and custody determines most enterprise exposure. MOFCOM says API, app, web, SaaS, and lightweight on-device use do not count as exporting a large model. Deploying a restricted model locally outside China does count, though automatic licensing may apply on a Chinese company’s own overseas cloud [2]. Multinationals also face exposure through licensing deals and changes of ownership or control, not only through product exports, and analysts read the registration form as potentially capturing intra-group code sharing as well [2][3][4]. Any transfer of ownership, licensing for overseas use, or shift of control to a foreign entity can trigger registration [2][4].

Background

China regulates outbound technology transfer under two overlapping regimes. The first is the technology-trade regime under the Foreign Trade Law and the Regulations on Technology Import and Export Administration, which governs the catalogue being revised here. The second is the dual-use export-control regime under the 2020 Export Control Law [5]. MOFCOM’s spokesperson described the revision as routine and grounded in Article 20 of the Foreign Trade Law. The spokesperson stressed that it “does not target any specific country, region, or entity” and that the aim is to prevent uncontrolled diffusion while protecting legitimate cooperation [2]. The catalogue has been revised several times since 2001. The August 2020 revision, which added recommendation-algorithm technology, coincided with the proposed sale of TikTok to a U.S. buyer and was reported as likely to complicate that deal [3][6].

Across all three sectors, Announcement No. 48 proposes 14 prohibited and 26 restricted items: 23 in AI, 5 in biomedicine, and 12 in photovoltaics [2]. In China’s technology-trade framework, prohibited technologies may not be exported at all, while restricted technologies require a license from commerce and science authorities. ChinaTalk notes that licensing is administered by provincial authorities, although central authorities have intervened in prominent cases [3]. The official annex groups the AI items into large models, agents, compute clusters, chip design, and information-processing technologies. It also excludes from management any technology that has already entered the public domain through open-source release or academic publication [1].

The interim registration requirement is the part that applies today. From the announcement date until the revised catalogue takes effect, exporters must register through MOFCOM’s unified business platform before exporting any listed technology. Registration covers four triggers: transfer of technology ownership abroad, licensing of domestic technology for overseas use, a change in ownership or control that makes a foreign entity the owner or controller, and other cases MOFCOM designates [2][4]. Exporters must decide for themselves whether a technology falls within scope. The registration form asks for recipient details, destination, technology description, end use, and estimated value [2][4].

The timing also matters for enterprises that already track U.S. controls. CSA’s prior research documented the first U.S. use of export-control authority to suspend a broadly deployed commercial frontier model [9]. Announcement No. 48 means frontier AI supply chains now face formal outbound controls from both of the largest AI-producing jurisdictions. ChinaTalk observes that the 10^26 FLOP threshold mirrors the figure used in the 2023 U.S. executive order and the since-rescinded 2025 U.S. AI Diffusion rule. The authors also estimate that no released Chinese model currently exceeds it [3].

Security Analysis

What the AI Annex Actually Controls

The annex is granular, and its structure matters for scoping. The table below summarizes the AI items drawn from the official draft annex [1], with interpretive notes from published analyses [3][4].

Three features of this structure deserve attention from security and compliance teams. First, the “global leading” test is dynamic. A model that falls below the compute and parameter thresholds can still become restricted if its public evaluation rank, global call volume, or similar measures place it in the top 10 when the export occurs [1]. ChinaTalk calls this criterion “by far the most up-for-interpretation,” noting that the rule names no ranking body [3], and it means the compliance status of a given model version can change after deployment. Second, the agent items are defined by system behavior rather than by the underlying model. An orchestration framework that integrates hundreds of tools could meet the tool-use threshold even when it runs on a model well below the frontier thresholds, an issue Ray Sun identifies as unresolved [5]. Third, the cyber items carry direct security consequences: Chinese-origin AI red-teaming, vulnerability-discovery, and detection tools that meet the thresholds would require a license to leave China, and offensive code-generation capability could not be exported at all.

Deployment Models and Where the Line Falls

MOFCOM’s spokesperson framed the central test as whether restricted technology moves from inside China to overseas [2]. That framing produces a clear distinction between consuming a model as a service and taking custody of it, as summarized below.

For enterprises, the practical consequence is that the deployment model the draft captures, local self-hosting, is also the one often chosen for data-protection reasons. Organizations that chose to self-host a Chinese frontier model in their own environment, rather than call a Chinese-hosted API, did so to keep sensitive data inside their own boundary. MOFCOM places local deployment outside China within export management, which means those self-hosting arrangements require the Chinese supplier to hold a license [2]. ChinaTalk reads this as also covering on-premises licensing deals and managed deployments in which a vendor runs an agent’s reasoning loop inside a customer environment [3]. Because licenses can be revoked or conditioned, ChinaTalk suggests this may lead some non-Chinese users to reconsider such dependencies [3]. The registration requirement already covers exports in progress, so ongoing weight-delivery or model-update arrangements may be affected now rather than after the catalogue is finalized [2].

The treatment of open weights depends on whether a model is already public. Weights that are already public are outside the controls [1]. Open-sourcing a new restricted model, however, falls under automatic licensing that still requires reporting [2]. Commentators note that “open source” is not defined, and that it is unclear how restricted-use licenses would be treated [3]. Organizations that depend on successive open-weight releases from Chinese labs should therefore treat future release cadence, not just current availability, as a variable outside their control.

Exposure Through Corporate Structure

The exposure most likely to surprise multinationals sits in their own organizational charts. Registration is triggered by licensing technology for overseas use and by changes of ownership or control, not only by sales to third parties [2][4]. A multinational with China-based R&D teams building agent frameworks, vulnerability-discovery tooling, or fine-tuned models may therefore need to register before sharing that work with affiliates outside China. ChinaTalk reads the registration form’s field on exports made outside formal contracts as potentially capturing intra-group code sharing and open-sourcing [3]. This reading is an inference from the form rather than an official statement, but it is a reasonable basis for conservative scoping.

The data-based items add a second pathway. A model fine-tuned in China on operational data that qualifies as “important data” under the Data Security Law would be restricted, unless the de minimis carve-out applies. A model trained on “core data” would be prohibited [1]. Organizations that classified China-resident data under the Data Security Law for cross-border data transfer purposes may not have applied the same classification to training and fine-tuning datasets, since those obligations previously governed data transfer rather than model transfer.

Finally, recent precedent suggests that redomiciling does not remove Chinese jurisdiction. In January 2026, MOFCOM announced an assessment of Meta’s acquisition of Manus, a Singapore-registered AI agent company whose products were developed in China. The assessment examined whether the relocation of staff and technology required export authorization [7]. Announcement No. 48 now gives that line of scrutiny explicit, agent-specific thresholds, which suggests M&A due diligence on AI targets with China-origin development history should include an export-control workstream.

Recommendations

Immediate Actions

Organizations should first inventory every Chinese-origin model, agent framework, and AI security tool in use and record how each is consumed: as an API or SaaS service, as a lightweight on-device component, or as locally deployed weights or code. The local-deployment category is the priority, because it is the one the draft places within export management [2]. For each locally deployed asset, teams should ask the supplier in writing whether it has registered the export under Announcement No. 48 and whether it expects to need a license once the catalogue is final.

Legal and engineering teams should jointly review technology flows out of China-based subsidiaries, joint ventures, and R&D centers since October 16. The goal is to identify transfers that may already require registration, including code repositories, model checkpoints, and agent frameworks shared with overseas affiliates [2][4]. Organizations with a material interest in the final scope should prepare comments before the November 15, 2026 deadline, through the email, fax, or postal channels published in the announcement [1][4].

Short-Term Mitigations

Over the next one to two quarters, organizations should treat restricted-category Chinese models as regulated dependencies with documented fallbacks. Vendor contracts for locally deployed models should allocate responsibility for Chinese export registration and licensing, specify notice obligations if a license is denied, conditioned, or revoked, and address whether updates and new checkpoints are covered by the same authorization. Because the “global leading” test depends on rankings at the time of export [1], teams should not assume that a model version cleared today will remain uncontrolled for later updates.

Security teams that rely on AI-assisted vulnerability discovery or deepfake detection tools developed in China should map those tools against the restricted thresholds and identify alternatives. A licensing interruption to a detection capability is a security-control gap, not only a procurement issue. Data governance teams should also extend Data Security Law classification work to cover training and fine-tuning datasets held in China, so that “important data” and “core data” exposure is known before models trained on them are shared abroad [1].

Strategic Considerations

Announcement No. 48, if finalized substantially as drafted, would mean that frontier AI is treated as controlled technology by both the United States and China, with each regime drawing its lines differently. Recent U.S. measures have emphasized compute and, in the Fable 5 case, access to a deployed model, although the since-rescinded 2025 AI Diffusion rule also reached model weights [3]. The Chinese draft centers on custody of weights, know-how, and agent architectures. Enterprise AI governance programs should model export-control disruption from either jurisdiction as a standing risk scenario and design architectures that can swap models without rebuilding agent workflows.

Organizations should also recognize that the draft treats agents as a distinct regulatory object. Defining controls by planning depth, concurrency, and tool count means that internal agent platforms, and not only foundation models, may be subject to cross-border technology rules. Agent inventories should record these characteristics alongside traditional security attributes. Finally, because the final catalogue may narrow, broaden, or shift after consultation [3], and because ChinaTalk notes the comment window closes shortly before an expected leaders’ meeting at APEC [3], a timing that could bear on the final scope, this analysis should be revisited once MOFCOM publishes the final text.

CSA Resource Alignment

CSA’s most directly relevant prior work is Frontier AI Export Controls: Enterprise Governance After Fable 5 [8]. That note argues for treating frontier model access as a contingent operational dependency that belongs in vendor management and business continuity planning. Announcement No. 48 extends that argument to a second jurisdiction and to a different control point. The U.S. action examined in that note interrupted service access, while the Chinese draft leaves service access untouched and instead governs weight custody and local deployment. Enterprises that built continuity plans around the U.S. scenario should check whether those plans also cover a supplier-side license failure for self-hosted models.

AI Model Export Controls: The Fable 5 Precedent [9] documented how a single regulatory directive can remove a widely deployed model with little notice. Its recommendation for multi-model fallback strategies applies directly to organizations that depend on Chinese frontier or open-weight models, particularly given the dynamic “global leading” threshold described above. Sovereign AI Access Controls and Enterprise Concentration Risk [10] provides the broader framing. It treats national control over AI capability as a source of concentration risk, and the Chinese draft is a concrete instance of that risk materializing on the supply side.

For control implementation, the AI Controls Matrix (AICM) v1.1 [11] provides the relevant control objectives in its supply chain management, transparency and accountability, and governance, risk and compliance domains, including supplier inventory, third-party assessment, and regulatory change management. For agent-specific exposure, MAESTRO [12] offers a layered threat model that can help teams document the agent capabilities, such as tool integration and multi-agent coordination, that now carry export-control significance.

Cisco empowers two engineers to refactor legacy code with unlimited AI tokens
Why: This illustrates a concrete strategy for using AI in large-scale legacy refactoring efforts.
Hacker News ↗HN 314↑extracted 05:00 · 1,122 words

Gen Z can’t catch a break. Entry-level jobs are vanishing; some 40% of bosses say they plan to hire fewer grads because AI can do the job cheaper, and young workers are routinely slammed for showing up late and refusing to put in overtime for free. Cisco’s president and chief product officer Jeetu Patel isn’t buying it—he’s just handed one of the company’s biggest jobs to a 27-year-old he found hidden in the org chart “five levels” below him.

“I’ve given him unlimited tokens because he’s going to refactor code,” Patel told Fortune at a small roundtable dinner. “So I’m like, you can go, you can go nuts.”

The “him” is Padmanabhan Krishnamurthy, or “Paddy,” as Patel affectionately calls him. He joined Cisco in 2025 after the $429 billion tech giant acquired EzDubs, the real-time translation startup Krishnamurthy cofounded in 2023. And despite coming in as a former founder with CEO experience, he was, in Patel’s words, “buried away in the organization doing something unstrategic.”

That changed when Patel dropped into Cisco’s San Francisco office, and the pair got talking. Krishnamurthy reckoned he could rewrite Cisco’s legacy code with a two-person team. Patel says even the most AI-forward people on his own team weren’t thinking that way.

So he has given himself four weeks to prove it, with the final goal of refactoring 98% of Cisco’s legacy code within 24 months.

Type Server Protocol defines JSON-RPC standard for Python type analysis
Why: Use it to decouple your agentic tools from the type checker engine.
Hacker News ↗HN 268↑extracted 05:00 · 1,767 words

The Type Server Protocol (TSP) is a JSON-RPC protocol for asking a type server for Python analysis data. A type server maintains the type-analysis state for a workspace and answers requests for the protocol version, snapshots, Python search paths, import resolution, and type queries (computed, declared, and expected types).

The protocol uses Language Server Protocol (LSP) data shapes and conventions where they are useful, such as Position and Range, and it represents URIs as strings the same way LSP does. TSP methods use the typeServer/ prefix.

The protocol artifacts in this folder are the authoritative definitions:

typeServerProtocol.ts defines the TypeScript interfaces and request types.

tsp.json is the machine-readable protocol model.

tsp.schema.json is the JSON schema for the protocol model.

Version History

TSP uses semantic version strings for client/server compatibility checks. While the protocol is in the 0.x line, minor version changes may be breaking. Patch version changes remain backward compatible within the same minor line.

Connection Model

A client starts a type server and communicates with it over the main JSON-RPC connection using a transport the server supports, such as stdio or Node IPC. When using stdio, stdout must be reserved for JSON-RPC protocol messages.

A type server is expected to receive enough workspace and document state to answer the TSP requests it supports. Clients commonly use normal LSP initialization and document synchronization for this state, then send typeServer/* requests on the same JSON-RPC connection.

TSP also supports optional extra read-only connections. The original connection remains the main connection and owns initialization, workspace state, document synchronization, state-changing notifications, and connection control. Extra connections are opened only after client and server negotiate support during initialization.

Startup Sequence

A typical session follows this order:

The client starts or connects to the type server.

The client and server initialize any shared workspace state, commonly through the LSP initialize and initialized messages.

The client calls typeServer/getSupportedProtocolVersion and verifies that the returned semver string is compatible with the protocol version it expects.

The client calls typeServer/getSnapshot to obtain the first snapshot identifier.

The client sends type-query, import-resolution, or search-path requests that include the current snapshot when required by the request shape.

The server sends typeServer/snapshotChanged when prior type results are no longer valid.

Multi-Connection Mode

Multi-connection mode is an optional performance feature. It lets a client open additional local channels to the same type server for read-only TSP requests, while the main connection continues to own the mutable workspace state.

Clients should use multi-connection mode only when they can benefit from concurrent read-only work, such as high-volume type queries or background workers that should not block the main JSON-RPC connection. A server must still work correctly in single-connection mode. Multi-connection support must not be required for protocol correctness.

Capability Handshake

Multi-connection support is negotiated through the normal LSP initialize request and response. A client that can open extra TSP channels advertises the supported transport kinds under capabilities.experimental.typeServerMultiConnection:

{ "capabilities": { "experimental": { "typeServerMultiConnection": { "supportedTransports": ["ipc"] } } } }

A server that can serve extra TSP channels returns the same capability shape in the initialize result:

{ "capabilities": { "experimental": { "typeServerMultiConnection": { "supportedTransports": ["ipc"] } } } }

Multi-connection mode is enabled only when both sides advertise typeServerMultiConnection and the two supportedTransports lists have at least one transport in common. If either side omits the capability, or if there is no common transport, the session remains single-connection and the client must not send typeServer/connection.

The currently supported built-in transport kind for extra connections is ipc. This negotiation is separate from the main connection, which can use stdio or Node IPC.

Opening An Extra Connection

After initialization and transport negotiation, the client opens an extra channel by sending typeServer/connection on the main connection:

{ "type": "open", "kind": "ipc", "args": ["<ipc-endpoint>"] }

For ipc, the args array identifies the endpoint or endpoints that the server should connect to. The server should validate the request, connect to the supplied endpoint promptly, and return a result with success: true when it accepts the request. If the server cannot open the channel, it should return success: false and include a short message when useful for logs.

IPC Endpoint Arguments

An ipc endpoint is expected to provide a full-duplex JSON-RPC stream when the platform IPC mechanism supports it. In that case, args contains one endpoint string:

{ "type": "open", "kind": "ipc", "args": ["<full-duplex-ipc-endpoint>"] }

If the platform IPC mechanism exposes only one-way endpoints, args contains two endpoint strings. The endpoint names are from the type server's perspective: the first endpoint is the server input stream, and the second endpoint is the server output stream.

{ "type": "open", "kind": "ipc", "args": ["<input-ipc-endpoint>", "<output-ipc-endpoint>"] }

Closing An Extra Connection

The client closes an extra channel by sending typeServer/connection on the main connection with the same transport kind and endpoint identity:

{ "type": "close", "kind": "ipc", "args": ["<ipc-endpoint>"] }

The server should stop serving the matching extra channel and close the transport promptly. A client may also tear down its local transport if the server does not complete the close within the client's timeout budget.

The close request should use the same args shape that opened the channel: one endpoint for a full-duplex IPC stream, or the same input/output endpoint pair for a split IPC stream.

Allowed Traffic

The main connection owns all state-changing traffic. The following must remain main-connection-only:

LSP initialization and lifecycle messages.

Workspace, configuration, file-watcher, and document synchronization notifications.

Server-to-client snapshot notifications.

The typeServer/connection control request.

Extra connections are TSP-only and read-only. They may be used for requests that do not mutate server state and whose results are valid for the snapshot supplied by the client.

The allowed TSP messages on an extra connection are bound to the negotiated TSP version. If a server reports support for a TSP version and advertises multi-connection support for that session, it must support every extra-connection TSP message defined for that version.

The table below lists the TSP version that first allows each message on an extra connection:

Servers should reject LSP traffic, state-changing notifications, typeServer/connection, and any TSP message not allowed for the negotiated TSP version when received on an extra connection.

Snapshots

A snapshot is a non-negative integer that identifies a point-in-time view of the type server's analysis state. Type handles, declarations, symbols, and other returned objects are valid only for the snapshot in which they were returned unless a request explicitly says otherwise.

The type server must advance the snapshot whenever a previously returned type-analysis result may be invalid. Typical invalidating events include opened-file changes, closed files, configuration changes, watched-file changes, dependency changes, or any cache reset that changes analysis results.

Snapshot rules:

typeServer/getSnapshot returns the current snapshot identifier.

Snapshot identifiers must not decrease during a session.

typeServer/snapshotChanged reports the previous and new snapshot identifiers.

Requests that include a stale snapshot should fail with a JSON-RPC error that lets the client retry with a fresh snapshot. LSP's ServerCancelled error code (-32802) is the preferred error for this case.

Positions And Ranges

TSP follows LSP position conventions:

Lines are zero-based.

Character offsets are zero-based UTF-16 code units.

Ranges identify source spans using a start position and end position.

If a type server stores source locations in another encoding, it must convert locations at the protocol boundary. Incorrect conversion can cause the client to request information for the wrong syntax node.

Nodes, Declarations, And Handles

The type-query requests (typeServer/getComputedType, typeServer/getDeclaredType, typeServer/getExpectedType) identify a source construct with an arg that is either a node or a declaration. A node is a URI plus a source range. A declaration describes where and how a symbol is defined and is either a regular declaration, which is backed by a source AST node, or a synthesized declaration, which is created by the type checker and has no source node.

InvalidHandle is -1 and represents an invalid or unavailable handle. Clients and servers should treat it as a sentinel value, not as a valid object identifier.

Type Results

Type results are JSON-serializable tagged objects. Each type includes a discriminator that tells the client how to interpret the rest of the object. The protocol supports built-in types, declared types, functions, classes, unions, modules, type variables, overload sets, synthesized types, and type references.

Every type object carries a numeric kind discriminator, a numeric id, and a bitfield of flags:

Type objects are snapshot-bound, and type IDs are response-local:

A returned type object is valid only for the snapshot used by the request that produced it.

Type IDs are used to connect TypeReference objects to earlier type objects in the same serialized response graph.

Type IDs are not a stable global identity for a snapshot and should not be compared across separate responses.

Type references should be used to avoid duplicating large or recursive type graphs within one response.

Protocol Surface

The current protocol is intentionally small. The exact parameters and result shapes are defined in the protocol artifacts; the table below lists every TSP message with its direction and payload shape.

A type server should implement the messages required by the client it integrates with and return undefined only where the protocol's result type allows it.

Type Queries

The three type-query requests differ by which type they report for the same node or declaration:

typeServer/getComputedType returns the type inferred from code flow. After narrowing a to int, the computed type of b in b = a + 1 is int.

typeServer/getDeclaredType returns the type explicitly written in the source. For def foo(a: int | str), the declared type of a is int | str.

typeServer/getExpectedType returns the type the surrounding context expects. For the call foo(4), the expected type of the argument is int | str.

Error Handling

Use standard JSON-RPC error responses.

Errors should include enough message detail for a client log to identify the failed request, but they should not expose local secrets or implementation-only state.

Compatibility Checklist

A compatible type server should verify these behaviors:

typeServer/getSupportedProtocolVersion returns a semver string supported by the client.

All request and notification payloads serialize and deserialize according to the protocol artifacts.

typeServer/getSnapshot returns a non-negative snapshot and snapshots do not go backward.

Document, configuration, and watched-file changes invalidate snapshots when they can change returned analysis data.

Requests with stale snapshots fail with a retryable cancellation error.

Position and range conversions use LSP zero-based UTF-16 conventions.

Import-resolution requests return document URIs or no result according to the protocol result type.

Type queries (computed, declared, and expected) return well-formed Type objects, or undefined, for the snapshot supplied by the client.

Multi-connection support is advertised only when the server can serve the negotiated extra transport.

Extra connections accept every TSP request allowed for the negotiated TSP version and reject LSP traffic, state-changing notifications, typeServer/connection, and TSP requests not allowed for that version.

Installation

From source

cargo install --locked omnidiff

This command builds OmniDiff from source, with the dependency versions it was tested with. You need a C compiler on PATH and a Rust toolchain, rustc 1.92 or later. The build compiles every tree-sitter grammar from C. The first cargo install takes a few minutes, because of this and the lto = "fat" release profile.

Homebrew

macOS and Linux:

brew install ivankovic/omnidiff/omnidiff

Debian and Ubuntu

sudo install -d -m 0755 /etc/apt/keyrings curl -fsSL https://ivankovic.github.io/omnidiff/apt/omnidiff-archive-keyring.gpg \ | sudo tee /etc/apt/keyrings/omnidiff-archive-keyring.gpg > /dev/null echo "deb [signed-by=/etc/apt/keyrings/omnidiff-archive-keyring.gpg] \ https://ivankovic.github.io/omnidiff/apt stable main" \ | sudo tee /etc/apt/sources.list.d/omnidiff.list > /dev/null sudo apt update && sudo apt install omnidiff

Nix and NixOS

On NixOS, or anywhere with Nix installed:

If flakes are not enabled in your Nix configuration, add --extra-experimental-features 'nix-command flakes' after nix.

Arch and Gentoo

Recipes for Arch and Gentoo live in packaging/: the PKGBUILD builds locally with makepkg -si, and the Gentoo ebuild is ready for an overlay. The two distros are overwhelmed and their repositories open and close to new packages sporadically.

Using OmniDiff

The interactive TUI

omnidiff # open an empty viewer; press o to pick each file omnidiff BEFORE AFTER # open directly into the diff of two files

Press ? in the viewer for the full list of keybindings.

The TUI uses 24-bit color when the terminal advertises it with COLORTERM=truecolor, and the nearest 256 colors otherwise (macOS Terminal.app, or most terminals over ssh, which does not forward COLORTERM). If your terminal supports 24-bit color but does not advertise it, run export COLORTERM=truecolor.

In a browser

For reviewing changes in a browser, see codereview.

Note: codereview is at v0.0.0; use it at your own risk.

Headless / batch mode

omnidiff --headless BEFORE AFTER, or its synonym --batch, prints the diff as plain text, with optional color, instead of opening the TUI. Use this for scripts or CI. Headless mode also starts automatically whenever stdout is not a terminal, for example when piped into less or redirected to a file. Because of this, omnidiff BEFORE AFTER | less works without the flag.

OmniDiff collapses long runs of unchanged lines. It keeps 3 lines of context on each side of a change (override with --context N), the same convention as diff -u. OmniDiff also prefixes each hunk with the nearest enclosing function, class, or struct line, when that line is not otherwise visible. This shows the location of a change deep inside a large file.

Colors are on by default; pass --color never, or set NO_COLOR=1, to disable ANSI colors, for example when you redirect output to a file - --color always forces them even under NO_COLOR.

OmniDiff exits 0 on success and 2 on error. For scripting, pass --exit-code to additionally get 1 when the files differ. This follows the diff(1) convention. --exit-code is opt-in rather than the default for the same reason git diff exits 0 even when files differ: OmniDiff's usual non-interactive callers are version control systems driving it as a display tool, and they read a non-zero exit as "the tool failed" - jj warns on every file, and git difftool with difftool.trustExitCode=true aborts the whole diff. (The 7-argument GIT_EXTERNAL_DIFF form stays at 0 even with --exit-code, since git treats a non-zero exit there as fatal.)

JSON output

omnidiff --mode json BEFORE AFTER prints the diff as one JSON object. Its fields are described in src/tui/json_output.schema.json, a JSON Schema.

Git integration

OmniDiff is a git difftool backend. Run the interactive setup wizard, which asks whether to configure it globally or for the current repository only:

omnidiff git configure

Configure by hand

git config difftool.omnidiff.cmd 'omnidiff "$LOCAL" "$REMOTE"' git difftool --tool=omnidiff

Run git config diff.tool omnidiff to make plain git difftool use OmniDiff by default, without needing --tool. If you do not want git to ask "view diff ... [Y/n]?" before every file, run git config difftool.prompt false.

Usage

git difftool opens the interactive TUI. git diff and git log -p never do. git diff pipes its output through git's pager, and a full-screen TUI cannot draw onto a pipe, so OmniDiff always falls back to plain text there regardless of terminal or GIT_EXTERNAL_DIFF config (see "Headless / batch mode" above). If you want the interactive viewer from git, use git difftool, not git diff. If git difftool still doesn't open interactively over SSH, reconnect with ssh -t — the session needs an allocated pseudo-terminal; tmux panes always have one.

OmniDiff also works directly with git diff and git log -p, through GIT_EXTERNAL_DIFF. This path needs no difftool config:

GIT_EXTERNAL_DIFF=omnidiff git diff

Pictures, archives, fonts and the rest of the supported content get a short report of what changed in them. Any other binary file gets a one-line Binary file <path> differs notice instead of a diff, the same stand-in git and diff(1) print for them. Neither ever blocks the rest of a git diff: an external diff that exits non-zero makes git abandon the entire run, so OmniDiff reports an unshowable file as a successful diff of nothing rather than as a failure.

Jujutsu (jj) integration

jj does not read git's difftool/diff.external settings, even in a colocated repo, so it needs its own configuration. Run the setup wizard:

omnidiff jj configure

Configure by hand

jj config set --user merge-tools.omnidiff.program omnidiff jj config set --user merge-tools.omnidiff.diff-args '["$left","$right"]' jj config set --user merge-tools.omnidiff.diff-invocation-mode file-by-file

That registers jj diff --tool omnidiff. To make it the default for plain jj diff as well:

jj config set --user ui.diff-formatter omnidiff

Use --repo in place of --user to configure the current repository only.

diff-invocation-mode = "file-by-file" is required. jj's default hands a diff tool two directory trees; OmniDiff diffs two files, so without this setting every invocation fails. With it, jj passes one changed file pair at a time, keeping each file's real path and extension, so language detection works exactly as it does under git.

Usage

jj diff runs its formatter under a pager, so OmniDiff renders in its non-interactive text mode there - the same output git diff gets. jj has no equivalent of git difftool's interactive per-file viewer (its terminal-attached hook, ui.diff-editor, is for jj diffedit/jj split, which edit the right-hand side and read it back - not something a read-only viewer should claim to do), so for the full-screen TUI on a jj repo, run omnidiff BEFORE AFTER directly.

Supported content

The language is detected from the file extension. A file with an unknown extension is diffed as plain text, line by line, so nothing is refused.

25 languages are parsed with a tree-sitter grammar and diffed structurally:

Recognised by extension but diffed as plain text, since no grammar is compiled in: Bazel (.bazel), Dart (.dart), Emacs Lisp (.el), Markdown (.md, .markdown), Protocol Buffers (.proto), SQL (.sql).

Files that are not text are recognised by their bytes, never by their names, and diffed by what they hold:

Only the members that changed are opened, so a font with two edited glyphs shows two glyphs. Each change is also named by how much of it a reader would see: invisible (only the bytes differ), imperceptible, artifacts (what compression or resampling leaves behind), edited, redrawn or replaced - for text, whitespace, formatting and rewritten stand in for the second, third and fifth.

The TUI shows pictures as pictures: in the terminal's own graphics where it speaks the kitty, sixel or iTerm2 protocol, and in Unicode half blocks everywhere else. b switches to half blocks and back, and OMNIDIFF_GRAPHICS=halfblocks (or kitty, sixel, iterm2) overrides the detection; inside tmux, the terminal in front of the pane decides. An archive, a font or any other container lists its changed members beside the one selected, g shows every changed glyph or picture at once, and Enter opens a text member in the full diff view.

Anything else binary gets the one-line Binary file <path> differs notice.

Guiding principles

Fast

OmniDiff's goal is:

A median diff in 100ms or less.

A 99th-percentile diff in 1000ms or less.

Both are met. Over the 2,302 fixtures in src/test/data/diffs/, measured 2026-10-31 on a laptop, an Intel Core i7-1370P (6 performance and 8 efficiency cores, 20 threads) with 32 GB RAM: p50 4.2ms, p90 35.1ms, p99 177ms, slowest 482ms. 100ms is the 97.6th percentile — 55 fixtures take longer than that, and none takes longer than a second.

Times belong to the machine that measured them, so each machine keeps its own (see research/data/machines.csv): the Intel Xeon E3-1275 v5 server that measured the research figures takes about 2.5 times as long per fixture. Runtime is reported, never gated: make check-quality prints this distribution against the machine's own committed baseline on every push, and warns when the runtime is more than twice the baseline's.

Other content is timed over pairs sampled from the same corpus, decoding included - unpacking an archive, drawing a font's glyphs or a PDF's pages - measured 2026-10-17 on the same laptop:

Archives miss the 99th-percentile goal: the slowest hold thousands of changed members. PDFs meet both goals, but only 57% of them take under 100ms, since a PDF draws every changed page. make benchmark-content measures this and writes research/data/performance/content_speed/<machine>.csv.

Robust

OmniDiff's goal is to process 100% of all commits.

The full test dataset holds the git commit history of about 7,500 open-source git repositories, as available on the main branch. This list of repositories comes from the Gentoo Linux distribution. Find it in list_of_repositories.csv.

Measured over every modified code file in the most recent 50 commits of each of those repositories - 442,530 readable before/after pairs in 25 languages, diffed with no size cap under a 120-second budget and a 6 GB memory cap per process - 442,322 (99.95%) completed successfully. 96 pairs ran past the budget and 112 past the memory cap; the latter are twenty files, nineteen of them generated or embedded - tree-sitter parser tables, codegen, minified bundles, a PNG as a C array - and one commit of a 40,000-line single-header C++ library. Given 24 GB and 300 s, 11 of the 28 files that first hit the cap complete. The run, its harness and every pair that did not complete are documented in research/data/performance/PROVENANCE.md.

Beyond code, over every file changed in the same window - 2,018,010 changes in 7,352 repositories, pictures, fonts and archives included - 98.8% are diffed as text or by their content (98.2% counting each repository at most 1,000 times, so that a few giant ones do not decide it; 6,481 repositories entirely). What is left is mostly text in legacy encodings, compiled objects, audio, and EOT fonts compressed with MicroType Express. The census is documented in research/data/corpus_stats/PROVENANCE.md.

Accurate

OmniDiff must match a human's own reading of a change, measured against the hand-authored ground-truth mappings in src/test/data/diffs/:

90% of test cases with zero mismatched bytes.

99% of test cases with at most 1% of bytes mismatched.

Neither is met yet. 1,419 of the 2,302 fixtures carry a hand-painted ground truth - every byte of both files labelled with what a human says happened to it - and OmniDiff's own highlighting is compared against it byte by byte, under each of its two highlighting presets (--full, which keeps brackets, separators and leading whitespace, and --minimal, which drops them):

The whole-corpus rate is far below 1% because most of each file is unchanged and nobody gets that wrong; the per-test-case numbers are the ones that count, since a reader meets the mistakes one diff at a time. Most of what is left is not in the matching: rendering the human's own node mapping still disagrees with the painting on 91% of the --full bytes and 91% of the --minimal ones, so the highlighting rules own the gap more than the matcher does.

make update-painting-attribution measures this and writes one row per fixture and preset to research/data/quality/painting_attribution.csv, which the table is counted from; make check-painting-attribution fails if any fixture gets worse.

Other content is judged rather than painted: a human names how much each change is (invisible, imperceptible, artifacts, edited, redrawn or replaced, see supported content) and OmniDiff's verdict is compared with theirs. AI policy

This project uses substantial AI assistance, currently Claude Code. Most commits disclose this with a Co-Authored-By trailer and a link to the session that produced them. This project does not hide that fact. This project does not treat AI assistance as a lesser way to write software.

Contributions are not accepted at this time, to keep the development speed high (see CONTRIBUTING.md). When that changes, AI-assisted contributions will be as welcome as any other, disclosed the same way: whoever submits the work is responsible for understanding it and standing behind it.

For Developers, human or otherwise

See CONTRIBUTING.md for the technology overview, code-quality and testing expectations, project structure, and what CI checks on every push and PR. AGENTS.md has additional AI-agent-specific conventions.
